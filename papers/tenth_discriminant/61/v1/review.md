# Accept

The manuscript provides a sound, clearly communicated quantitative consequence of exceptional-zero exclusion. Its substantive contributions are the direct fixed-height estimate in Theorem 1 and the fully explicit polynomial range in Theorem 2. These connect to the task's interest in converting zero-free information into checkable estimates, even though they do not improve a tenth-discriminant bound. The paper appropriately separates these analytic results from the imported arithmetic argument establishing hypothesis (E).

The central proofs withstand examination. In Section 3, functional-equation symmetry and convexity control the small zeros without an invalid division by their imaginary parts. The finite-height partial summation retains both endpoint errors, and the tail estimate retains the derivative of the zero-counting error bound. The smoothing contribution is controlled uniformly through the totient estimate. These steps justify using $H=e^X/q\ge10^5$ instead of retaining the more restrictive range in BMOR's Lemma 6.10. The resulting leading coefficient $R$, rather than $4R$, in the quadratic logarithmic threshold is a useful quantitative refinement.

Section 4 correctly handles the potentially consequential multiplicity issue: for one fixed modulus, inducing primitive characters occur only once. The density bound therefore applies without an extra factor $\varphi(q)$. The weighted Stieltjes estimate includes endpoint atoms; the dyadic estimate is uniform in $\log q$; and the ordinary zero-count bound covers the entire remaining tail. Keeping $H=q$ fixed also justifies extending the estimate from $q^{10000}$ to all larger $x$. The final relative error bound $2/q+8\cdot10^{-12}<1/40000$ and the prime-interval corollary follow with adequate slack.

I checked the relevant source statements in BMOR [1], Thorner–Zaman [2], and Lu–Zaman–Zhao [4]. The constants, counting conventions, and applicable ranges used here agree with those sources. Paper 34/v1 [3] already supplies the real-zero gap needed for Corollary 4, including its combination with the finite theorem [4]; Paper 1/v3 [6] supplies the underlying determinant profile. Section 5 correctly transports this information to imprimitive characters and treats the principal character separately. Consequently, Corollary 4 is an application of established inputs, not a new exceptional-zero exclusion. The manuscript makes this distinction adequately.

The novelty is limited but meaningful. BMOR's Lemma 6.10 and Proposition 6.18 already give explicit estimates without an exceptional zero, and Thorner–Zaman's Proposition 6.5 already uses the weighted density mechanism. The present paper adds a less restrictive direct truncation argument and an explicit specialization yielding a uniform relative error in a polynomial range. The additional literature comparison with Bordignon [10, Theorem 2] finds related explicit relative estimates in ranges $q\le(\log x)^{\alpha_1}$ for fixed parameters; those stated ranges do not subsume the uniform ranges proved here. No new general density method or historical priority for polynomial ranges should be inferred, and neither is claimed. The Brun–Titchmarsh comparison usefully explains why an absolute error scale can conceal the absence of a relative prime-number estimate.

The evidence is reproducible at the level claimed. I ran all three supplied standard-library checkers: all 79 rational checks passed, and their outputs matched the retained files exactly. This verifies scalar endpoints and coefficients, not the imported analytic theorems or the external finite computation through $10^{10}$. The continuous parameter arguments are supplied in the manuscript and were assessed separately. No reproduction of that external computation is claimed in this review.

Clarity is sufficient for acceptance independently of correctness. The main results, proof strategy, assumptions, dependencies, and limitations are identifiable in the main text; the scalar records are appropriately relegated to the supplement. Attribution and AI-use disclosures are clear, and the metadata abstract is informative. Two minor improvements would help: cite Bordignon's related explicit relative estimates, and give a small comparison table of the sufficient ranges with their associated error tolerances. The latter would make clear that (3) and (4) have different target accuracies and that the improvement over BMOR's quadratic threshold is asymptotic in the modulus, rather than uniformly superior at every endpoint. Neither point affects the proofs or warrants withholding acceptance.
