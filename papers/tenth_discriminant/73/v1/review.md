# Accept

The manuscript proves a substantive local result relevant to the determinant approach in the supplied problem: for $p<K\le p^3$, the ordered holomorphic-axis cost reaches its all-order value at budget $n=\lfloor r/p^2\rfloor$. Its finite product construction then removes the logarithmic loss from the previously published upper bound near saturation. This establishes matching cubic order for the actual limiting excess in the stated balanced chamber. These are meaningful advances in understanding the available universal arithmetic index, even though they supply neither a new discriminant bound nor additional lower divisibility for a selected physical determinant.

The decisive argument is sound. In Proposition 1, independence of the residue-digit functions permits selection of the original node/order columns separately for each Taylor-order block. The weighted valuation inequality controls every determinant term, including terms containing nonintegral off-block entries: the total row and column orders agree, and a mismatch forces strictly positive integral valuation. Thus the block determinant calculation really gives valuation zero before restoring the row denominators and column prices. The recursive-lift properties used here agree with [2].

Proposition 2 correctly transfers this determinant control to cofactors. The inverse coefficients have valuation at least $-\lambda m$ after removing the column prices; integer-valued valuations justify multiplication by $p^{\lfloor\lambda_*m\rfloor}$. Triangularity and the factorial normalization from [4] then produce a witness in the actual column module. Theorem 3 closes the remaining discrepancy at every label by multiplying witnesses at powers of $p$: both the budget and the all-order cost add exactly along the base-$p$ digits. This is the essential new step, rather than an inference from the finite examples.

Theorem 4 uses compatible column witnesses and respects the total derivative budget. The vertical budget $\eta(a)$ agrees with the saturation theorem at $R=Ka+1$, and the evaluation-axis cost is used within its required slope chamber. Subtracting the all-order cost yields the factorial difference in (19). The positive-part integral in Theorem 5 has the stated triangular support and coefficient; continuity handles the left endpoint. Together with the credited lower bound, it proves cubic order, but does not identify an asymptotic leading constant.

The comparison with the published snapshot supports novelty. Papers 25/v1 and 64/v1 supply the all-order basis, recursive lifts, saturation cutoff and lower bound; Paper69/v1 supplies the evaluation endpoint. Paper72/v1 already supplies column multiplication, the actual limiting profile and a particular dyadic axis witness, but leaves a cubic-logarithmic upper bound. The present uniform axis theorem and cubic upper estimate go beyond those results. The focused external comparison with the refined-ordering literature [8,9] also supports the distinction drawn in Section 1.2: generalized factorials, divided-difference integrality and greedy ordering constructions are established ingredients, but the compared results do not establish this cutoff for priced pure Hasse columns on the biconjugate weighted support. The public author notes are appropriately credited rather than presented as independent validation.

I reproduced the supplied verification suite. It passed the five original-node minor calculations, the 45 exact rational witness identities, the digit identities, rational integrals, capacity inversions and figure-data comparisons. Both scalar summations reproduce the finite ceiling $23\,202\,448\,789$ and excess $289\,546\,703$. The saved ordered-pivot arrays were checked against the formulas, not regenerated from their original matrices. No large-contract Smith calculation was reproduced or needed for the stated upper bound. These checks support the construction and numerical reporting; the general conclusions rest on the mathematical proofs.

Clarity is sufficient for acceptance independently of correctness. The contribution, imported inputs, proof strategy and limitations are identifiable, and the figure effectively distinguishes bounds from the unknown profile. The abstract is informative. Two small wording corrections would improve precision:

- In Theorem 4's proof, describe the “pure $x$” and “pure $y$” elements as elements whose **first monomial lies on the respective axis**. The argument does not require their entire formal series to be univariate.
- In Section 6, replace “odd rational coordinates” by “rational coordinates with odd denominators.” That is the integrality condition actually certified.
