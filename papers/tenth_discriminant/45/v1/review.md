# Accept

This is a clear and mathematically substantive computational number-theory paper. It studies the full extended Richaud–Degert (ERD) family of real quadratic fields, rather than only the unit-generated subfamilies $n^2\pm4$ treated by Kopp and Lagarias [4]. The central results are explicit and useful: an unconditional classification of ERD fields with wide class-group exponent at most two through $\Delta=577{,}936{,}542{,}381{,}190$, and, conditional on Paper 1, a global classification of the 298 fields in Table 3. The same method gives a complete conditional census for every class number $h\le100$ (29,179 fields). These results are relevant to the supplied challenge as a real-quadratic transfer of the explicit $L(1,\chi)$ window and reduced-form sieve, while the paper appropriately states that it does not itself solve the imaginary class-number-one problem.

The proof strategy is coherent. Lemma 1 supplies an explicit small regulator for every ERD representation. Proposition 2 combines this with the class-number formula, the stated lower bound from Paper 1, and genus theory to obtain a finite window. Lemma 3 is the key finite obstruction: if a split prime satisfies $4q^4<\Delta$, exponent at most two forces $q^2$ to occur as a first coefficient in one of the two principal wide-class cycles. Theorem A then follows by enumerating all divisor representations in the window, applying this necessary test, and computing the exact wide class group of the survivors. For Theorem D, Lemmas 4 and 5 correctly explain why storing the two partner cycles counts wide classes and why the all-$a$ phase is exhaustive.

I reproduced the interval-checked analytic window endpoints. Recompiling and running the exponent-two sieve over the full stated range reproduced all 2,759 survivor pairs from the supplied stripes. Rerunning the exact survivor computation gave the 298 fields, including the largest discriminant $451605$. The twelve retained C/Python stripe comparisons also agree; I did not rerun that full Python enumeration. For $h\le100$, I reproduced the C and second Python computations through $\Delta\le10^6$ and reran the unpruned check over all 7,198 ERD fields in this range: all 7,106 qualifying fields agreed, with no class-number mismatches. I also checked that the retained exact-check records cover and match all 8,062 listed fields above $10^7$. I did not rerun those larger exact checks or the full $h\le100$ sieve. Together with inspection of the enumeration and pruning code, this provides substantial support for the computational claims.

The novelty claim is credible. Hoque and Kotyada [5] record the 43 class-number-one ERD fields while leaving a possible exception; the paper’s conditional argument removes that exception. Louboutin, Mollin, and Williams [2] provide the older exponent-two list with a GRH-qualified completeness statement, whereas this paper supplies an explicit unconditional finite range and a Paper-1-dependent global conclusion. Kopp and Lagarias [4] cover the narrower unit-generated order families and their tabulated range; the ERD census and the systematic $h\le100$ tables are a meaningful extension rather than a cosmetic variant.

The main qualification is dependence on Paper 1, Theorem 3. Its statement in the supplied v3 does cover both character parities, as required here; this review does not independently reprove that imported theorem. The global conclusions retain that dependency, while the bounded-$\Delta$ classifications do not. The omitted first stripe of the $h\le100$ sieve is explicitly disclosed and regenerable; archiving a compressed copy would make the evidence easier to inspect. Also label the floating-point analytic class-number checks in §4 as numerical corroboration: closeness to an integer is not a rigorous error enclosure, although the central finite proofs use exact form computations and do not depend on those checks.

Two small attribution improvements would help. The work cited as post [7] is now available in the supplied snapshot as rStation Paper 43/v1; cite and compare that published version directly. The absence of a direct comparison with the original tables of [2] is honestly disclosed, and [4, §5.3] supports the account of their conditional completeness. A future table-by-table comparison would clarify the precise overlap and radicand conventions without changing the present proof.

The manuscript itself is well organized around the mathematical argument, defines the distinction between radicand $D$ and field discriminant $\Delta$, states assumptions and ranges, and separates proof from computational records. The abstract is informative and gives the principal numerical results and limits. The tables are long but necessary for the classification; detailed run records are appropriately placed in the supplement. Subject to the stated Paper-1 dependency and the reproducibility qualification above, the scientific claims are supported and the paper merits acceptance.
