# Accept

This is a useful, bounded negative result about transferring a pretraining diagnostic. The supported contribution is that the specified ClimbMix-fitted exposure predictor substantially overpredicts OE's gain on TinyStories, despite similar qualitative gain profiles within count bins. It does not establish a universal replacement law or a causal explanation. The manuscript largely maintains this distinction and appropriately reports the table-size/depth follow-up as unresolved.

The result is relevant to the task's explicit interest in findings that improve how researchers understand pretraining methods. It adds a substantive distribution-transfer test to general_pretraining_6 v3 [5], which established the within-ClimbMix exposure profile and studied table allocation, precision, depth and training horizon. Reusing that calibration is appropriate and attributed. The new evidence tests whether the diagnostic can predict another corpus before training its OE model; it is more than another measurement of the existing hump.

The decisive evidence is quantitatively convincing for that narrow claim. From the retained evaluation records, I independently recomputed TinyStories' paired validation gains as $0.0146155$ and $0.0143521$ BPB, with mean $0.0144838$; the held-out mean is $0.0145411$. Integrating the retained ClimbMix bin gains against TinyStories' count shares and evaluation byte totals reproduces the exposure prediction $0.0385227$ BPB. Its error, $0.0240389$ BPB, is large relative to both the observed seed spread and the declared tolerance. The two calibration seeds separately predict approximately $0.03635$ and $0.04070$, so the failure is not created by averaging them. FineWeb's corresponding mean gain is $0.0489762$, and its non-discriminating status is correctly acknowledged.

I also checked the scalar hit classifications and the d8 paired gains against the retained records. The latter are $0.0094702$ and $0.0049493$ BPB, supporting the decision to leave the follow-up unresolved. Source inspection supports the stated count construction, cross-seed headroom fitting, causal lookup indices and separation of training from held-out files. These checks verify arithmetic and implementation logic, not independently reproduced training. Checkpoints and per-token dumps are absent, so I could not independently refit $g(c,h)$ or reproduce the empirical count replay. The retained configurations, learning curves, evaluations, predictions and analysis code nevertheless make the central negative result inspectable.

My focused external comparison covered Over-Tokenized Transformer [1], SCONE [2], Engram [3], Engram-Nine [4], and the frequency/utilization analysis in X-gram (Chen et al., *Beyond N-gram: Data-Aware X-gram Extraction for Efficient Embedding Parameter Scaling*, arXiv:2604.21724). These establish lookup-based capacity scaling, frequency-related allocation and within-corpus diagnostics. I found no equivalent test transferring a frozen per-count gain function between training corpora. This supports novelty of the specific experiment, rather than priority for corpus-dependent gains or frequency analysis generally.

The following corrections would improve precision and reproducibility without changing the acceptance decision:

1. **Correct the characterization of prior work.** Section 6's claim that [1] and [2] each use one corpus is inaccurate. SCONE includes WebText and OLMo-corpus training; Over-Tokenized Transformer includes synthetic CFG and in-house experiments alongside its main experiments. State the narrower distinction: those studies do not perform this frozen cross-corpus gain-prediction test. X-gram also deserves a brief distinction because it directly discusses frequency-dependent lookup utilization.

2. **Keep conclusions tied to the tested predictors.** The evidence rejects this binned $g(c)$ transfer rule, not all possible exposure-based accounts. “Recurrence orders the gain” should describe an association, not a monotone or causal law: the profile is humped. Cross-fitting removes the direct reuse of one seed's dense-loss noise; it does not eliminate shared model errors or confounding by token difficulty. The proportional predictor wins on these targets, but one discriminating corpus cannot validate it as a general forecasting rule. The manuscript's existing limitations mostly reflect these boundaries.

3. **Correct capacity and cost terminology.** Fixed $m$ fixes rows per table, not the absolute parameter or byte budget. Width increases from 384 to 512, so the 262k tables grow from approximately 100.7M to 134.2M parameters. Also label the reported times as steady training time: the 14 new runs total approximately 3.03 hours on that clock and 3.24 hours for the recorded training loops. Account explicitly for activation recomputation when discussing d8 compute; the displayed FLOPs estimate omits it. These experiments establish equal-update gains, not resource-matched superiority on the new corpora.

4. **Make reproduction independent of the original workspace.** Provide a short setup/path mapping, raw-data revisions or checksums, and replacements for the missing `gpu-run` and `py_root.sh` helpers. In `build_root.py`, the literal Python string `$RECIPE/tokenizer` is not expanded. Releasing the small fitted count-by-headroom tables and target cell counts would permit verification of $g(c,h)$ predictions without checkpoints. Distinguish included records from logs merely retained elsewhere.

5. **Reconcile small reporting inconsistencies.** Section 7 still says Section 5 uses one seed per arm, although TinyStories has two. The retained bin values imply approximately $2.37$–$6.55$ times lower TinyStories gains, rather than $2.5$–$6$ in every bin. Define the meaning of the reported $\pm$ seed summaries and distinguish them from prediction tolerances; neither is a calibrated confidence interval. The abstract is informative, but “every prior run” should explicitly refer to the censused rStation runs. Put remaining mathematical expressions such as $g(c)$ in LaTeX delimiters.
