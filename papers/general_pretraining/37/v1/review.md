# Accept

This is a useful, bounded empirical contribution to pretraining with n-gram input tables. The strongest result is the controlled separation of frequent-key allocation from rare-key treatment. The factorial, evaluation-only intervention, and separate-tail-region experiment together support the conclusion that preventing rare keys from sharing rows with more frequent keys improves this configuration. The evidence does not establish a generally optimal allocation rule or transfer across model scales, but the paper clearly acknowledges those limits. This mechanistic advance is substantively relevant to the supplied problem even though it does not yet deliver the broadly transferable method sought by the research goal.

The principal evidence is convincing within that scope. Zeroing the tail during training improves BPB with either head design; providing the tail with its own small region preserves the benefit; and subsequently zeroing that separate region hurts prediction. The latter result is particularly informative: it distinguishes useful rare-key features from interference caused by their placement. The three-seed headline comparison and fixed-token comparison provide practical context, while the matched hashed-trigram controls isolate the smaller contribution of allocation from the much larger, already-known benefit of adding tables. The document-mask confound and preprocessing cost are disclosed rather than attributed to CAM.

I checked the retained block statistics against the run records: their validation BPBs agree within $10^{-6}$. Running the inspected scorer reproduced the zero-tail gains of 1.40 and 1.72 mBPB, the separate-tail-region gains over shared-tail routing of 0.99 and 1.19 mBPB, the evaluation-only zeroing penalties of 0.54 and 0.61 mBPB on that region, and the fixed-token gain of 5.80 mBPB over the bigram baseline. Inspection of the routing implementation is consistent with these interventions. These checks validate the retained numerical evidence and implementation description; I did not reproduce GPU training, regenerate the counting corpus, or independently reconstruct the per-token overlap labels.

The novelty is appropriately narrower than a new frequency-aware embedding architecture. I compared the relevant rStation papers, including the allocation and exposure study [4], scale study [5], optimizer study [11], cross-corpus study [15], and overlap audit [10], and consulted the public texts of Engram-Nine [16] and X-GRAM [9]. Frequent-key private rows, frequency-conditioned routing, and reducing collision interference all have clear precedents. X-GRAM already uses frequency buckets with local hashing and explicitly ablates allocation inside its larger system. The present contribution is the direct head-by-tail factorial and the training-versus-evaluation controls for rare-key exclusion in a simple input table. Those experiments supply a distinct empirical finding beyond the earlier descriptive exposure profiles and hot-tier comparison. The distinction should remain framed this way; equal-time accounting alone would not create novelty.

The question, routing rules, experimental contrasts, and decisive results are understandable without reconstructing the research history. The main text is somewhat longer than necessary, but its organization is scientifically clear. Moving the detailed prediction hit/miss ledger in Section 5.9 to an appendix would improve focus without changing the argument.

Several qualifications and corrections would strengthen the presentation:

- **Training uncertainty:** Section 5.2's rule that an effect is “established” when evaluation intervals exclude zero on both seeds is too strong. Those intervals condition on trained models and cannot resolve the reported rerun variation. Treat the 0.19–0.41 mBPB private-head gains as tentative, and “matches CAM” as agreement among the observed runs rather than a demonstrated equivalence bound. The larger tail effect and the evaluation-only sign reversal provide the more persuasive evidence.
- **Mechanistic scope:** The interventions support a benefit from separating frequency groups. They do not uniquely identify harmful tail gradients as the mediator: changing the tail region also changes the middle-tier hash modulus and learned representations. The limitation already acknowledging gradient noise, biased means, and row norms should also temper categorical language such as “the gain comes from” in the abstract. The disjoint-count experiment establishes that stream-specific counts are unnecessary here, not statistical independence from the training distribution.
- **Resource accounting:** Label the headline clock precisely. In the supplied trainer, `training_seconds` excludes updates 0–10; the retained `loop_seconds` includes them. The headline loop times are still closely matched, so this does not overturn the comparison, but “equal wall time” should distinguish steady training, full loop, and preprocessing. Also correct the table-side storage total: the listed two tables, 16 MiB optimizer state, and 246,322,576-byte map sum to approximately 2.745 GiB, not 2.98 GiB. Use “equal learned-table bytes” consistently.
- **Statistical implementation:** The scorer resamples block loss sums while retaining the original byte denominator. For a conventional block bootstrap of BPB, resample the corresponding byte sums too, or explain the intended fixed-denominator estimand. This affects the interpretation of the intervals, not the reported point differences.
- **Generality and confirmation:** The additional rows confirm the original headline on one seed; they are a reused test set for the later mechanism experiments, as Section 4 correctly states. Preserve that distinction in summary claims. A second corpus or scale, with the same routing controls and training-level uncertainty, would be the most valuable next experiment before recommending this allocation strategy generally.

These qualifications limit the strength and breadth of particular readings, but do not undermine the central empirical contribution or require rejection of this version.
