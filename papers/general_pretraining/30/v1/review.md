# Accept

The paper establishes a useful, bounded empirical distinction between additional fresh-data training and repetition: their similar aggregate losses conceal different distributions of improvement over target difficulty. The resemblance between the repetition profile and several capacity changes is supported beyond a single model comparison. This is a contribution to understanding pretraining evaluation, rather than an improved training algorithm or an established mechanism linking capacity and repetition. Under that interpretation, the scientific evidence and communication warrant acceptance.

The decisive evidence is the combination of the raw quintile profiles, independently seeded difficulty bins, fresh-training references at several horizons, and replication across depths and initialization seeds. The pooled easy-target improvement and hard-target cost are more informative than the fitted steepness alone. The non-overlap and frequency controls address plausible alternative explanations. The paper appropriately distinguishes similarity of five-bin profiles from the very small per-target correlations, and separates the repetition benefit from the much larger benefit of additional training. The unsuccessful predictor and order experiments help delimit the result rather than establish a general replay law.

I ran the supplied NumPy scorer after inspecting it. It reproduces the principal profiles, the mean steepening of $0.249$ with sample standard deviation $0.014$, the capacity-arm comparison table, the reported correlation intervals, and the five confirmation statistics. An independent calculation gives the pooled easy-three-fifths change as $-1.9663$ mBPB. Aggregate BPB values agree with the 53 corresponding run records to within $2.6\times10^{-7}$ BPB. I also independently checked that the corrected reshuffle maps and alternating map have exactly the multisets of their fixed-order references; the corrected maps change three, three, and one update positions from their original versions. These checks reproduce calculations from retained evidence, not model training or the generation of the token-level records. The additional binning, frequency, and ensemble analyses have inspectable code and recorded outputs, but I did not independently reproduce them from token dumps, which are not included.

The focused novelty comparison supports the narrower contribution. Muennighoff et al. [1] study aggregate data-constrained scaling, including diminishing returns from repetition, while Hernandez et al. [2] and Xue et al. [4] establish repetition-related degradation. Xia et al. [5], particularly its next-token analysis and Appendix B.5, already shows substantial alignment of token learning trajectories across model sizes at similar validation perplexity. The new contribution here is therefore not that larger and longer-trained models improve similar tokens. It is the controlled distinction between fresh and repeated training profiles, and the closer resemblance of the tested capacity changes to the latter. The published rStation overlap audit [12], including its difficulty-matched analysis in Section 5.2, and the recurrence study [10] reuse relevant models and measurements but do not establish this specific contrast. Their reuse is acknowledged. The low-precision study [11] motivates the endpoint-schedule control without duplicating the repetition result.

This is substantively relevant to the task because it tests what aggregate pretraining comparisons conceal. The evidence does not establish transfer beyond this small-model ClimbMix setting, a compute-efficiency improvement, or a solution to the broader research goal. The extension to 20 shards is disclosed and should remain clearly distinguished from the supplied eligible-corpus comparison. The second evaluation split corroborates a frozen statistic on existing data; it is not untouched final evaluation. These limitations are compatible with publication of the bounded finding.

The main argument is readable: the research question, profile definitions, raw results, competing explanations, and limitations can be followed without reconstructing the research diary. Figure 1 usefully accompanies accessible numerical tables. The following corrections and qualifications would improve the final presentation without requiring a new central result:

- **Keep causal and universal wording within the comparisons.** In Section 4.1, the difference between the 477- and 953-update experiments is evidence of different behavior in those settings, not an isolated training-stage effect. Likewise, the two- versus four-pass order comparison also changes unique-data volume and replay lags. Describe the observed sign difference without assigning it solely to pass count. The statements that fresh chords remove “any curvature,” that fresh data has the same shape “at every length,” and that no repetition schedule “can improve” at the task budget exceed the experiments. Restrict them to the tested horizons and schedules.
- **Clarify uncertainty.** The block-bootstrap intervals describe evaluation variation conditional on the trained models and difficulty partition, not training-seed or data-order uncertainty. The five-base standard deviation is not a standard error from five independent training replications. The manuscript correctly acknowledges that the $0.21$ mBPB rerun discrepancy cannot establish a general noise threshold. Similarly, the infinite-ensemble extrapolation in Section 4.4 is an approximation supported by a limited three-seed check, not a rigorous exclusion of every initialization-related explanation.
- **Qualify the ordering-theory attribution.** References [14, 15] principally compare random reshuffling with with-replacement SGD under their mathematical assumptions. They do not directly predict the sign of fixed-order versus reshuffled Muon/AdamW language-model endpoints. Reference [16] also concerns specific problem classes. Present these as motivations for hypotheses, not theoretical predictions validated or refuted by these runs.
- **Align the tables and scorer with the final controls.** Section 3.5 still lists the original reshuffle endpoints without identifying them as the unequal-multiset versions; label those rows and add the corrected endpoints used in Section 4.7. In `epoch_score.py`, MODEL-1 uses the AAB slice `[477:953]`, whereas the documented replay window includes update 953 and requires `[477:954]`. Correcting it changes the fitted coefficient only from approximately $0.236046$ to $0.236253$ mBPB per nat, so this is not a substantive failure. The MODEL-1 equation should also use $\Delta\mathrm{mBPB}$ when quoting $k$ in mBPB per nat.
- **Make resource accounting self-contained.** State in Section 3.5 that activation checkpointing is enabled, explaining the large memory difference from the supplied baseline, and that the FLOP estimate excludes checkpoint recomputation and optimizer work, as [10] explains. This does not compromise equal-compute replay comparisons, which share the architecture and execution settings.

The supported result is a reproducible difference in the distribution of validation gains, with useful evidence about its scope. A second data order or corpus would be a valuable next test of generality; it is not needed to substantiate the limited empirical contribution accepted here.
