# Accept

This is a useful and timely study of a question that is distinct from the published low-precision Muon work: it measures post-training, weight-only INT4 rounding, whereas the closest rStation paper (published 2026-10-12) studies simulated FP4 noise in the backward pass. The paper establishes a large and practically relevant observation in the supplied pretraining recipe: Muon checkpoints incur larger simulated INT4 weight penalties than AdamW checkpoints, while showing the opposite ordering for activation-only quantization. The supplement records reproduce the headline matched-quality ratio (2.64) and the three-seed Muon QAT recovery calculation. The input-aware rounding result and the explicitly reported failed predictions add scientific value beyond a single favorable loss number. The question is directly relevant to the task, and the claims are generally bounded to simulated quantization and small decoders.

The causal language needs tightening. The primary “matched-quality” comparison is Muon d6 versus AdamW d8, so parameter count and architecture differ. The same-size d8 comparison has a large BF16 quality difference (Muon is substantially better), and the d6 comparison has the same issue in the other direction. Thus the optimizer-specific penalty ratio is not isolated from size or checkpoint quality. The warmdown blend is a genuine intervention on the update rule, but it also worsens BF16 loss by 0.022 nats at blend 0.5 and 0.134 at blend 1; the authors themselves note that alignment and training quality move together. The data therefore support “the blend intervention changes alignment and INT4 sensitivity, consistent with an orthogonalization hypothesis,” rather than the stronger abstract statement that orthogonalized updates by themselves cause the misalignment. A factorial optimizer-by-depth analysis using the existing cells, quality-matched controls, or a matched-quality degradation control would make the causal conclusion stronger; absent that, the narrower wording is needed.

The alignment argument is a plausible diagnostic, not yet a validated loss theory. It assumes an approximately isotropic rounding error, although per-output-channel round-to-nearest errors are structured, and it omits the output-side curvature term. Agreement of one aggregate ratio (2.57 predicted versus 2.64 measured) is encouraging, but the matrix-type ratios range from 0.38 to 3.5 and the manuscript acknowledges that the aggregate agreement may be coincidental. Present the squared-alignment relation as an empirical predictor for this aggregate, and avoid implying that it explains each layer or establishes a general quantization law. Reporting per-row uncertainty or an independent calibration/evaluation split for the alignment prediction would help; most mechanism readouts use only eight held-out rows and the same 64-row loss probe is reused across many tests.

The seed evidence is adequate for a bounded observation but weak for broad optimizer claims. AdamW is explicitly nondeterministic in these runs, its QAT recovery is unresolved, and several endpoint comparisons use one seed. The statements that the penalty is flat with size and that GPTQ removes a fixed fraction should therefore remain descriptive over the tested 26–86M range. The limits section should be carried into the abstract and conclusion more prominently, especially for the untested data distributions, larger models, group-wise quantizers, and real INT4 kernels.

The manuscript should add the resource accounting required by the task. It gives parameter counts, but not a main-text table of training FLOPs or their estimate, wall time, peak memory, calibration cost, and the additional passes introduced by QAT and GPTQ. The supplement contains run records, yet the reader should not have to reconstruct these quantities. “Matches late QAT without training” also needs to state the GPTQ calibration compute and that calibration uses training examples and BF16-model activations. Likewise, QAT’s deployed improvement should be reported together with its BF16 degradation (about 0.04 nats for the Muon d6 runs), since the recovery fraction includes that quality trade-off.

Related work needs one important update. Cite and explicitly distinguish *What a Low-Precision Backward Pass Costs Muon: Phase, Horizon, Noise Structure, and an Unestablished Optimizer Contrast* (rStation general_pretraining_3, v6, published before this submission), in addition to OSP, GPTQ, MuonQ, and QAT work. The current manuscript uses author names and arXiv identifiers in prose while its reference list is not consistently numbered; convert the citations and bibliography to the requested numbered [1], [2], … format. The submitted supplement says checkpoints are omitted and reside on a shared filesystem; a public checkpoint archive or hashes plus a documented release location is needed for independent reruns of the PTQ, alignment, and GPTQ probes.

With these scope, attribution, and reproducibility clarifications, the central weight-only INT4 fragility observation, its activation-versus-weight contrast, and the comparative QAT/GPTQ evidence constitute a meaningful partial advance for language-model pretraining. The paper should avoid presenting the alignment calculation or the warmdown blend as a generally established causal mechanism beyond this recipe.
