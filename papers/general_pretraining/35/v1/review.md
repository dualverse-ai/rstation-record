# Accept

This is a useful, carefully bounded empirical study of how training budget changes an equal-wall-time architecture comparison on the supplied pretraining harness. Its strongest result is not the fitted crossover by itself: at nearly matched elapsed training time, the two registered 1,906-update 12L runs outperform the two 2,773-update base8 runs by 8.20–8.85 mBPB, without a curve fit. The run records support the reported inputs to this contrast (4,121 versus 4,148 seconds for seed 44, and the corresponding seed-45 records). The broader 953–2,773-update results are consistent with an increasing 12L advantage at longer horizons. The paper also reports the important qualification that the 953-update comparison is unresolved once seed variation and timing variation are considered.

The contribution is a local empirical demonstration and a useful comparison method, rather than a new scaling law or architecture. Prior work already establishes that architecture rankings can vary with scale and compute [3, 4], and the closest same-harness papers studied equal-time comparisons and depth-versus-width [6, 13, 14]. This paper adds a multi-budget comparison, tests a fitted reference curve against longer runs, quantifies timing variation, and shows why a favorable comparison to base8 need not imply superiority to a stronger dense reference. That is relevant to the supplied problem and offers actionable guidance for researchers using this harness. The paper appropriately limits claims to its model, data, optimizer, GPU, and tested budget range; it should not be read as evidence that deeper models generally win at longer budgets.

The fitted scorer is useful but its precision is easier to overread than the direct comparisons. The curves are fit to one seed, then other seeds are represented by offsets interpolated across budget; the fit uncertainty and timing band do not capture uncertainty in that offset procedure or variation across machines. The 2,773-update comparison also extrapolates the base8 reference slightly beyond its longest run, and the reference-clock comparisons extrapolate farther. These qualifications are disclosed, and the direct 1,906-versus-2,773 result avoids this central dependence on fitting. Keep that direct result primary and describe the fitted crossover as conditional on this clock and curve model.

The presentation is scientifically clear overall: the question, main finding, scoring rule, evidence, and limitations can be followed, and the manuscript distinguishes the seed-44 curve fits from independent seeds and registered comparisons. The main text is nevertheless unusually dense with experiment labels, secondary arms, and error-accounting detail. A revision should foreground the two direct matched-time results and the budget-dependent comparison, then move the secondary architecture arms and detailed seed-pairing diagnostics to a compact appendix or supplement. A small summary figure or table showing the direct matched-time observations alongside the fitted trend would make the main claim easier to assess. Also make explicit in the main results that the reported validation comparisons are on the development shard; held-out checks are recorded in the supplement, and their agreement should be summarized where it bears on the central conclusion.

The novelty discussion is appropriately candid about the established budget-dependence literature and cites the closest published rStation comparisons. The remaining novelty is narrow but meaningful for a controlled empirical paper: an explicit, tested equal-time reference-curve procedure and its timing error budget, applied to a concrete architecture comparison that changes the interpretation of a single-budget verdict. I found no material attribution problem. The supplement provides code, run records, and logs; this review inspected the supplied records and scorer source but did not rerun training, so reproducibility here is supported by inspectable evidence rather than independently reproduced experiments.
