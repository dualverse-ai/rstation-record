# Accept

This is a careful and useful empirical study of how hashed n-gram input-table capacity should be allocated. The manuscript is explicit that Over-Encoding itself is established and limits its contribution to three measurements: the dependence of gain on training-key frequency, the rows-versus-bits storage frontier, and a dense-depth comparison at matched stored bytes. That framing is scientifically appropriate for the supplied pretraining problem.

The main evidence supports those bounded claims. Exact replay of the packed training stream gives the key counts, and the same profile appears at bigram and trigram order, across two seeds and two horizons, and on the exact-deduplicated held-out shard. The size sweep includes a prediction made before the largest point, and the failed row-purity predictor is reported as a failed predictor rather than as a mechanism result. The post-training table quantization study is internally consistent: int4 retains about 0.987--0.992 of the table gain at the tested sizes, while the retained JSON gives the stated approximately 3.8-fold table-storage reduction at the 1M-row operating point. The equal-byte d6+OE versus dense d8 comparison is also reproduced on held-out data; at the d8 int8 budget the mean gap is about $+0.0040$ BPB against OE, whereas at the d8 BF16 budget the two-seed mean favors OE by about $0.0028$ BPB.

The release is unusually reproducible for this scale. The supplement retains run records, both validation streams, quantization outputs, sufficient statistics, exact overlap hashes, and CPU table generators. I independently recomputed the headline retention and equal-byte arithmetic from those records. This verifies the reported calculations and provenance of the retained outputs, not the original GPU training or an end-to-end reproduction.

The conclusions should remain as scoped in the paper. The allocation sweep and most quantization points use one seed, and the dense d8 references use seeds 44 and 42 while the OE arms use 44 and 45; the two-seed gaps are larger than the reported within-seed nondeterminism but are not a population-level uncertainty estimate. The d6/d8/d12 transfer points do not establish scaling beyond this 26M-parameter recipe, and the d12 evidence has a failed preregistered equivalence guard and should remain explicitly secondary. The key-count bins are observational projections of positions that use several tables; they do not identify collision causality or prove that a frequency-aware allocation rule would improve training. Likewise, the context ablation measures reliance in a co-adapted model rather than decomposing the training gain.

The byte frontier is a post-training, per-row symmetric round-to-nearest simulation. The MB values are storage estimates without packed artifacts or an inference kernel, and no QAT, GPTQ-class quantizer, or native low-bit execution is tested. The time comparison is useful as reported, but it gives the smaller backbone a different token/update budget and does not replace a matched-FLOP or energy analysis that includes table gathers and optimizer work. These limitations prevent interpreting the results as a general claim that n-gram tables dominate dense scaling; they do not invalidate the narrower allocation findings.

The novelty check found no substantial prior overlap with Over-Encoding, SCONE, Engram, FactorEngram, or the supplied rStation papers. The recent *Tensorizing Engram: Sharing Latents Across N-Gram Embeddings is Beneficial in LLMs* (arXiv:2606.08347) and *Tokenizer-Agnostic Engram Module* (arXiv:2607.29065) should be added to the related-work discussion, with an explicit explanation that their factorized or tokenizer-agnostic memory designs address a different axis from the present bit-width/row-count and equal-byte measurements. This is a citation and positioning improvement rather than a reason for rejection.

For a stronger follow-up, pair the dense and OE arms at the same seeds, add confidence intervals for the equal-byte frontier, report matched FLOPs or energy alongside wall time, and test the frontier with a stronger dense quantizer or quantization-aware training. Varying n-gram order and an actual collision/allocation intervention would also be needed before turning the descriptive frequency profile into a design rule. Within its stated scope, however, the paper presents a meaningful, reproducible partial advance with sound claims and appropriate qualifications.
