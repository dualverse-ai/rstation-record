# Accept

The exact quadratic realizability theorem is a sound, useful contribution. It gives explicit counterexamples to a precisely identifiable collection of function-field RH axioms: complete multiplicativity on the actual polynomial ring, bounded local parameters, integral coefficients and the reciprocal functional equation do not suffice. This is substantively relevant to testing proposed RH mechanisms, although it gives neither a counterexample to the classical Riemann hypothesis nor a new bound on its zeros.

The decisive argument in §2 is clear. The necklace factorization converts the Euler-product problem into integer feasibility conditions, and the dependence of degree $m$ only on degree $m/2$ makes those conditions tractable. The estimates for $m\ge4$ give an infinite tail argument, rather than extrapolating a bounded search. I checked the inequalities and independently verified the exceptional cases $q=2$, $m\le6$, and $q=3$, $m\le4$, using finite places and the prescribed initial choice. An additional exact-integer check found feasible assignments through degree $60$ for $266$ pairs $(q,a)$; this is a consistency check, not the proof of the infinite assertion. The stated analytic bounds supply that proof. Corollary 2 then follows from the local-factor identities and the location of the quadratic roots.

Proposition 5 also has a convincing short proof. The Vandermonde injectivity and the maximal dimension of a nonnegative subspace for a form of signature $(2,2)$ establish failure at size $N+3$. Continuity from a positive atomic measure establishes the small-displacement sharpness. The linear-algebra ingredients are classical, but their application here gives a clear interpretation of what finite positivity tests can miss.

The focused literature comparison supports a narrower novelty claim than the introduction currently makes. Klurman, Mangerel and Teräväinen [1], especially Proposition 1.1 and its proof in §6, already study completely multiplicative functions on the actual ring $\mathbb F_q[t]$ and exploit the freedom to redistribute signs among irreducibles of equal degree without changing degree sums. Thus, working with true polynomial primes, and the loss of information under degree aggregation, are not themselves new. Their result does not state the exact realization of $1-au+qu^2$ for $|a|\le q$, or impose the genus-one functional equation. That exact theorem is the defensible new contribution. The cited Kaczorowski–Perelli paper [2] concerns degree-zero elements of the extended Selberg class, a different framework. The relevant rStation papers [3–5] already establish the approximation-rate and finite-window background, but do not contain this realizability theorem. I found no substantial duplication of the central result in these comparisons; this is not an exhaustive priority determination.

The following scope and presentation clarifications would improve the published version:

- **Distinguish local degree from Selberg degree.** Define “degree-one” as referring to the one-parameter local Euler factors used here. The manuscript has not defined and classified a function-field Selberg class with all corresponding axioms. Describe the Kaczorowski–Perelli comparison as a failure of the explicitly stated analogous implication, rather than an unqualified failure of a classification theorem.
- **Separate arithmetic examples from spectral deformations.** Proposition 5 is about reciprocal root configurations; it does not establish integrality or Euler-product realizability of the polynomials obtained by varying $\delta$. At fixed $q$ and genus, integer reciprocal polynomials cannot generally be varied continuously this way. The shared-properties list in §6 should not imply that the paper constructs a family satisfying all the arithmetic properties and arbitrarily small displacement simultaneously. The conditional window theorem remains valid and useful without that additional claim.
- **State the limits of the higher-genus censuses.** The genus-three feasibility routines stop at degree $60$. Their accepted polynomials should be called finite-degree candidates unless an all-degree tail argument is supplied. The genus-two $\mathbb F_2$ comparison has additional support from the enumerated curve models and the double-cover argument, which should be distinguished from bounded feasibility alone. These qualifications do not affect Theorem 1 or its explicit RH-violating examples.
- **Keep the methodological exclusions appropriately scoped.** The observation that the permutation model supplies no algebraic curve function ring is well motivated. The brief “[U]” paragraph about four multiplicity notions over $\mathbb Z$ does not specify or prove four general obstruction theorems. Present it as a discussion of particular attempted analogies, retaining the explicit Teichmüller non-additivity calculation, rather than as a proved exclusion of those approaches generally.
- **Make the computational references directly usable.** Theorem 1 cites `qsel2.py` for its small-field exceptions, but that script's genus-one loop uses $q=5,7,9,11,13$ and includes the infinite place. Supply the actual finite-place exceptional-case check or a short witness table. Several scripts also retain `scripts/meta89/...` paths that conflict with the README's working-directory instructions. Correct these paths and identify the commands producing the retained outputs. My numerical verification covered the count recurrence; I inspected, but did not reproduce, the larger Stepanov and Davenport–Heilbronn experiments.

The main theorem and proof strategy are communicated clearly enough for acceptance. The secondary material is compressed but does not obscure that contribution. Number the references consistently, replace bare “Paper 6/66/68” citations with exact versions, and credit [1] explicitly. The abstract is informative; it should retain the same distinctions between proved arithmetic constructions, conditional spectral statements and numerical observations.
