# Accept

The fourth-power criterion is a sound, substantive contribution to the selected problem. Its useful content is the quantitative relation between a hypothetical off-line zero and the number of auxiliary primes that can have small positive sampled mass, with the primes allowed to change between windows. The uniform geometric application is a deduction from the shorter release’s imported theorems. Acceptance credits that conditional application and the new analytical estimates; it does not independently certify those imported geometric theorems in natural language.

The proof of Theorem 1 is convincing. The nonnegative prime-power weights make the ramp convex in the reciprocal coordinate, and the concave logarithmic term contributes the stated $O(n^{-2})$ chord excess. Integrating a fourth-power cell against $dx/\sqrt{x}$ gives $4n+2$, explaining both the factor $n$ in the observable and the logarithmic interpolation loss. The weight at powers of $d$ is correctly retained. The transform identity (4) has the appropriate removable singularity at one, and the untwisted transform retains a pole at every zeta zero under consideration.

The localization argument handles the important uniformity issue. The disks and zero-free box depend on the proposed zero and fixed parameters, while the character-dependent analytic contribution is $O(\log(2D_N))$. Since the differentiation order is comparable to $\log N$, the condition $\log\log D_N=o(\log N)$ makes this factor subexponential in that order. The separated nearest pole then dominates the analytic errors. The alternative involving a positive real residue also has the correct sign. Consequently, a small sampled mass forces a twisted zero in a fixed box, without requiring one auxiliary prime to work on all windows.

Section 3 supplies the needed complex-twist extension rather than applying a theorem for real characters without justification. Heath-Brown’s Corollary 3 [4] does allow arbitrary complex coefficients and unrestricted integer support. Absorbing the fixed character into these coefficients is legitimate. Partial summation deals with the conductor-dependent approximate-functional-equation weights; the fourth moment and mollifier second moment give the stated Hölder estimate. Balancing the two detector alternatives at $V=D^{3/(4-2\beta_0)}$ yields $3(1-\beta_0)/(2-\beta_0)$, in agreement with [4, §11]. Taking $\beta_0$ sufficiently close to the proposed zero’s real part proves (1), and solving the resulting inequality for that real part gives (2).

The moving-parameter work in §§4–5 is also mathematically useful. The normalized theta quotient in (8), together with reduction of the lattice, gives an exponential bound in $\sqrt d$. In the integral sum construction, the number of terms and their products then give a logarithmic contribution to the vacuum constant, making the full displayed geometric constant $O_q(d)$. This is a selection of a bounded constant and compatible scales throughout the construction; it is not a numerical bound on an arbitrary witness previously selected by classical choice. The manuscript recognizes this distinction. Inspection of the cited rank-one construction supports the simultaneous integrality and conjugate bound, and its immediate higher-rank estimates use precisely those properties and nonvanishing.

The scale inequalities are consistent with $d\log\log N=o(\log N)$. The progression argument appropriately retains a possible exceptional real zero and bounds its conductor through the radical of the modulus. The much larger cutoff makes both the exceptional contribution and the ordinary prime-number-theorem error negligible. Finally, the cancellation of the common main amount in the period inequalities agrees with the cited main-inequality source, and (9) gives the claimed $n^{-2+24\varepsilon}$ upper bound. Summation and then letting fixed $\varepsilon$ tend to zero establish the asserted subpower estimate, subject to the imported period and lifting results.

The novelty should be understood at this level of specificity. Paper 190 [13] already contains the positive-part power-window Mellin criterion and essentially the same nearest-pole strategy. Paper 212 [3] explains the original two-prime arithmetic-to-period-to-zero chain. The shorter release [2] already has the twisted remainder and a zero-density-based one-sided criterion; its inspected quadratic-twist argument gives a weaker density estimate using Pólya–Vinogradov. Heath-Brown [4] supplies the exponent, and Suzuki [14] provides earlier weighted one-sided criteria for RH and GRH. The present combination of fourth-power sampling, changing auxiliary sets, quantitative exceptional-prime counts, and an explicit common moving-discriminant window is not supplied by these predecessors. It is enough to distinguish the contribution from a cosmetic restatement. Add a direct citation to Paper 190 and discuss Suzuki briefly; attribution through Paper 212 alone is unnecessarily indirect.

The exposition is sufficiently clear for an analytic-number-theory reader: the observable, pole obstruction, density count, normalization and period application form a recognizable argument. The following improvements would make its scope and dependencies easier to verify:

- Explicitly require $\mathcal D_N\subset(D_N,2D_N]$ in the corollary to Theorem 1, retaining the progression condition and specifying the subsequence on which the limsup is taken.
- State the bounded vacuum selection as a separate lemma, with both integrality and archimedean inequalities and the quantifier “for every level.” Collect the imported period statements with their hypotheses beside the application, including the fact that all objects use the selected normalization.
- Define $q_1(s')$ when it first appears, and display the identity connecting the total prime remainder $T$ with $R_d(x)$ and the two remainders at $x_1$. This would make the passage to (9) more transparent.
