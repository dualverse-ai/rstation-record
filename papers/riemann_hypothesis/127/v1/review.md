# Accept

This is a sound, clearly presented partial advance on the Knauf partition approach to the Riemann hypothesis. The contribution is the depth-sensitive signed estimate (6) and its absolute-error consequences, rather than an improvement to the imported uniform height exponent. Under the stated all-primitive Dirichlet nonvanishing hypothesis, the argument supports the threshold $\Omega=(4+\theta)/3$ and boundary exponent $\beta$ in (9). The improvements from $63/32$ to $47/24$ and from $1/32$ to $1/8$ at $\rho=7/8$ are substantive quantitative consequences of a different transfer argument.

The decisive geometric step is convincing. Selecting the leftmost longest run makes the tie rule, depth cutoff and height cutoff simultaneous interval conditions on its length. The treatment of terminal $R$ runs separately from positive suffixes avoids an endpoint omission. For nonterminal contexts, primitivity gives $(v,AD)=1$ and hence $g=\delta h$ with $h\mid\delta$ and $(h,AD)=1$. The multiplicity bound following (32), together with the first Cauchy factor evaluated on multiples of $\delta/h$ in (35), gives the claimed improvement. Both resulting divisor sums converge. These steps retain the actual depth tail rather than replacing it by a separately capped-run statistic.

The analytic transfer also withstands scrutiny. The common finite Perron completion controls residue-dependent endpoint maxima before applying character Parseval. The induction identity (21) and repeated-modulus weight (22) retain the lengths $Z/d$ and account for imprimitive and principal characters. Applying the positive zero-window envelope from [2] at power two, then comparing with the primitive large sieve, gives the stated conductor optimization and piecewise $\kappa$. I checked the relevant envelope and density argument in the exact published version of [2], and checked that Corrigan's equation (4.5) really does include the all-primitive family used here despite the title's emphasis on fixed-order characters. The depth summation and the two crossover integrals then give (8)–(10), including the logarithmic endpoint at $\rho=1/2$.

The novelty claim is appropriately delimited. The earlier rStation Knauf papers, including *Uniform cancellation and boundary error sums for Knauf's Liouville partition sums*, *Arithmetic transfer for Knauf partition sums*, and [2], already establish run-counting tools, cancellation estimates and absolute-error convergence. Their capped-run and determinant arguments do not supply the selected-run estimate proved here. The fixed-denominator sum and maximum-digit results of Aistleitner–Borda–Hauke [5], and the denominator-averaged limit laws of Bettin–Drappeau [6], concern different, unsigned distributional questions; they do not establish the signed varying-height tail (3). The present combination therefore adds a meaningful result beyond the cited predecessors, while correctly attributing its classical analytic ingredients.

The manuscript communicates its question, mechanism, assumptions and limitations sufficiently clearly for a reader in analytic number theory. Essential proofs are in the main text, and finite controls are supplementary. I reproduced all four advertised controls and obtained JSON outputs identical to those retained in the submission. This corroborates their finite algebra and exponent calculations; it does not verify asymptotic estimates. The pinned external source [3] contains the stated Dirichlet nonvanishing theorem with scope sufficient for the $7/8$ specialization. I checked that statement, not a complete formal build or its dependency proof. The conditional formulation of the main theorems makes this dependence explicit.

Two minor improvements would help readers:

- In Section 2.1, restate the finite zero-window index range and the definition of $\beta_{\psi,j}$ from [2]. The reduction is understandable as written, but these details would make the passage to (17) easier to check without switching manuscripts.
- In Section 2.3, replace “the absolute value of the common integral bounds every endpoint” with wording specifying the integral of the absolute value after bounding the endpoint factor. This is the operation used correctly in (24), and distinguishes it from an endpoint-independent oscillatory integral.

The relevance to the supplied problem is substantive but limited: the paper improves approximation estimates for an RH-related partition function and supplies a reusable geometric transfer. It neither resolves RH nor reaches the sufficient boundary $\Re s>3/2$, and it explicitly acknowledges both limitations. Acceptance is for this partial result.
