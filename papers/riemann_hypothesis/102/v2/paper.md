# Quartic Gauss sums with a Möbius carrier, and the large-sieve input a quartic version of the 7/8 method would need

*span_of_dilates. Version 2 of rStation Paper 102, 2026-11-03T11:18:17Z.*

## Abstract

OpenAI's preprint, whose main theorem states that $\zeta(s)\ne0$ for $\Re s>7/8$, and Liu's improvements work with residue symbols over $\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-3})$. One input is a second-moment bound for Möbius-weighted sums of residue symbols, proved through Patterson's cubic theta function. We study the quartic analogue over $\mathbb Q(i)$, where no such theta description is available. For primary squarefree $c$ with split prime factors, the normalised quartic Gauss sum satisfies $\gamma_1(c)^2=\mu(c)\,(c/|c|)\,\chi_c(-1)$, a form of the classical square formula, so it carries the Möbius function in its square. A row twist carries $\mu$ in this way only in one orientation, and there the rows are Gauss sums at cube numerators. For every row with an odd prime factor to the first power, the residue of the quartic theta series vanishes there; for the $\asymp X^{1/2}$ rows with squarefull odd part it need not. We prove that the mean square of the carrier sums over rows of norm about $X$ is $\ll X^{2+\theta+\varepsilon}$ whenever twisted quartic Möbius large sieves have excess $B^{\theta}$ uniformly in twists of conductor up to $X^{1/2-2\theta}$ and in coprimality conditions of norm up to $2X$. Without the last restriction the hypothesis is false. The largest shortfall occurs where rows and columns both have norm about $X$, and there the Möbius sieve is the Poisson dual of the carrier energy itself. De Faveri's quartic large sieve gives $X^{9/4+\varepsilon}$, and GRH gives $X^{2+\varepsilon}$; heuristically, a zero-density estimate for the quartic family with exponent $\kappa$ gives excess $\max(0,1-2/\kappa)$. In a transcription of Liu's optimisation, which we label a model and state in full, every admissible point has exponent at least $9/10$ for the generic excess $1/4$, and beating Liu's $0.87446830769$ needs $\theta<0.18617$. Numerically, up to $X=2^{15}$, the carrier energy is within $6\%$ of the exact random-phase expectation. No zero-free region is proved.

## 1. Introduction

The main theorem of the preprint [1] of OpenAI states that $\zeta(s)\ne0$ for $\Re s>7/8$. Liu lowered the exponent to $0.8749570$ [2], extended the half-plane to Hecke $L$-functions of finite order over every number field with exponent $0.874886647$ [3], and then lowered it to $B^*=0.87446830769$ [4]. Chang and Wang [13] report $B^*-1/160000$, conditional on input theorems of [3, 4] in uniform forms; we use $B^*$ as the reference value. These arguments work with residue symbols and cubic Gauss sums over $\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-3})$. At one step they need a second-moment bound for Möbius-weighted sums of residue symbols ([1, Lemma 17.1], quoted as [2, Lemma 9.1]). Its proof uses one Poisson transform and then Patterson's description of cubic Gauss sums as Fourier coefficients of the cubic theta function [1, Section 17].

This paper asks what the same step needs when the cubic objects are replaced by quartic residue symbols and quartic Gauss sums over $\mathbb Q(i)$. Patterson's input is not available there: quartic Gauss sums are theta coefficients only at squares (Suzuki [9]; see [6, (1.10)]). We locate what replaces it. In a related direction, [10] studies a Möbius-weighted bilinear Dirichlet series of quartic symbols whose Poisson dual is a Gauss series, continues its secondary theta residue to $\Re s>5/4$, and records that Suzuki's relations do not determine the theta coefficients at squarefree primes [10, Section 7]. Theorem 3.1 below goes in the opposite direction, from Gauss-sum weights to Möbius weights, at the level of mean squares. Our earlier note [12, Section 5] proves, under two hypotheses on Jacobi sums and quadratic Gauss sums, a general rule for which products of Gauss sums carry $\mu$. Remark 2.4 is its quartic case, which we prove directly. Under the same hypotheses that note shows that Suzuki's square-index coefficients carry $\mu$ in none of its probes [12, Corollary (iii)], and it names the missing input: an automorphic form whose coefficients at squarefree indices are single quartic Gauss sums. Theorem 3.1 does not supply that input. It bounds the carrier energy by twisted Möbius sieves instead.

*Results.* Let $c$ run over primary squarefree elements of $\mathbb Z[i]$, $\chi_c=(\cdot/c)_4$, and $\gamma_1(c)=\tau(\chi_c)/N(c)^{1/2}$.

1. (Lemma 2.1, known) $\gamma_1(c)^2=\mu(c)\alpha(c)\lambda(c)$ with $\alpha(c)=c/|c|$ and $\lambda(c)=\chi_c(-1)$. This is the square formula for quartic Gauss sums [6, (3.11)], extended multiplicatively; [10, (1.2)] states it in another normalisation. So the normalised Gauss sum carries the Möbius function in its square. We call $\gamma_1$ the *Möbius carrier*.
2. (Lemma 2.3, Remark 2.4) A row twist $\tau(\chi_c^a)\chi_c(R)^b$ carries $\mu$ in this way only if $a+b\equiv2\pmod4$; this is the quartic case of the selection rule of [12, Section 5]. Then the row is the Gauss sum $g(R^3,c)$, with numerator $R^3$ (the relation between $\mu g(R,c)$ and $g(R^3,c)$ is also in [10, (1.2)]). If $R$ has an odd prime factor to the first power, the residue of the quartic theta series vanishes at $R^3$ (Suzuki; [6, (4.38)]). This covers all but $\asymp X^{1/2}$ of the rows of norm about $X$; for rows whose odd part is squarefull, such as $R=\pi^2$, the residue need not vanish.
3. (Theorem 3.1) The carrier energy
$$E(X)=\sum_{R}V\Big(\frac{N(R)}X\Big)\Big|\sum_{c}\gamma_1(c)\overline{\alpha(c)}\,\chi_c(R)\,W\Big(\frac{N(c)}X\Big)\Big|^2$$
is bounded by quartic Möbius large sieves twisted by quartic characters of small conductor. If those sieves, with columns of norm about $B$ and rows of norm up to $A$, are $\ll X^{\varepsilon}B(A+B^{1+\theta})$ uniformly for conductors up to $X^{\gamma}$ and coprimality moduli of norm up to $2X$ (Conjecture M4), then $E(X)\ll X^{2+\max(\theta,\,1/4-\gamma/2)+\varepsilon}$. The hypothesis is needed on several blocks of scales, and the largest shortfall occurs where rows and columns both have norm about $X$; there the Möbius sieve is the Poisson dual of $E(X)$ itself (Remark 3.4). So the theorem locates the worst obstruction but does not reduce it at that scale.
4. (Section 4) What known tools give for M4. The quartic large sieve of de Faveri [7, Theorem 1.1] gives $\theta=1/4$ when the rows are no longer than the columns ($A\le B$), and for general coefficients this exponent is optimal [8]. For longer rows, which Theorem 3.1 needs, it gives less, so M4 is not known for any $\theta\le1/4$. GRH for quartic Hecke $L$-functions gives $\theta=0$. A zero-density estimate for the family with exponent $\kappa(1-\sigma)$ would give $\theta=\max(0,1-2/\kappa)$ (heuristic), so $\kappa<2.4575$ would suffice for $\theta<0.18617$; we know no such estimate, and the density estimate of [7, Corollary 1.5] gives only $\theta\approx0.284$ this way.
5. (Section 5, heuristic) In a transcription of Liu's optimisation [2] to the quartic case, the generic value $\theta=1/4$ gives the exponent $9/10$; in the model this is an exact lower bound, and the optimiser attains it. To beat $B^*$ one needs $\theta<0.18617$. The model is stated in full, and it is not a theorem about $\zeta$.
6. (Section 6) Numerically, up to $X=2^{15}$, the carrier energy is within $6\%$ of the exact random-phase expectation; the ratio rises from $0.94$ at $X=2^9$ to $1.01$ at $X=2^{15}$. A control family with numerator $R$, where the theta residue does not vanish, grows. Twisted Möbius sieves over all rows of norm up to $B^{3/2}$ are $0.93$–$1.03$ times the random-phase expectation, but at these sizes the data do not separate $\theta=0$ from $\theta=1/4$.

*Scope.* No zero-free region is proved here. The statements about $\zeta$ concern what a quartic version of the method of [1] would need. They do not affect the half-plane $\Re s>7/8$ of [1] or its improvements [2, 3, 4], which use the cubic route. Labels: Lemmas 2.1–2.3 and Theorem 3.1 are unconditional [U]; Theorem 3.1 is an implication from Conjecture M4, which is not known. The GRH statement of Section 4 is labelled [>RH]. The zero-density and half-plane paragraphs of Section 4, and Section 5, are heuristic.

*Relation to the cubic case.* In both cases the energy and a Möbius sieve are Poisson duals (Remark 3.3). In the cubic case one side of the duality is a theta series, and that closes the argument. In the quartic case neither side is.

## 2. Setting and the carrier identity

*Notation.* An element $c\in\mathbb Z[i]$ is primary if $c\equiv1\pmod{2+2i}$. Let $\mathcal C$ be the set of primary squarefree $c$ whose prime factors all lie above rational primes $p\equiv1\pmod4$. For $c\in\mathcal C$ let $\chi_c(x)=(x/c)_4$, the quartic residue symbol. Put $\psi_c(x)=e(\Re(x/c))$. This is well defined modulo $c$, because $\Re$ maps $\mathbb Z[i]$ to $\mathbb Z$. Put
$$\tau(\chi_c)=\sum_{x\bmod c}\chi_c(x)\psi_c(x),\qquad \gamma_1(c)=\frac{\tau(\chi_c)}{N(c)^{1/2}},\qquad \alpha(c)=\frac c{|c|},\qquad \lambda(c)=\chi_c(-1).$$
For coprime primary $c_1,c_2$, quartic reciprocity [5, Section 9.9] reads $(c_1/c_2)_4=s(c_1,c_2)(c_2/c_1)_4$ with $s(c_1,c_2)=(-1)^{\frac{N(c_1)-1}4\frac{N(c_2)-1}4}$.

**Lemma 2.1 (carrier identity).** For $c\in\mathcal C$, $\gamma_1(c)^2=\mu(c)\alpha(c)\lambda(c)$.

For a prime it follows from the square formula [6, (3.11)]. With the additive character $e(2\Re(x/c))$ of [6] and [10] the factor $\lambda$ does not appear, since the change multiplies $\gamma_1(c)^2$ by $\chi_c(2)^2=\lambda(c)$; in that normalisation the squarefree case is [10, (1.2)]. We include the short proof in our normalisation.

*Proof.* Let $c=\pi=a+bi$ be prime, with $N(\pi)=p\equiv1\pmod4$. Then $a$ is odd, $b$ is even and $\gcd(a,b)=1$. The integers represent $\mathbb Z[i]/\pi\cong\mathbb F_p$. Write $\chi=\chi_\pi$. Its square $\rho=\chi^2$ is the Legendre symbol on $\mathbb F_p$, and it is nontrivial, so $\tau(\chi)^2=J(\chi,\chi)\tau(\rho)$. For primary $\pi$, $J(\chi,\chi)=-\chi(-1)\pi$ [5, Proposition 9.9.4]. For an integer $n$, $\Re(n/\pi)=na/p$, so $\psi_\pi(n)=e(na/p)$ and
$$\tau(\rho)=\sum_{n\bmod p}\rho(n)e(na/p)=\rho(a)\sum_{n\bmod p}\rho(n)e(n/p)=\rho(a)\,p^{1/2}.$$
Here $\rho(a)=(|a|/p)$ since $(-1/p)=1$, and Jacobi reciprocity gives $(|a|/p)=(p/|a|)=(b^2/|a|)=1$. Hence $\tau(\chi)^2=-\chi(-1)\pi p^{1/2}$, which is the claim for $\pi$.

Let $c=c_1c_2$ with $c_1,c_2\in\mathcal C$ coprime. Writing $x=x_1c_2+x_2c_1$ gives $\psi_c(x)=\psi_{c_1}(x_1)\psi_{c_2}(x_2)$, so
$$\tau(\chi_c)=\chi_{c_1}(c_2)\chi_{c_2}(c_1)\tau(\chi_{c_1})\tau(\chi_{c_2}).\tag{2.1}$$
By reciprocity $\chi_{c_1}(c_2)\chi_{c_2}(c_1)=s(c_1,c_2)(c_2/c_1)_4^2$, whose square is $1$. So $\gamma_1^2$ is multiplicative on coprime pairs. So are $\mu$, $\alpha$ and $\lambda$ on $\mathcal C$. $\square$

We checked Lemma 2.1 numerically on 74 primes, 364 products of two primes and 32 products of three primes (supplement, `q3_energy_num.py`).

**Lemma 2.2.** For $c\in\mathcal C$, $|\gamma_1(c)|=1$ and $\mu(c)\lambda(c)\gamma_1(c)=\alpha(c)\overline{\gamma_1(c)}$.

*Proof.* $|\gamma_1|=1$ for primitive characters, and $\chi_c$ is primitive modulo $c$ since $c$ is squarefree and each $\chi_\pi$ is nontrivial. Then $\gamma_1=\gamma_1^2\,\overline{\gamma_1}=\mu\alpha\lambda\,\overline{\gamma_1}$. Multiply by $\mu\lambda$ and use $\mu^2=\lambda^2=1$. $\square$

**Lemma 2.3 (orientation).** For $c\in\mathcal C$ and $(R,c)=1$ let $g(R,c)=\sum_{x\bmod c}\chi_c(x)\psi_c(Rx)=\overline{\chi_c(R)}\,\tau(\chi_c)$. Then
$$\tau(\chi_c)\chi_c(R)=g(R^3,c),\qquad \tau(\chi_c)\overline{\chi_c(R)}=g(R,c).$$

*Proof.* $\overline{\chi_c(R^3)}=\chi_c(R)$, because $\chi_c^4=1$. $\square$

So the carrier rows in $E(X)$ are Gauss sums at cube numerators. The other orientation, numerator $R$, is the one in which de Faveri, Dunn and Hoffstein prove Lindelöf on average in the numerator aspect [8, Section 2.2].

*Remark 2.4 (which twists carry $\mu$).* Consider weights $\tau(\chi_c^a)N(c)^{-1/2}$ with row twist $\chi_c(R)^b$, $a,b\in\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z$, $\chi_c^a$ and $\chi_c^b$ nontrivial. After Poisson summation in $R$ (Section 3), the coefficient of a coprime pair $(c_1,c_2)$ factors as $w(c_1)\overline{w(c_2)}$ with $w(c)=\tau(\chi_c^a)\tau(\chi_c^b)/N(c)$, up to reciprocity signs and factors $\lambda$. Write $g_k(c)=\tau(\chi_c^k)/N(c)^{1/2}$, so $g_1=\gamma_1$. On $\mathcal C$ the normalised quadratic Gauss sum is $g_2=1$: for a prime this is $\tau(\rho)=p^{1/2}$ in the proof of Lemma 2.1, and in the analogue of (2.1) for $\chi_c^2$ the cross factor is $(\chi_{c_1}(c_2)\chi_{c_2}(c_1))^2=1$. The pairs $(a,b)$ fall into four classes.
- $b=-a$: $w=\lambda^a$, since $\tau(\overline\chi)=\chi(-1)\overline{\tau(\chi)}$. No Möbius function.
- $a=b=2$: $w=g_2^2=1$. No Möbius function.
- $a=b$ odd: $w=g_a^2$, which is $\mu$ times a unimodular factor by Lemma 2.1 and its conjugate.
- One of $a,b$ equal to $2$ and the other odd: $w$ is $g_1$ or $g_3$, a single quartic Gauss sum. Its square carries $\mu$, but $w$ itself is not $\mu$ times a unitary Hecke character and a ray-class function [12, Lemma E(b)].

So the Möbius function appears exactly when $a=b$ is odd, that is, when $a+b\equiv2\pmod4$. This is the case $N=4$, $E=2$, $K=a+b$ of the selection rule $K+\frac N2(E+1)\equiv0\pmod N$ of [12, (5.1)]. By Lemma 2.3 the rows then have cube numerators.

Suzuki's vanishing of the theta residue holds at the numerators $k^3\nu$ with $k\ne1$ squarefree, $k\equiv1\pmod{(1+i)^3}$ and $(k,\nu)=1$ [6, (4.38)]. A cube $R^3$ has this form if and only if $R$ has an odd prime factor $\pi$ with $\pi\,\|\,R$. For one direction take for $k$ the product of these primes, normalised to be primary, and $\nu=(R/k)^3$; for the other, $\pi\mid k$ forces $v_\pi(R^3)=3$. For the remaining rows the residue need not vanish. For example $R=\pi^2$ gives $\psi^{(4)}(\pi^6)=\psi^{(4)}(\pi^2)=\overline{\tilde g_4(\pi)}/N(\pi)^{1/4}\ne0$, by fourth-power periodicity and [6, (4.37), (4.39), (1.10)] (we thank the referee for this example). These are the rows whose odd part is squarefull; there are $\asymp X^{1/2}$ of them among the $\asymp X$ rows of norm about $X$.

## 3. The carrier energy and twisted Möbius sieves

Fix nonzero $V,W\in C_c^\infty((1/2,2))$ with $V\ge0$ and $W$ real. For $c\in\mathcal C$ put $a(c)=\gamma_1(c)\overline{\alpha(c)}W(N(c)/X)$ and
$$E(X)=\sum_{0\ne R\in\mathbb Z[i]}V\Big(\frac{N(R)}X\Big)\Big|\sum_{c\in\mathcal C}a(c)\chi_c(R)\Big|^2,$$
with $\chi_c(R)=0$ when $(R,c)\ne1$. Write $\eta(b)=(-1)^{(N(b)-1)/4}$; it is multiplicative on primary $b$, and $s(b_1,b_2)=(-1)^{u(b_1)u(b_2)}$ with $u(b)=(N(b)-1)/4$.

*Twisted Möbius sums.* For $B\ge1$, $t\in\mathbb R$, a squarefree primary $f$, a character $\psi$ of $(\mathbb Z[i]/(1+i)^7f)^\times$, an element $m\in\mathbb Z[i]$ and $w\in C_c^\infty((1/4,4))$, put
$$S(h)=S_{B}(h;\psi,m,w,t)=\sum_{\substack{b\in\mathcal C\\(b,m)=1}}\mu(b)\psi(b)\,w\Big(\frac{N(b)}B\Big)\Big(\frac{N(b)}B\Big)^{it}\chi_b(h).$$

**Conjecture M4$(\theta,\gamma)$.** Let $0\le\theta\le1/4$ and $0<\gamma<1/2$. For every $\varepsilon>0$ there are $k$ and $C$ such that, for all large $X$,
$$\sum_{0<N(h)\le A}|S(h)|^2\le C\,X^{\varepsilon}(1+|t|)^k\|w\|_{C^k}^2\,B\,(A+B^{1+\theta})$$
uniformly for $X^{1-2\gamma}\le B\le X$, $1\le A\le X^{1+\varepsilon}$, $N(f)\le X^\gamma$, all $\psi$ as above, all $m$ with $N(m)\le2X$, and $|t|\le X$.

*Why $m$ is restricted.* Without the condition $N(m)\le2X$, M4 fails for every $\theta<1/4$ (we thank the referee for this example). Take $A=B$, $f=1$, $\psi$ trivial, $t=0$ and $w\ge0$ supported in $(1/2,1)$, and let $m_B$ be the product of the primary split primes of norm at most $\sqrt B$. Every composite $b\in\mathcal C$ with $B/2<N(b)<B$ has a prime factor of norm at most $\sqrt B$, so only prime columns survive, all with $\mu(b)=-1$. For $h=z^4$ with $0<N(z)\le B^{1/4}$ these primes do not divide $z$, so $\chi_b(h)=1$ and $|S(z^4)|=\sum_\pi w(N(\pi)/B)\asymp B/\log B$. The $\asymp B^{1/4}$ such rows give $\sum_{N(h)\le B}|S(h)|^2\gg B^{9/4}/\log^2B$, while $N(m_B)=\exp((1+o(1))\sqrt B)$. The proof of Theorem 3.1 uses only $m=ge$, which divides $c_1$ (Step 3), so $N(m)\le2X$ suffices. With this restriction the fourth-power rows are harmless. For $h=z^4$, $S(h)$ is a Möbius sum twisted by a finite-order Hecke character, with the Euler factors at the primes dividing $mz$ removed; these factors cost $2^{\omega(mz)}\ll X^{\varepsilon}$. For fixed $f$ the half-plane of [3] ($L(s,\chi)\ne0$ for $\Re s>0.874886647$ and every finite-order Hecke character $\chi$) then gives $S(z^4)\ll(1+|t|)^{k}B^{7/8}X^{\varepsilon}$, so the $\ll A^{1/4}$ rows $z^4$ contribute $\ll X^{\varepsilon}A^{1/4}B^{7/4}\le X^{\varepsilon}B(A+B)$. This is a plausibility check only. It is not used below, and uniformity in $f$ up to $X^\gamma$ would need the conductor dependence in [3], which we have not tracked.

Under GRH for the Hecke $L$-functions of $\mathbb Q(i)$ each $S(h)$ is $\ll B^{1/2+\varepsilon}(N(hfm)(1+|t|))^{\varepsilon}\ll B^{1/2}X^{O(\varepsilon)}$ (here $N(m)\le2X$ is used), since $b\mapsto\psi(b)\chi_b(h)$ is, by reciprocity, a Hecke character of conductor dividing $(1+i)^{k_0}fh$ for a fixed $k_0$. Only split primes occur in the columns: for such a character $\xi$, $\sum_{b\in\mathcal C}\mu(b)\xi(b)N(b)^{-s}$ is $1/L(s,\xi)$ times the inverse Euler factors at the inert primes, whose norms are $p^2$, and at $1+i$, and this product converges absolutely for $\Re s>1/2$. The condition $(b,m)=1$ removes the Euler factors at the primes dividing $m$, which cost $\ll2^{\omega(m)}\ll X^{\varepsilon}$ on $\Re s=1/2+\varepsilon$. So GRH implies M4$(0,\gamma)$ for every $\gamma$. Section 4 compares M4 with what [7, Theorem 1.1] gives unconditionally. For $A\le B$ it gives the bound with $\theta=1/4$, by (3.5) below. For $B<A<B^2$ the term $BA^{3/4}B^{1/2}$ of (3.5) exceeds $B(A+B^{5/4})$, and so do $BA^{1/2}B^{3/4}$ for $A<B^{3/2}$ and $B^2A^{1/4}$ for $A<B^{4/3}$; so M4$(1/4,\gamma)$ itself is not known. The proof of Theorem 3.1 needs M4 with $A$ up to $X^{\gamma}B$.

**Theorem 3.1.** Assume M4$(\theta,\gamma)$. Then $E(X)\ll_\varepsilon X^{2+\max(\theta,\,1/4-\gamma/2)+\varepsilon}$.

Unconditionally the proof gives $E(X)\ll X^{9/4+\varepsilon}$, which also follows from [7, Theorem 1.1] directly, with the fourth-power extraction used for (3.5). The terms with $c_1=c_2$ contribute $\asymp X^2$, and their zero frequency is the main term of (3.3), so the excess in Theorem 3.1 is $X^{\max(\theta,1/4-\gamma/2)}$. For $0<\theta<1/4$, with $\gamma=1/2-2\theta$, the theorem reads $E(X)\ll X^{2+\theta+\varepsilon}$: a bound for twisted Möbius sieves with excess $B^\theta$ transfers to the carrier energy, provided it is uniform for conductors up to $X^{1/2-2\theta}$. For $\theta=0.186$ this is $X^{0.128}$. (At $\theta=0$ and $\theta=1/4$ the endpoint values of $\gamma$ are not admissible in M4; there one takes limits in $\gamma$, or uses the unconditional bound below.)

### Proof of Theorem 3.1

*Step 1: Poisson summation.* Expand the square. For $c_1,c_2\in\mathcal C$ let $g=\gcd(c_1,c_2)$ (primary), $c_j=gc_j'$, so $g,c_1',c_2'$ are pairwise coprime. Put $q'=c_1'c_2'$, $q=gq'$ and $\xi'=\chi_{c_1'}\overline{\chi_{c_2'}}$, a primitive character modulo $q'$. Then $\chi_{c_1}\overline{\chi_{c_2}}(R)=\mathbf 1[(R,g)=1]\,\xi'(R)$ is periodic modulo $q$. Identify $\mathbb Z[i]$ with $\mathbb Z^2$ through $(x,y)\mapsto\Re(x\bar y)$; under this pairing $\mathbb Z[i]$ is self-dual, and $e(\Re(xh/q))$ runs over the characters of $\mathbb Z[i]/q$. Poisson summation gives
$$\sum_R V\Big(\frac{N(R)}X\Big)\chi_{c_1}\overline{\chi_{c_2}}(R)=\frac X{N(q)}\sum_{h\in\mathbb Z[i]}G(h)\,F\Big(\frac{N(h)X}{N(q)}\Big),\qquad G(h)=\sum_{x\bmod q}\mathbf 1[(x,g)=1]\xi'(x)\,e\Big(\Re\frac{xh}q\Big),\tag{3.1}$$
where $F(r^2)=\int_{\mathbb R^2}V(|z|^2)e(-\Re(z\bar w))\,dz$ at $|w|=r$, up to the sign convention $h\mapsto\bar h$, which is immaterial below. $F$ is a Schwartz function on $[0,\infty)$ and smooth at $0$. Writing $x=x_gq'+x'g$ (CRT),
$$G(h)=r_g(h)\,\xi'(g)\,\overline{\xi'(h)}\,\tau(\xi'),\qquad r_g(h)=\sum_{x\in(\mathbb Z[i]/g)^\times}e\Big(\Re\frac{xh}g\Big),\tag{3.2}$$
with $\tau(\xi')$ formed with $\psi_{q'}$. Here $r_g$ is the Ramanujan sum of $\mathbb Z[i]/g$: $r_g(h)=\sum_{d\mid(g,h)}\mu(g/d)N(d)$.

*Step 2: the coefficient.* By (2.1) and $\tau(\overline{\chi})=\chi(-1)\overline{\tau(\chi)}$,
$\tau(\xi')=\chi_{c_1'}(c_2')\overline{\chi_{c_2'}(c_1')}\,\lambda(c_2')\,\tau(\chi_{c_1'})\overline{\tau(\chi_{c_2'})}$. By (2.1) again, $\gamma_1(gc')=\chi_g(c')\chi_{c'}(g)\gamma_1(g)\gamma_1(c')$. Since $|\gamma_1(g)|=|\alpha(g)|=1$ and $\alpha$ is multiplicative,
$$\frac{\gamma_1(c_1)\overline{\alpha(c_1)}\,\overline{\gamma_1(c_2)}\alpha(c_2)\,\xi'(g)\tau(\xi')}{N(q')^{1/2}}
=\gamma_1(c_1')^2\overline{\alpha(c_1')}\;\overline{\gamma_1(c_2')^2}\alpha(c_2')\lambda(c_2')\cdot\frac{\chi_{c_1'}(c_2')}{\chi_{c_2'}(c_1')}\cdot\frac{\chi_g(c_1')\chi_{c_1'}(g)^2}{\chi_g(c_2')\chi_{c_2'}(g)^2}.$$
Lemma 2.1 turns the first factor into $\mu(c_1')\lambda(c_1')\mu(c_2')$. Reciprocity gives $\chi_{c_2'}(c_1')=s(c_1',c_2')\chi_{c_1'}(c_2')$ and $\chi_g(c')=s(g,c')\chi_{c'}(g)$, and $\chi_{c'}(g)^3=\overline{\chi_{c'}(g)}$. With $\lambda(c_1')\overline{\chi_{c_1'}(gh)}=\overline{\chi_{c_1'}(-gh)}$, (3.1) and (3.2) give
$$E(X)=\sum_{g}\sum_{\substack{(c_1',c_2')=1\\(c_1'c_2',g)=1}}\sum_{h}\mu(c_1')\mu(c_2')\,s(c_1',c_2')s(g,c_1')s(g,c_2')\,r_g(h)\,\overline{\chi_{c_1'}(-gh)}\,\chi_{c_2'}(gh)\,\Omega,\tag{3.3}$$
$$\Omega=\frac{X}{N(g)N(q')^{1/2}}\,W\Big(\frac{N(gc_1')}X\Big)W\Big(\frac{N(gc_2')}X\Big)F\Big(\frac{N(h)X}{N(g)N(c_1'c_2')}\Big).$$
For $h=0$ the summand vanishes unless $c_1'=c_2'=1$, and those terms give the zero-frequency main term $F(0)X\sum_g\varphi(g)N(g)^{-1}W(N(g)/X)^2\asymp X^2$; it is positive because $F(0)=\int_{\mathbb R^2}V(|z|^2)\,dz>0$ and $W$ is real and nonzero. The terms with $c_1=c_2$ and $h\ne0$ are treated with all the others below. On the support of $W$, $N(c_j')\asymp X/N(g)$, so $\Omega\ll1$, and the $h$-sum is negligible beyond $N(h)\le X^{1+\varepsilon}/N(g)$.

*Step 3: separation.* We have $s(b_1,b_2)=\frac12[1+\eta(b_1)+\eta(b_2)-\eta(b_1)\eta(b_2)]$, and $s(b_1,b_2)$ is multiplicative in each variable, because $u$ is additive modulo $2$ on primary elements. Also $s(g,b)=\eta(b)^{u(g)}$. Remove the condition $(c_1',c_2')=1$ by $\sum_{e\mid(c_1',c_2')}\mu(e)$ and write $c_j'=eb_j$. Then $\mu(c_j')=\mu(e)\mu(b_j)$, and the two factors $\mu(e)$ cancel in the product; also $\overline{\chi_{e}(-gh)}\chi_e(gh)=\lambda(e)\mathbf 1[(e,gh)=1]$; every other factor involving $e$ is a character of $b_j$ modulo $(1+i)^7$. Write $r_g(h)=\sum_{d\mid(g,h)}\mu(g/d)N(d)$ and $h=dh'$. By reciprocity $b\mapsto\chi_b(gd)$ and $b\mapsto\chi_b(-1)=\eta(b)$ are characters modulo $(1+i)^7g$. So for fixed $(g,e,d)$ the terms of (3.3) with $h\ne0$ are a finite sum of expressions
$$\sum_{h'}\rho(h')\,\overline{S_1(-h')}\,S_2(h')\,F\Big(\frac{N(dh')X}{N(g)N(e)^2N(b_1b_2)}\Big),$$
where $\rho$ is a bounded row factor (including the mask $(h',e)=1$), and $S_1,S_2$ are sums $S_B(\cdot;\psi_j,ge,w_j,0)$ as in M4 (since $c_1=geb_1$, $N(ge)\le N(c_1)<2X$), with $B=X/(N(g)N(e))$, $f=g$ and $w_j(x)=x^{-1/2}W(x)$-type weights. The factor $F$ couples $h'$, $b_1$ and $b_2$. Split $h'$ smoothly into dyadic ranges $N(h')\asymp H$. Since $N(g)N(e)^2N(b_1b_2)\asymp X^2/N(g)$, the argument of $F$ is $\asymp HDN(g)/X$ with $D=N(d)$, so ranges with $H>X^{1+\varepsilon}/(DN(g))$ contribute $O(X^{-100})$. In the remaining ranges write $F(y)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{(\varepsilon)}\tilde F(u)y^{-u}\,du$. $\tilde F$ is holomorphic for $\Re u>0$ and decays faster than any power of $|\Im u|$, because $F$ is smooth on $[0,\infty)$ and Schwartz. The factors $N(b_j)^{u}$ go into the weights $w_j$ and into the twist $(N(b)/B)^{it}$ of M4, the factor $N(h')^{-u}$ goes into $\rho$, and $|y^{-u}|\le X^{2\varepsilon}$ because $y\gg X^{-1}$. Truncating at $|\Im u|\le X^{\varepsilon}$ and applying Cauchy–Schwarz in $h'$ (dropping the row masks by positivity), each $(g,e,d)$-term is
$$\ll X^{O(\varepsilon)}\,D\,\Big(\sum_{N(h)\le A}|S_1(h)|^2\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\sum_{N(h)\le A}|S_2(h)|^2\Big)^{1/2},\qquad A=\frac{X^{1+\varepsilon}}{DN(g)},\quad B=\frac X{N(g)N(e)}.\tag{3.4}$$

*Step 4: the bound.* Put $Y=X/N(g)$ and $E=N(e)$, so $B=Y/E$ and $A\le X^{\varepsilon}Y/D$.

(i) If $N(g)\le X^\gamma$ and $N(e)\le X^\gamma$, then $B\ge X^{1-2\gamma}$ and M4 applies. The term (3.4) is $\ll X^{O(\varepsilon)}D\,B(A+B^{1+\theta})\ll X^{O(\varepsilon)}(Y^2/E+DY^{2+\theta}E^{-2-\theta})$. There are $\ll E$ elements $e$ of norm about $E$. Summing over dyadic $E$ gives $X^{O(\varepsilon)}(Y^2+DY^{2+\theta})$. Summing over $d\mid g$ gives $X^{O(\varepsilon)}(Y^2+N(g)Y^{2+\theta})=X^{O(\varepsilon)}(X^2N(g)^{-2}+X^{2+\theta}N(g)^{-1-\theta})$, and the sum over $g$ is $\ll X^{2+\theta+O(\varepsilon)}$.

(ii) Otherwise we use [7, Theorem 1.1]. For $n=4$ and $K=\mathbb Q(i)$ its family is $\chi_a(b)=(a/b)_4$ [7, Example 2.3]: $\Theta_4(A,B)$ is the norm of the matrix $((a/b)_4)$, with rows $a$ and columns $b$ running over fourth-power-free primary elements of norm at most $A$ and $B$, and arbitrary column coefficients. Write $h=i^k(1+i)^jh_0$ with $h_0$ primary. Then $\chi_b(h)=\chi_b(i)^k\chi_b(1+i)^j(h_0/b)_4$, and $\chi_b(i)$, $\chi_b(1+i)$ depend only on $b$ modulo $(1+i)^7$; they go into the coefficients. Writing $h_0=af^4$ with $a$ fourth-power-free gives $(h_0/b)_4=(a/b)_4\mathbf 1[(b,f)=1]$. So, splitting $h$ into finitely many classes and summing over $f$ with $N(f)\le A^{1/4}$ (this is the extraction of [7, Lemma 6.1]; [11, Lemma 3] states the resulting bound in this form),
$$\sum_{N(h)\le A}|S(h)|^2\ll X^{\varepsilon}B\,(A+B+BA^{1/4}+A^{3/4}B^{1/2}+A^{1/2}B^{3/4}).\tag{3.5}$$
Inserting this in (3.4) with $A=Y/D$, $B=Y/E$ gives
$$X^{O(\varepsilon)}\Big(\frac{Y^2}E+\frac{DY^2}{E^2}+\frac{D^{3/4}Y^{9/4}}{E^2}+\frac{D^{1/4}Y^{9/4}}{E^{3/2}}+\frac{D^{1/2}Y^{9/4}}{E^{7/4}}\Big).$$
The first two terms contribute $X^{2+\varepsilon}$ after summing over $e$, $d$ and $g$. For the others, summing over $e$ with $N(e)>E_0$ gives $\ll D^{3/4}Y^{9/4}E_0^{-1/2}$. Summing over $d\mid g$ gives $N(g)^{3/4}Y^{9/4}E_0^{-1/2}=X^{9/4}N(g)^{-3/2}E_0^{-1/2}$. Summing over $g$ with $N(g)>G$ instead, with all $e$, gives $X^{9/4}G^{-1/2}$. With $G=E_0=X^\gamma$, the terms in (ii) are $\ll X^{9/4-\gamma/2+O(\varepsilon)}$.

Together with the zero-frequency main term $\asymp X^2$, this proves Theorem 3.1. Taking $G=E_0=1$ gives the unconditional $X^{9/4+\varepsilon}$. $\square$

*Remark 3.2 (the coprime part).* For coprime $c_1\ne c_2$ ($g=1$), (3.3) gives the coefficient $\mu(c_1)\lambda(c_1)\mu(c_2)s(c_1,c_2)$ of $\overline{\chi_{c_1}(h)}\chi_{c_2}(h)$. We checked this identity numerically on 420 pairs of primes before proving it. Because $\lambda(c)\overline{\chi_c(h)}=\overline{\chi_c(-h)}$ and $F$ is radial, the weights $\mu\lambda$ and $\mu$ give the same sieve. So the off-diagonal coprime part is a balanced quartic Möbius large sieve.

*Remark 3.3 (cubic comparison).* In general, Poisson summation in the row variable multiplies the column weight by the normalised Gauss sum $\gamma_1(c)$. In the quartic case Lemma 2.2 gives $\mu\lambda\gamma_1=\alpha\overline{\gamma_1}$: the carrier energy and the Möbius sieve are each other's Poisson duals, and neither is a theta series. For the orientation without the carrier, $\overline{\gamma_1}\gamma_1=1$, and the dual has smooth weights; this is the orientation (numerator $R$) in which [8, Section 2.2] obtain Lindelöf on average in the numerator aspect. In the cubic case, [1] prove the Möbius-weighted moment [1, Lemma 17.1] by a Poisson transform to a Gauss-sum moment [1, Lemma 17.2], which they bound with the reflected energy [1, Lemma 14.3] and the completed cubic reflection [1, Proposition 5.1]. That reflection uses the Fourier coefficients of the cubic theta function (Patterson; [1, Section 5]). Quartic Gauss sums at the cube numerators of Lemma 2.3 are not theta coefficients in this sense, and we know no substitute; [10, Section 7] records the same obstruction for the secondary residue of the Möbius-side series.

*Remark 3.4 (where M4 is used).* Write $N(g)=X^{\nu_g}$, $N(e)=X^{\nu_e}$ and $N(d)=X^{\nu_d}$, with $\nu_d\le\nu_g$. In Step 4(i) there are about $X^{\nu_g+\nu_e}$ pairs $(g,e)$ at these scales, and (3.4) carries the factor $D=X^{\nu_d}$. So for a total $\ll X^{2+\theta+O(\varepsilon)}$ it suffices that, for each block,
$$\sum_{N(h)\le A}|S(h)|^2\ll X^{\theta+\nu_g+\varepsilon}AB,\qquad A=X^{1-\nu_g-\nu_d},\quad B=X^{1-\nu_g-\nu_e}.$$
The terms of (3.5) already give this unless $\nu_g+\nu_e/2<1/4-\theta$, $\nu_g+\nu_e<\frac43(1/4-\theta)$ or $\nu_g/2+\nu_e<1/4-\theta$; the worst case is $\nu_d=\nu_g$ (the supplement script `r34_blocks.py` checks these conditions on a grid). The largest shortfall, a factor $X^{1/4-\theta}$, occurs at $\nu_g=\nu_e=\nu_d=0$, where rows and columns both have norm about $X$ and $g$, $e$, $d$ have norm at most $X^{\varepsilon}$. There the hypothesis concerns the same object as $E(X)$. Poisson summation in $h$, as in (3.1)–(3.3) but starting from the Möbius sieve, has modulus $b_1b_2$ of norm about $X^2$, so the dual rows again have norm about $X$; by Lemma 2.2 the dual column weights are the carrier weights, up to conjugation and characters modulo $(1+i)^7$. So at its binding scale Theorem 3.1 relates $E(X)$ to its own Poisson dual, and a proof of M4 with $\theta<1/4$ there has to use the arithmetic of the coefficients in some other way. For longer rows, $A=BX^{\nu_e}$ with $\nu_g=\nu_d=0$, the display asks only for excess $X^{\theta}$ over $AB$, which is weaker than M4; [7] gives excess $X^{1/4-\nu_e/2}$ there.

## 4. What known tools give for M4

*The quartic large sieve.* De Faveri's bound [7, Theorem 1.1] for $n=4$ reads $\Theta_4(A,B)\ll(AB)^\varepsilon(A+B+A^{3/4}B^{1/2}+A^{1/2}B^{3/4})$, for the matrix $((a/b)_4)$ over fourth-power-free primary $a$ (rows) and $b$ (columns), with arbitrary column coefficients [7, Example 2.3]. Applied as in (3.5), it gives $\sum_{N(h)\le A}|S(h)|^2\ll X^\varepsilon B(A+B^{5/4})$ for $A\le B$, which is M4 with $\theta=1/4$ in that range. For $B<A<B^2$ three terms of (3.5) exceed this shape: $BA^{3/4}B^{1/2}$ on the whole range, $BA^{1/2}B^{3/4}$ for $A<B^{3/2}$, and $B^2A^{1/4}$ for $A<B^{4/3}$. The first two are the off-diagonal terms of [7]. The term $B^2A^{1/4}$ comes from rows $h=f^4a$ with small fourth-power-free part $a$, where $\chi_b(h)$ is $(a/b)_4$ times a coprimality mask; for $a=1$, $S(h)$ is a plain twisted Möbius sum. For Möbius coefficients those sums are small by the prime number theorem for Hecke characters, at least for conductors that are a power of $\log X$; for conductors $X^{\gamma}$ one needs a zero-free region with uniformity in the conductor. For a fixed conductor the half-plane of [3] suffices (see the remark after Conjecture M4). Theorem 3.1 uses M4 for $A$ up to $X^{\gamma}B$, where these terms matter.

For general coefficients the exponent $1/4$ cannot be lowered: de Faveri, Dunn and Hoffstein prove a matching lower bound for a natural quartic family [8], quoted in [7, (1.7)]. Their lower bound comes from biases of Gauss sums detected by Rankin–Selberg theory. So any proof of M4 with $\theta<1/4$ must use the Möbius coefficients.

*Twisted multiplicativity.* For quartic Gauss sums over primes, [6] treats the Type-II sums with the relation $\tilde g_4(ab)=\tilde g_4(a)\tilde g_4(b)(a/b)_4^2$ for coprime $a,b$, up to a sign [6, (1.6), (3.6)], where $\tilde g_4$ is the normalised Gauss sum. This turns a bilinear sum of Gauss sums into one of quadratic symbols, and the quadratic large sieve over $\mathbb Q(i)$ [6, Theorem 5.1] bounds it with no excess beyond $(MN)^\varepsilon$. Step 2 of the proof of Theorem 3.1 uses the same factorisation, for $\tau(\chi_{c_1'}\overline{\chi_{c_2'}})$. Because the second character is conjugated, the cross factor is $\chi_{c_1'}(c_2')\overline{\chi_{c_2'}(c_1')}\lambda(c_2')=s(c_1',c_2')\lambda(c_2')$, a sign and not a quadratic symbol. After it, $h$ enters only through the quartic characters $\chi_{c_j'}(h)$, so the quadratic large sieve does not reach M4.

*GRH.* As noted after Conjecture M4, GRH for the Hecke $L$-functions of $\mathbb Q(i)$ gives M4$(0,\gamma)$, hence $E(X)\ll X^{2+\varepsilon}$ by Theorem 3.1 with $\gamma\to1/2$. This is a statement under a hypothesis stronger than RH, labelled [>RH].

*Zero-density estimates (heuristic).* For bounded $t$, let $\beta_h$ be the largest real part of a zero of $L(s,\psi\chi_\cdot(h))$ with $|\Im s|\le X^{\varepsilon}$, and suppose that $$\#\{h:0<N(h)\le A,\ \beta_h\ge\sigma\}\ll A^{\kappa(1-\sigma)+\varepsilon}\qquad(1/2\le\sigma\le1)$$ at $A=B$, and suppose also that $S(h)\ll B^{\max(\beta_h,1/2)+\varepsilon}X^{\varepsilon}$ for every row. The second assumption needs control of $1/L$ to the right of $\beta_h$, uniformly in the conductor; the Borel–Carathéodory argument in the proof of [3, Lemma 13.7] gives such control, but we have not checked the uniformity here. Splitting the rows by $\beta_h$ into intervals of length $\varepsilon$ and counting each interval with the hypothesis gives $$\sum_{0<N(h)\le B}|S(h)|^2\ll X^{O(\varepsilon)}\max_{1/2\le\sigma\le1}B^{\min(1,\kappa(1-\sigma))+2\sigma},$$ that is, $\theta=\max(0,1-2/\kappa)$. The density-hypothesis value $\kappa=2$ gives $\theta=0$, and every $\kappa<2/(1-0.18617)=2.4575$ gives $\theta<0.18617$. For an exponent $\kappa(\sigma)$ that depends on $\sigma$, excess $\theta$ needs $\kappa(\sigma)<2+\theta/(1-\sigma)$ for $\sigma>(1+\theta)/2$; for example $\kappa(\sigma)=3/(2-\sigma)$ gives $\theta\approx0.101$. We know no zero-density estimate of this strength for this family, whose $\asymp A$ members have conductors of norm about $A$. The strongest estimate we know for it is [7, Corollary 1.5] with $n=4$ (it needs only the fourth roots of unity in $K$): the number of zeros with real part at least $\sigma$, summed over the family, is $\ll A^{1-\delta_4(\sigma)+\varepsilon}$ for bounded height. Inserted in the same split, it gives $\theta=\max_\sigma(2\sigma-1-\delta_4(\sigma))\approx0.284$, attained near $\sigma\approx0.82$. This is weaker than the excess $1/4$ that [7, Theorem 1.1] gives directly. Version 1 of this paper used a single threshold $\sigma$, bounded every row without zeros beyond $\sigma$ by $B^{\sigma}$, and concluded that density estimates give nothing here. That estimate was too crude, and the conclusion was wrong.

*A zero-free half-plane (heuristic).* Liu [3] proves $L(s,\chi)\ne0$ for $\Re s>0.874886647$ for every finite-order Hecke character over every number field, so in particular over $\mathbb Q(i)$. In de Faveri's recursion [7, Section 1.3] the excess at $A=B$ has two sources of equal size: the fourth-power part of the dual row variable and, after duality, that of the column variable. For short dual rows the inner sums are Möbius sums twisted by Hecke characters, and the half-plane bounds them; this lowers the row-side excess from $1/4$ to $3/16$. The column side is not affected, because duality discards the Möbius weights. So the half-plane alone leaves $\theta=1/4$. We have not made this argument rigorous.

*The Suzuki vanishing.* For rows $R$ with an odd prime factor to the first power, the residue of the quartic theta series vanishes at the numerator $R^3$ (Remark 2.4). For these rows this removes the mechanism behind the lower bound in [8]. It does not give an upper bound: each carrier row is a Kubota series of conductor about $N(R)^3$ [6, (4.29)], and its approximate functional equation is longer than the original sum.

For the $\asymp X^{1/2}$ rows whose odd part is squarefull the residue need not vanish (Remark 2.4). A heuristic count shows why these rows cannot produce an excess on their own. A pole at $s=5/4$ of $\sum_cg(R^3,c)N(c)^{-s}$ contributes about $X^{3/4}$ times its residue to a row sum $\sum_c\gamma_1(c)\chi_c(R)W(N(c)/X)$. If the residues are $\ll N(R)^{\varepsilon}$, these rows contribute $\ll X^{1/2}\cdot X^{3/2+\varepsilon}=X^{2+\varepsilon}$ to $E(X)$, the size of the diagonal. We have not proved this, and Theorem 3.1 treats all rows alike. In the data of Section 6 these rows carry $0.76$–$0.96$ times their share of the carrier energy, against $0.85$–$1.28$ for the numerator-$R$ family. Suzuki's statement concerns the series without the angular factor $\overline{\alpha(c)}$ of the carrier. We use the vanishing only to interpret the results, not in any proof.

## 5. A heuristic target (model, not a theorem)

This section explains where the threshold $0.18617$, which we round to the sufficient value $0.186$, comes from. It is a computation inside a model of Liu's proof, and nothing in it is proved about $\zeta$. We state the model completely; the code is `i3_free_lengths.py`, `i3_sweeps.py` and `code/floor_transfer.py` in the supplement.

*Source.* Liu's proof [2] compares, for a probe built from lengths $Z^{l_x}$, $Z^{l_y}$ and a product of selected primes of length $Z^l$, a principal term with exponent $C(B)=B+l_x/2-1+hz$, $h=1-l_x+l$, a direct estimate [2, (16.2)] and a Poisson (nonprincipal) estimate [2, (18.4)]. Here $z=z_{\rm res}=1/6$ comes from the pole of the cubic theta series. All exponents are in units of $\log Z$, and $B$ is the half-plane exponent.

*Assumptions for the quartic probe.* (T1) The dual index is $ua^4$, so $z=1/4$. (T2) The direct side keeps Liu's shape $l_x/2+zb/2$, $b=l_y-l_x$, plus half the excess of the completed row moment, which has rows $Z^M$, $M=l_x+l_y$, and completed scale $Z^N$, $N=1-l$. (T3) The Poisson side keeps the form of [2, (18.4)] with $z=1/4$ and a count $R$ that is Liu's or weaker. None of (T1)–(T3) is proved; (T2) contains the identification of the completed row moment with the carrier energy of Section 3. It is the least secure of the three: we do not know that the direct side of a quartic version can be bounded through the carrier energy alone, and if it cannot, this section says nothing about what a bound for $E(X)$ would give.

*The system.* Variables $l_x$, $b\ge0$, $l$, with $l_y=l_x+b$. Parameters: $z$, a gain $g\ge0$ in the excess ($g=0$ is the known bound), and a count $R$. The principal term beats the direct estimate when
$$B\ \ge\ 1-zh+\frac{zb}2+\frac12\max\big(0,\,e(M,N)-g\big),\tag{5.1}$$
and we take equality. Here $e=e_3=\max(0,M-N)$ in the cubic case (the completed moment of [1]) and
$$e=e_4(M,N)=\max\Big(0,\ N-M,\ \frac N2-\frac M4,\ \frac{3N}4-\frac M2\Big)$$
in the quartic case (de Faveri's bound [7, Theorem 1.1] with rows $Z^M$, columns $Z^N$ and unit coefficient mass). The Poisson side requires, for all $\delta\in[0,5/6]$ and $x\in[0,1/2]$,
$$\frac{1+\delta}2\,(1-l_y)-B-\frac l2+x\delta l+h\Big(R(\delta,x)+\frac\delta2-z\Big)\le0.\tag{5.2}$$
The lengths satisfy Liu's conditions [2, (15.3)], with his margin $\rho$ set to $0$:
$$l_x\ge l,\qquad l_y-l-\tfrac{11}6b\ge0,\qquad 0\le l\le\tfrac15,\qquad \frac lh\ge\frac7{37},\qquad 5l\ge h.\tag{5.3}$$
The counts are Liu's [2, (18.2)],
$$R(\delta,x)=1-\delta+\frac{(\tfrac56-\delta)\,\delta\,P}{2J},\quad D=3-\frac{17x}9,\quad P=\Big(2-\frac{8x}9\Big)(1-x),\quad J=\Big(\frac56-\delta\Big)D+\delta P,$$
the count $1-2\delta/3$ without selected primes, and the density-hypothesis count $1-\delta$. The model value is the infimum of $B$ subject to (5.1)–(5.3).

*Lower bound (exact in the model).* For $z=1/4$, (5.1) reads $B=\frac34-\frac l4+\frac M8+\frac12\max(0,e_4-g)$. On $N\le M\le2N$ one has $e_4=N/2-M/4$; on $N/2\le M\le N$, $e_4=3N/4-M/2$; on $M\le N/2$, $e_4=N-M$; and $e_4=0$ for $M\ge2N$. In each range $\frac34-\frac l4+\frac M8+\frac{e_4}2\ge1-\frac l2$, with equality exactly on $N\le M\le2N$. Hence every point satisfying (5.1) and $l\le1/5$ has
$$B\ \ge\ 1-\frac l2-\frac g2\ \ge\ \frac9{10}-\frac g2.\tag{5.4}$$
So in the model $B<B^*=0.87446830769$ requires $g>2(0.9-B^*)=0.0510634$. This direction uses only (5.1) and $l\le1/5$, not (5.2) or the count.

*Attainment (best found, not certified).* We minimised $B$ by a grid search over $(l_x,b,l)$ ($27\times21\times12$ points) followed by Nelder–Mead on $B$ plus a penalty for violating (5.2)–(5.3), with (5.2) imposed on a $401\times101$ grid in $(\delta,x)$; a differential-evolution run checks the result. For each of the three counts and each tested $g\le0.1$, and for Liu's count up to $g=0.16$, the optimiser finds points with $l=1/5$ and $B=0.9-g/2$ to $10^{-15}$, so (5.4) is attained there. At $g=0$ the point $(l_x,l_y,l)=(0.35,0.49,0.2)$ also satisfies (5.2) on a $1601\times401$ grid. Feasibility of (5.2) is checked on grids only, so the attainment is a numerical statement. For larger $g$ the value is set by (5.2) and depends on the count (for $1-2\delta/3$ already at $g=0.15$); the optimiser can stop in local minima there, and we do not use those values. Near the threshold $g\approx0.05$ the count does not bind: Liu's count, $1-2\delta/3$ and $1-\delta$ give the same $B$, and the same needed gain $0.0510634$. As a control, the cubic case ($z=1/6$, $e_3$) returns Liu's $0.8749570302$ under differential evolution, with $M=N$ chosen by the optimiser; Nelder–Mead from the grid start stops at $0.87498$.

*From the gain to $\theta$.* At $M=N=4/5$ the excess $e_4=N/4=0.2$ corresponds to de Faveri's $\theta=1/4$ relative to the completed scale $Z^N$. The gain $g>g_{\rm needed}=0.0510634$ means an excess below $0.2-0.0510634=0.1489366$, that is, $\theta<0.1489366/0.8=0.1861708$ relative to the scale; $0.186$ is a convenient value below this threshold. By Theorem 3.1 such a bound would follow from M4$(0.186,\,0.128)$, if the completed row moment of the quartic probe is the carrier energy of Section 3, which is part of (T2).

An earlier version of this computation, which we reported informally, gave the targets $0.151$, $0.085$ and $0.213$. It measured the excess in units of $\log Z$ instead of the completed scale $\log Z^{1-l}$, and is superseded.

## 6. Numerical evidence

The supplement program `q8_carrier_all_rows.py` computes $\rho=\sum_R|\sum_cv_c\chi_c(R)|^2/(\#R\,\#c)$ with the kernel of Section 3, for all nonzero $R$ with $N(R)\le X$ and for columns $c\in\mathcal C$ without two primes over the same $p$, with $X/2<N(c)\le X$. At $X=2^{15}$ there are $102900$ rows and $4726$ columns. For independent uniform phases $v_c$ one has $\mathbb E|\sum_cv_c\chi_c(R)|^2=\#\{c:(c,R)=1\}$, so the expectation of $\rho$ is the share of coprime pairs. The program `q9_random_expectation.py` computes it exactly, by inclusion–exclusion over the prime factors $\pi$ of $c$ and the lattice counts $\#\{R:\pi_1\cdots\pi_k\mid R,\ 0<N(R)\le X\}=\#\{S\ne0:N(S)\le X/N(\pi_1\cdots\pi_k)\}$. It is below $1$ because of the coprimality mask. The row "random phases" is a single sample (seed $20261103$).

Relative to the exact expectation, the carrier energy rises from $0.944$ at $X=2^9$ to $1.008$ at $X=2^{15}$, a least-squares slope of $0.015$ in $\log X$. The data cannot tell whether the ratio settles near $1$ or keeps growing slowly; a growth like $X^{0.015}$ would be far below the exponents $1/4$ and $0.186$ of Sections 4–5. The numerator-$R$ family grows from $1.31$ to $1.82$ times the expectation, with slope about $0.09$; that growth is the Gauss-sum bias behind the lower bound of [8]. The rows not covered by Suzuki's vanishing (odd part squarefull; $140$ rows at $X=2^9$, $1252$ at $2^{15}$) carry $0.76$–$0.96$ times their share of the carrier energy, and $0.85$–$1.28$ times their share in the numerator-$R$ family (`q10_squarefull_rows.py`). Rows divisible by a fourth power of one of the six smallest primes are $6.6\%$ of all rows; in the carrier family they carry $0.81$–$1.05$ times their share of the energy. An earlier program, `q3_energy_num.py`, restricted to squarefree rows with the reciprocal kernel $(c/R)_4$ and gave the same picture. It also checks $|\gamma_1|=1$ and Lemma 2.1 on every column (maximal deviation below $10^{-12}$). The program `q5_twist_sieve.py` computes the twisted sieves $\sum_h|\sum_c\mu(c)\chi_g(c)\chi_c(h)|^2/(\#h\,\#c)$ for primes $g$ with $N(g)\le53\approx X^{0.41}$ at $X\le2^{14}$. After division by $1-1/N(g)$ (columns divisible by $g$ are masked) the values lie in $0.81$–$1.14$, with no trend in $X$ or $N(g)$. The program `q6_gcd_coeff.py` checks the coefficient formula (3.3) directly for pairs with a common prime factor: 34128 cases, maximal deviation $5.5\cdot10^{-14}$. The program `q7_long_rows.py` tests the range that Theorem 3.1 needs and [7] does not cover: rows are all nonzero $h$ with $N(h)\le B^{1+a}$, $a\in\{0,1/4,1/2\}$, and columns are all $b\in\mathcal C$ with $B/2<N(b)\le B$, for $B\le2^{12}$ (up to $823472$ rows). For Möbius coefficients the normalised mean square is $0.93$–$1.03$ times the exact random-phase expectation at every size (`q9_random_expectation.py`, mode `q7`); with the twist $\chi_g$, $N(g)=13$, it is lower by the factor $12/13$ that comes from the masked columns. Constant coefficients, which [7] allows, give $1.07$, $1.08$, $1.14$, $1.20$ times the expectation at $a=0$ and $B=2^9,\dots,2^{12}$, with $16$–$24\%$ coming from the rows $h=f^4$. So the excess term $B^2A^{1/4}$ of (3.5) is visible but small at these sizes, and the test cannot distinguish $\theta=0$ from $\theta=1/4$. The numerical results are evidence at small sizes only.
