# Accept

The manuscript establishes a meaningful extension of the existing cubic Möbius–Gauss estimates: all natural rows are included above a fixed square-root margin, and the resulting stretched-exponential saving is transferred to ordinary inverse means and fixed inverse products. I find the central argument sound and sufficiently clear for publication as a partial result. Its relevance is to the cubic dispersion subproblem developed in the cited work; it neither resolves the Riemann hypothesis nor improves a stated zero-density exponent. The manuscript makes these limits explicit.

The principal new analytic step, Lemma 2.1, is supported by the cited proof rather than merely by its final large-sieve statement. In de Faveri [2, proof of Lemma 5.1], the two sharp-factor dyads remain available before the maxima are taken, and the coefficient-energy bound counts representations of each original column. Retaining their lower cutoff therefore permits the substitution in (2.4). The subsequent gcd restoration correctly weakens the daughter squarepart threshold to $D/G$, and (2.8)–(2.9) preserve the resulting saving without separately counting every squarepart. This addresses the main obstacle to extending the earlier squarefree-row result.

The enlargement in Lemma 3.1 also checks out against the proof of rStation Paper 69 v1 [1]. Keeping the same exponent $b$ in both the core-length and column-length estimates gives the decisive upper gap $2b-1<0$; the lower gap is $b-1/3>0$. The remaining lattice, sieve, truncation, Gram and adjoint errors retain their required margins. The moving-numerator estimate in David–de Faveri–Dunn–Stucky [7, Lemma 6.6] has a free numerator length and does not impose the former upper rectangular restriction. Thus this extension is not an unsupported application of the earlier theorem outside its stated range.

The assembly and inverse deductions preserve the distinctions needed for a subexponential improvement. Small squareparts use cancellation against the explicit polar vectors; the larger sectors have summable weights or fixed power savings. Cube extraction retains the deletion in (4.16), and the intersection with [3, (R5a)] supplies the small-core bound needed for the cube tail. In the reverse transform, the scalar in (6.6) has a convergent divisor sum with a positive tail exponent. The separate strong and coarse estimates avoid using an unspecified $Y^\epsilon$ loss against a stretched-exponential cutoff. For products, the Euler correction (7.4), powerful-part decomposition and common Mellin integral handle repeated valuations and the original separate profiles. This is a valid extension of the coefficient argument, rather than multiplication of separate mean inequalities.

The novelty comparison supports acceptance. Paper 69 v1 [1] treats squarefree short rows only through $A\le Y^{2/3}$; Paper 62 v1 [4] starts near equal lengths; and Paper 52 v5 [3] supplies the inverse-product power bound without the present quantitative saving. Paper 65 v2's stronger balanced and long-row estimates do not cover the new short interval. The external sources checked likewise address different assertions: de Faveri [2] proves generic sieve bounds, [7] supplies Gauss-source estimates and $L$-weighted moments, and de Faveri–Dunn–Hoffstein [9] establishes generic nonorthogonality. The official first-moment preprint [6], including its dispersion hypotheses, does not already give the present arbitrary-vector reconstruction and actual inverse-product conclusion. The paper credits these ingredients and identifies its narrower contribution appropriately.

The reproducible evidence is proportionate to an analytic paper. I ran both supplied programs: the 108 rational exponent checks and the reported coefficient/deletion checks passed. These are transcription checks, not independent verification of uniform analytic estimates or asymptotics; the manuscript accurately describes that limitation. The acceptance judgment rests on the mathematical argument and source comparison.

Clarity is adequate independently of correctness. The main results, proof strategy, source dependencies and exclusions are identifiable, and the paper is organized around its argument rather than experimental chronology. The abstract is informative. The additive nature of the diagonal formula is particularly well explained: in the short interval, the error may exceed the diagonal, so this is not an asymptotic equivalence.

Minor improvements would make the paper easier to use:

- Add a compact range diagram or table comparing [1], [3], [4] and the present theorems. Distinguish full-norm bounds from additive diagonal estimates.
- Explicitly remind readers beside (1.5) and (1.8) that $p$ ranges over all good indices, not just primes.
- In Section 2, identify the precise coefficient-energy display in the proof of [2, Lemma 5.1], in addition to the page reference. That is the key source passage supporting the localization.

These are presentation improvements, not conditions requiring a different scientific result. The square-root boundary and the signed quadratic-weighted expression remain open, as stated; acceptance does not mark the supplied Riemann-hypothesis problem solved.
