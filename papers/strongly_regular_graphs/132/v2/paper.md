# Order-eleven symmetry forces at least 44 distinct closed neighborhoods in DSRG(77,13,4,3,2)

## 1. Problem and contribution

The existence of a directed strongly regular graph with parameters $(77,13,4,3,2)$ is open [1,2]. This paper studies one concrete symmetry class: a graph with an automorphism of order $11$.

The main theorem is conditional.

**Theorem 1.** If a DSRG$(77,13,4,3,2)$ has an automorphism of order $11$, then it has at least $44$ distinct closed in-neighborhoods and at least $44$ distinct closed out-neighborhoods.

Here a closed in-neighborhood is a column of $B=A+I$, and a closed out-neighborhood is a row of $B$. The proof first reduces equal-column classes to cyclic weighted quotients. The case of $33$ equal-column classes is then completely enumerated: the three possible class-orbit weight patterns are $(4,2,1)$, $(3,3,1)$ and $(3,2,2)$. Exact rank-one group-ring arithmetic gives no quotient for the first two patterns and four normalized quotient records for the last. An independent finite-field implementation returns the same four records. None of the four has physical binary rows. A known DSRG$(44,10,4,3,2)$ control, generated by the same code, does lift and passes literal checks for both the graph and its complement.

Every order-eleven automorphism is necessarily fixed-point-free, as shown below. The theorem does not exclude this symmetry class and does not settle existence of the target graph. It gives a complete restriction on the $33$-class boundary and illustrates why a quotient solution must be followed by an individual-row lift.

## 2. Related work and distinction

For $t=\lambda+1$, putting $B=A+I$ removes the identity term from the quadratic equation. Bounds on classes of equal closed neighborhoods, including the bound needed here, are developed in Paper 76 [3]. Paper 117 [4] uses cyclic character decompositions and rank-one character matrices for a different parameter line; its $(44,10,4,3,2)$ construction supplies a useful positive control. Paper 119 [5] makes explicit the quotient-versus-row-lift distinction: quotient data alone need not be a graph, while independent local splits can sometimes reconstruct one. Paper 131 [6] studies a conditional saturated-$K_5$ route toward the same target. The present argument has a different hypothesis: it assumes a free order-eleven action and studies all closed-column class patterns with three class orbits.

More directly, Paper 23 [7, Sections 4–5] uses a diagonal sieve followed by rank-one forced completion in cyclic character fields. Its finite computations already separate quotient feasibility from realization. Maksimović and Rukavina [8, Sections 2–3] develop a broader orbit-matrix construction and refinement method for DSRGs, explicitly observing that an orbit matrix need not lift and sometimes refining partial orbit matrices early. These are methodological precedents. Our specific contribution is the complete weighted three-class-orbit census and the physical-row obstruction at the order-77 target.

The current task statement lists existence or a fully exhaustive nonexistence proof as the desired resolution [1]. The result below is therefore a conditional structural advance, rather than a resolution of the open parameter set.

## 3. Matrix and class setup

Let $A$ be the adjacency matrix of a DSRG$(77,13,4,3,2)$ and put $B=A+I$. The DSRG equation gives

$$
B^2=3B+2J,
\qquad B\mathbf 1=14\mathbf 1,
\qquad \mathbf 1^TB=14\mathbf 1^T.
$$

On the all-one space $B$ has eigenvalue $14$. On its complement it satisfies $x^2=3x$. Since $\operatorname{tr}B=77$, the spectrum is

$$
14^1,\qquad 3^{21},\qquad 0^{55}.
$$

Call two vertices column twins when their columns in $B$ are equal. The bound for equal closed-neighborhood classes from [3], applied to $B^T$, gives class size at most $4$. For completeness, let $C$ be such a class. Since $B$ has unit diagonal, $C$ is a mutual clique. For $x\in C$, the diagonal entry $(B^2)_{xx}=5$ first gives $|C|\leq5$. Since every column has degree $14$, there is an external predecessor $u\notin C$ with $B_{ux}=1$. Equality of the columns means that $u$ enters every member of $C$. The paths from $u$ to $x$ through $C\cup\{u\}$ then give $|C|+1\leq(B^2)_{ux}=5$. Hence $|C|\leq4$.

**Semiregularity.** Suppose an order-eleven automorphism fixes a vertex $u$. Its four mutual neighbors are all fixed, because a set of size four cannot contain an orbit of length eleven. If its thirteen out-neighbors contained an eleven-orbit, only two could be fixed, a contradiction. Thus every out-neighbor of a fixed vertex is fixed. Since $\mu=2>0$, any vertex is reachable from $u$ in at most two steps, so every vertex would be fixed. This contradicts the automorphism having order eleven. It therefore acts freely.

Assume now that an automorphism $\sigma$ of order $11$ acts without fixed vertices. It permutes the column-twin classes. Its action on the set of classes is free: an invariant class would contain a vertex orbit of size $11$, contradicting the class-size bound. Thus the classes occur in $c$ orbits of length $11$. If $w_1,\ldots,w_c$ are the class sizes within one representative from each orbit, then

$$
1\leq w_i\leq4,
\qquad \sum_{i=1}^c w_i=7.
$$

The number of distinct column classes is $11c$. We prove that $c\neq1,2,3$.

## 4. Cyclic quotient equations

Let $E$ be the class-incidence matrix and let $F$ record, for each physical row, which column classes it selects. Then $B=FE$. Write $Q=EF$. Since $E$ has full row rank, the equation for $B^2$ is equivalent to

$$
FQ=3F+2J,
$$

with the rectangular all-one matrix on the right. Here $E$ has size $11c$ by $77$, with entry one when a vertex belongs to a class; $F$ has size $77$ by $11c$. Multiplying by $E$ gives the quotient equation. The automorphism commutes with both incidence maps. Label the classes by $(i,s)$ so that it sends $(i,s)$ to $(i,s+1)$. Write $Q_{ij}(z)$ for the polynomial whose coefficient of $z^s$ is $Q_{(i,0),(j,s)}$. Matrix multiplication is then cyclic convolution in $\mathbb Z[z]/(z^{11}-1)$. Put $G=1+z+\cdots+z^{10}$. The equations become

$$
Q(z)^2=3Q(z)+2G\,\mathbf w\mathbf 1^T,
\tag{1}
$$

where every coefficient of $Q_{ij}$ lies in $[0,w_i]$ and $\mathbf w=(w_1,\ldots,w_c)^T$. The zero-shift diagonal coefficient is $[z^0]Q_{ii}=w_i$. At $z=1$, with $T=Q(1)$, we have

$$
T^2=3T+22\mathbf w\mathbf1^T,
\quad T\mathbf w=14\mathbf w,
\quad \mathbf1^TT=14\mathbf1^T,
\quad \operatorname{tr}T=17.
\tag{2}
$$

The principal character space of the original action has dimension seven, one coordinate for each vertex orbit. Let $r_0$ be the multiplicity of eigenvalue $3$ on that space; the Perron eigenvalue $14$ leaves $0\leq r_0\leq6$. Galois conjugacy makes the multiplicity $r$ of eigenvalue $3$ equal on all ten nonprincipal character spaces. Their spectra lie in $\{0,3\}$, so $r$ is also their rank. Counting the total multiplicity gives $r_0+10r=21$, hence $(r,r_0)=(2,1)$. The principal trace is therefore $14+3r_0=17$, establishing the last identity in (2). The nonzero spectra of the character matrices $FE$ and $EF$ agree, so $Q(\zeta)$ has rank $2$ for every nontrivial eleventh root $\zeta$.

For $c=1$, a $1$-by-$1$ character quotient cannot have rank $2$. For $c=2$, the principal quotient has eigenvalues $14$ and $3$, with right Perron vector $\mathbf w$ and left Perron vector $\mathbf1^T$. Thus (2) forces

$$
T=3I+\frac{11}{7}\mathbf w\mathbf1^T.
$$

The only possible weights are $(4,3)$ up to order, and this matrix is not integral. Therefore $c\geq3$.

## 5. The complete $c=3$ quotient census

The three weight patterns for $c=3$ are $(4,2,1)$, $(3,3,1)$ and $(3,2,2)$. We describe the finite enumeration because it is the part of the result that must be complete.

For a nontrivial character put

$$
N(z)=3I-Q(z).
$$

Equation (1) gives $N(\zeta)^2=3N(\zeta)$, and the rank statement gives $\operatorname{rank}N(\zeta)=1$. Also $\operatorname{tr}Q(\zeta)=6$. Since $\operatorname{tr}Q(1)=17$, the trace polynomial is

$$
\operatorname{tr}Q(z)=6+G.
\tag{3}
$$

Consequently, for each nonzero shift $s\in\mathbb Z_{11}$, exactly one diagonal block has coefficient $Q_{ii}(s)=1$ and the other two have coefficient zero. The ten nonzero shifts therefore form three labelled diagonal sets $D_i$. Their sizes are $T_{ii}-w_i$.

First, every integer matrix $T$ satisfying (2) and the bounds $0\leq T_{ij}\leq\min(14,11w_i)$ and $T_{ii}\geq w_i$ is enumerated. The initial enumeration selected rows. The independent column-first implementation chooses the first two columns from all bounded nonnegative vectors of sum $14$; the weighted row condition determines the last column. It then checks the remaining column sum, diagonal bounds, trace and every entry of the square identity. Both methods give the same lists. The counts are:

| weights $\mathbf w$ | augmentation matrices | rank-one quotient records | physical lift |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| $(4,2,1)$ | 5 | 0 | none |
| $(3,3,1)$ | 6 | 0 | none |
| $(3,2,2)$ | 10 | 4 | none |

For each $T$, all partitions of the ten nonzero shifts into the required $D_i$ are considered. The $2\times2$ minors of $N(\zeta)$ give a strong exact filter. In group-ring form, for $i\neq j$,

$$
Q_{ij}Q_{ji}=N_{ii}N_{jj}-c_{ij}G,
\quad
c_{ij}=\frac{(3-T_{ii})(3-T_{jj})-T_{ij}T_{ji}}{11}.
\tag{4}
$$

Every coefficient in (4) must lie between zero and $\min(w_iT_{ji},w_jT_{ij})$, since a convolution coefficient is bounded by either factor's coefficient bound times the other's augmentation. This is a necessary filter, not an extra hypothesis.

Each of the $21$ augmentation matrices has an off-diagonal entry of total weight one or two. Choose the smallest triple $(T_{ab},a,b)$ among these entries as an anchor. An independent translation of orbit $b$ puts a single monomial at zero. For a sum of two distinct monomials, translation followed by multiplication of all shifts by the inverse of their difference normalizes it to $1+z$; a double monomial becomes $2$, allowed only when $w_a\geq2$. Thus the exhaustive anchor choices are $h=1$, $h=1+z$, and $h=2$ as appropriate. Translation leaves all diagonals unchanged, and multiplication just permutes the set of diagonal partitions already enumerated. If $g$ is the third index, write $x=Q_{ag}$ and $y=Q_{gb}$. The rank-one minors give, modulo $G$,

$$
xy=-hN_{gg},
\quad Q_{ba}=\frac{N_{bb}N_{aa}}{h},
\quad Q_{bg}=-\frac{N_{bb}x}{h},
\quad Q_{ga}=-\frac{yN_{aa}}{h}.
\tag{5}
$$

The smaller of the two bounded domains for $x$ and $y$ is enumerated. Translation of the third orbit leaves the anchor and diagonals unchanged and rotates the chosen block, so only its lexicographically smallest rotation is needed. In every augmentation in this census the chosen block has total not divisible by $11$, so it cannot vanish in $\mathbb Q[z]/(\Phi_{11})$. Division in (5) determines the other block and then all entries. A field value together with the required augmentation determines a unique rational representative of length $11$, because the kernel of evaluation at an eleventh root is the one-dimensional span of $G$. The representative is kept only if every coefficient is an integer in the allowed range. Every candidate is then checked against the full integer identity (1). The chosen domain's nonzero condition is also checked in the reproduction.

This is an exhaustive calculation, rather than a search with a time limit. A separate implementation repeats the division in $\mathbb F_7[z]/(\Phi_{11})$. The polynomial $\Phi_{11}$ is irreducible over $\mathbb F_7$ because $7$ has order $10$ modulo $11$. A coefficient array maps to zero only if all eleven coefficients agree modulo $7$; for coefficients in $[0,4]$ they would then be equal as integers. Thus the chosen denominators are still nonzero. If two allowed arrays have the same field value and augmentation, their difference is constant modulo $7$, and its augmentation is $11$ times that constant. As $11$ is invertible modulo $7$, the constant is zero. Their coefficients are in $[0,4]$, so the arrays are equal. This proves unique recovery in the finite-field implementation. The full integer square check removes modular false positives. Its records agree entry by entry with the rational records.

The four $(3,2,2)$ records are normalized records, not a claim that there are four isomorphism classes. Two occur at one augmentation matrix and two at another.

## 6. Physical row lifting

A quotient record is not yet a graph. A physical row in class $a$ is a binary vector $f$ over all cyclic quotient positions. It must satisfy

$$
 f_{a,0}=1,
\qquad
\sum_{b,s}w_b f_{b,s}=14,
\qquad
fQ=3f+2\mathbf1^T.
\tag{6}
$$

The $w_a$ physical rows in class $a$ must also sum coefficientwise to the corresponding row of $Q$. These are sufficient conditions as well: pick representatives of the $w_a$ vertex orbits through that class and translate their rows around the eleven classes. Then $EF=Q$, $FQ=3F+2J$, and $B=FE$ has the required quadratic identity and row degree. Its column degree is $14$ by the column sums of $T$. The unit diagonal is imposed in (6). The choices for different class orbits are independent once $Q$ is fixed; additional equal columns would be harmless in this sufficiency statement.

The standard-library row checker enumerates every binary vector in the finite domain forced by $Q$: zero entries are excluded, entries equal to $w_a$ are forced, and the remaining entries are branched exhaustively subject to the weighted degree. It then tests (6) and every multiset decomposition into $w_a$ rows. In coordinates, the equation tested is $\sum_{k,s}f_{k,s}Q_{kb}(d-s)=3f_{b,d}+2$, with all shifts taken modulo eleven.

For each of the four $(3,2,2)$ quotient records, a class of size two has $122$ weighted-degree candidates and zero valid rows. The size-three class has $1497$ candidates and two valid rows, but those cannot be decomposed into three rows. Thus no $33$-class quotient lifts to a binary graph.

As a positive control, the same code handles the known DSRG$(44,10,4,3,2)$ with cyclic class weights $(3,1)$ and degree $11$. Its augmentation matrix is

$$
\begin{pmatrix}9&6\\2&5\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The quotient census returns one record. The size-three class has $90$ weighted-degree candidates, three valid rows and an exact decomposition; the singleton class has one row. Expanding the rows gives a $44$-vertex matrix $B$. The independent verifier checks all $2\cdot44^2=3872$ graph and complement entries, obtaining parameters $(44,10,4,3,2)$ and $(44,33,27,24,27)$.

## 7. Proof of Theorem 1 and limitations

If there were at most three class orbits, the preceding sections would give a contradiction: $c=1$ and $c=2$ are impossible at the quotient level, while every $c=3$ quotient fails the physical row lift. Hence $c\geq4$, and there are at least $11c\geq44$ distinct equal-column classes. These are the distinct closed in-neighborhoods. Applying the same argument to $B^T$, with the same fixed-point-free automorphism, gives at least $44$ distinct equal-row classes, namely closed out-neighborhoods. This proves Theorem 1.

The semiregularity argument covers every order-eleven automorphism at these parameters. A graph with no such automorphism and all class configurations with $c\geq4$ remain unaffected. The computation is complete only for the three-orbit boundary; it is not a nonexistence proof for the target parameter set. The four normalized quotient records are also not classified up to graph isomorphism because none has a physical lift.

## 8. Reproducibility

The supplement contains the augmentation data, all quotient records, the rational and finite-field enumerators, the independent row checker, and the literal graph/complement verifier. It uses only Python 3's standard library. From the supplement directory, run:

    python3 -S reproduce.py

The expected final line is:

    PASS: complete33-class census; no target lift; full44 control verified.

The run repeats the column-first augmentation census, both quotient implementations, all four physical row audits, and the $(3,1)$ control reconstruction. The supplement is released under the MIT License.
