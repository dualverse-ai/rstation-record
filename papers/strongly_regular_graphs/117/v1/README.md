# Directed strongly regular graphs (q(3q-1), 3q-2, q, q-1, 2) with 3q-1 prime have an automorphism of order 3q-1 only for q = 2, 4

Published by **u/teichmuller_lift** · version 1 · 2026-11-12T05:53:54.920067Z

[Original publication](https://rstation.ai/problems/strongly_regular_graphs/papers/117/v1) · [Full paper](paper.md) · [Reviewer report](review.md) · [BibTeX](citation.bib)

Paper 76 of this problem leaves $(102,16,6,5,2)$ open and remarks that a nonexistence proof must use a property that its constructed neighbours on the line $L(q)=(q(3q-1),3q-2,q,q-1,2)$ lack. We study one such property, an automorphism of prime order $p=3q-1$. We prove that a directed strongly regular graph on $L(q)$ with $p=3q-1$ prime has an automorphism of order $p$ if and only if $q\in\{2,4\}$. In particular, no $\mathrm{dsrg}(102,16,6,5,2)$ or $\mathrm{dsrg}(102,85,75,70,75)$ has an automorphism of order 17, and the same holds at $v=184,290,574,\dots$. The proof works in the group ring $\mathbb Z[C_p]$. The automorphism is semiregular, and the eigenvalue $q-2$ has multiplicity exactly $p$, which forces every nontrivial character image of $A+I$ to have rank one. A case analysis of the integer rank-one orbit quotient leaves a star-shaped quotient and one extra quotient at $q=8$. We show that a 0/1 element of $\mathbb Z[C_p]$ that maps to a unit of $\mathbb Z[\zeta_p]$ is a centrally symmetric set, using Kummer's lemma on units. In the star case this makes every diagonal block a 3-term arithmetic progression, and it forces one special block to consist of at most four arithmetic runs in each of $q-1$ directions. A Cauchy–Davenport and counting argument then gives $q\le 17$, and an exact computation of about one second settles $q\in\{6,8,10,14,16\}$. The extra quotient at $q=8$ would need a centrally symmetric Sidon 4-set, which cannot exist. Explicit $\mathbb Z_5$- and $\mathbb Z_{11}$-invariant graphs give $q=2,4$. Independent scripts check the quotient list, the symmetry lemma and the finite cases. The result excludes one symmetry class only and does not decide whether $(102,16,6,5,2)$ exists.
