# Weighted and odd neighborhood covers of a directed strongly regular graph of order 98

Mira Loom

## 1. The question

The existence of a directed strongly regular graph with parameters $(98,20,9,8,3)$ remains open in the Hobart–Brouwer table [1]. Write $A$ for its adjacency matrix and $B=A+I$. Each column of $B$ indicates the predecessors of one vertex together with the vertex itself. A positive weighted cover is a set of distinct such columns whose positive linear combination is the all-ones vector. Its size is the number of selected columns. The analogous question for closed out-neighborhoods uses the rows.

The closed-neighborhood class formulation for $t=\lambda+1$ was developed by meleta [6]. Public work by meleta and teichmuller_lift studied small class counts through the quotient matrix and class-size inventories [7]. Previous work bounded the minimum cover size between ten and twelve and located a unique unit weight in every ten-column cover [2]. The weights on the remaining nine columns were undetermined. At eleven columns there need be no unit weight to distinguish a region of the graph. We determine the entire weight list in both cases and prove that a weight strictly above one half occurs at every minimum size.

**Theorem.** If the minimum size is ten, one column has weight one, two columns have weight $2/3$ and seven have weight $1/3$. If the minimum size is eleven, three columns have weight $2/3$ and eight have weight $1/3$. If the minimum size is twelve, some weight is strictly greater than $1/2$. The same assertions hold for a minimum cover by rows.

At sizes ten and eleven three disjoint sets of size 21 leave 35 vertices. Every one of those 35 belongs to a triple of weight-$1/3$ columns. Each of the other vertices outside a unit column belongs to just two columns, one of each nonunit weight. Thus the theorem determines which membership patterns a construction may use while allowing repeated triples and unrestricted multiplicities. A minimum cover of size twelve and the existence problem remain open.

An *odd cover* is a set of closed neighborhoods in which every vertex occurs an odd number of times. **The shortest odd cover has exactly eight neighborhoods in either orientation.** The two weighted spectra above yield such a cover directly. Even when the minimum positive weighted cover has size twelve, counting the physical columns that differ by each shift in a binary plane forces an eight-column odd cover. Its indicators and the constant vector span a nine-dimensional real space containing exactly eight indicators of size 21. The singleton cells of all shortest covers impose a further restriction on the graph itself. **Every graph with these parameters has at least seventeen distinct closed in-neighborhoods and at least seventeen distinct closed out-neighborhoods.** This improves the bound of sixteen in [5].

## 2. Counts in the range of the closed adjacency matrix

Both margins of $B$ are 21, its diagonal is one, and $B^2=7B+3J$. Here $J$ is the all-ones matrix. If $x=By$ then
$$
 Bx=7x+\frac{\mathbf1^Tx}{7}\mathbf1.
$$
In particular a size-21 indicator in the column space has ten successors from an inside vertex and three from an outside vertex. Every integral range vector has mass divisible by seven. Every nonzero nonnegative integral range vector has mass at least 21 [2, Theorem 1]. These facts apply also to the row space. The Boolean exclusion of size fourteen already appears in [3, Lemma 9]. A class of identical rows of $B$ has size at most eight [2, Section 4].

Minimum cover columns are independent. In a real dependence among them both signs occur because each column has the same positive mass. Moving the weights along the dependence until the first one becomes zero reduces support and still covers every vertex. The sum of the weights is $98/21=14/3$. A vertex on a weight-one column uses just that label. Elsewhere at least two memberships are needed. We call a weight strictly between $1/2$ and one heavy, and distinguish it from weights exactly $1/2$. Two heavy supports are disjoint, and every heavy support misses every half-weight support.

The rank of $B$ is twelve. Indeed its restriction to the invariant zero-sum space has eigenvalues zero and seven, and the trace gives eleven occurrences of seven. If a minimum cover includes a unit support $S$, all rows in $S$ agree on the chosen columns. There are at least three distinct full rows there since $21>2\cdot8$. Three distinct Boolean vectors are affinely independent. Their two independent differences belong to the kernel of restriction to the chosen columns, so the cover has at most ten columns. At size ten each occupied membership outside the unit is a full row class by [2, Theorem 3]. Its size is consequently at most eight.

## 3. Pair and triple memberships

A two-column membership uses a heavy and its complementary weight, or uses two halves. At size ten remove the unit. The remaining 77 vertices use 189 incidences, so at least 42 use pairs. At size eleven no unit occurs and the 98 vertices use 231 incidences, so at least 63 use pairs. Equality in these lower bounds forces all remaining vertices to use triples.

At size ten three heavy supports are impossible because removing them from the 77-set would leave a size-fourteen indicator. If there are two heavies then a half column is also impossible by this argument. The 42 pair vertices exhaust the two heavies. At size eleven at most three heavies are possible, and three heavies preclude a half for the same reason. In that case the 63 pair vertices exhaust the three heavies. In each of these cases assigning $2/3$ to the heavies and $1/3$ to the other nonunit columns solves the cover equation at every vertex. Independence makes this solution unique.

These arguments leave fewer than two heavies at size ten and fewer than three at size eleven. The following sections exclude those weight distributions.

## 4. Ten columns

Let $W$ be the 77 vertices outside the unit. Write $h$ for the number of halves among the nine remaining columns. First suppose there is no heavy. At least 42 pair vertices require $h\ge4$, and the residual weight sum $11/3$ gives $h\le7$.

For $h=4$ every half incidence is in a pair, and the 35 other vertices use three light labels each. The range vector given by the sum of the nine columns minus $2\mathbf1_W$ indicates those 35. Removing any light from it gives a forbidden size-fourteen indicator. For $h=5$ the four lights have total weight $7/6$. Any three weigh more than $2/3$, so a vertex on one half uses exactly two lights, and a vertex on no half uses exactly three. There are consequently 42 double-half, 21 single-half and 14 no-half vertices. With $T$ the indicator of the 35 triple vertices as above and $F$ the sum of the half columns, $2\mathbf1_W-F-T$ indicates the fourteen no-half vertices. For $h=6$ the three lights have total weight $2/3$ and must occur in pairs on single-half vertices, contrary to their odd total of 63 incidences. For $h=7$ the two lights together weigh only $1/6$ and cannot occur at any vertex. This proves there is a heavy.

Suppose there is just one heavy $H$, of weight $1-a$. Its complement $D=W\setminus H$ has size 56 and contains every half. Two half supports in $D$ must meet because their disjoint union would leave a size-fourteen indicator. An intersection vertex has ten successors in each half and fifteen in $D$. There must therefore be at least five intersection vertices. These vertices all have the same membership, so each intersection has size at most eight. As the intersections within any one half are disjoint, $5(h-1)\le21$. At most two halves give at most $21+8$ pair vertices, below 42. With five halves the lights together weigh less than $2/3$. Every vertex of $D$ would then be on one or two halves. This gives 49 double-half vertices while their ten intersection classes require at least 50. Only three or four halves remain.

For four halves write $p,s,n$ for the counts on two, one and zero halves in $D$. We have $s=84-2p$, $n=p-28$ and $30\le p\le42$. At $p=42$ half the sum of the halves indicates 42 vertices in $D$, leaving fourteen. Hence $p<42$ and both $s,n$ are positive. The four light weights have total $L=2/3+a\ge1$ by a no-half vertex. At $L=1$ each no-half vertex uses all four lights and each single-half vertex uses exactly two. Indeed each side of its division of the lights has weight $1/2$. A heavy vertex uses one or two lights because its residual weight is $1/3$. With $U$ the sum of the lights, $U+2F-4\mathbf1_D-\mathbf1_H$ vanishes outside $H$ and is nonnegative integral of mass seven, a contradiction.

For $L>1$ a no-half vertex uses three lights of weights $x,y,z$ summing to one. The fourth light has weight $d=a-1/3\in(0,1/6)$. Each pair of $x,y,z$ weighs more than $1/2$. A single-half vertex has to use exactly one of them with $d$, so one has weight $1/2-d$. The other two both exceed $d$ since they sum to $1/2+d$ and each is below $1/2$. Thus every no-half vertex uses $x,y,z$. Each heavy vertex uses exactly one of them since its required weight $a$ is between $d$ and $1/2$. The three light margins give $63=21+s+3n=21+p$, contradicting $p<42$.

With three halves there are five lights. At most 24 vertices in $D$ can be half pairs. Hence at least eighteen vertices of $H$ use just one light of weight $a$. This requires at least three such lights because each membership has size at most eight.

Put $p=|\,\{x\in D:x\text{ uses two halves}\}\,|$. Then $21\le p\le24$, the lower bound coming from the 42 required pairs. The single-half and no-half counts are $63-2p\ge15$ and $p-7\ge14$. If all five light weights are $a$, their total gives $a=7/24$, and no subset sums to the $1/2$ required at a single-half vertex. With four copies of $a$ the fifth weight is $c=7/6-3a\ne a$ and $2/9<a<7/18$. Every heavy point must use a single $a$ label. A subset of the lights sums to $1/2$ only when $a=1/4$ or $a=1/3$, as follows by solving $ja=1/2$ or $c+ja=1/2$ for integer $j$. In the first case $c=5/12$ can occur at no vertex. In the second $c=1/6$ must occur exactly at the single-half vertices. Its margin 21 gives $p=21$ and fourteen no-half vertices. Their indicator is $\mathbf1_D-(F+\mathbf1_{\{c\}})/2$, where $\mathbf1_{\{c\}}$ denotes the corresponding light column.

There remain three copies of $a$ and weights $b,c$ different from $a$, with $b+c=7/6-2a$. If a heavy vertex uses more than one light it must use $b,c$, forcing $b+c=a=7/18$. A no-half vertex would then require two copies of $a$ together with a light of weight $2/9$, leaving weight $1/6$ for the fifth light. These weights cannot sum to $1/2$ on a single-half vertex. Thus every heavy point uses one $a$ label. The $b,c$ columns both lie in the $56-p\le35$ single-half and no-half vertices of $D$ and therefore meet. At a shared vertex their total with $j$ labels of weight $a$ is $7/6+(j-2)a$, equal either to $1/2$ or to one. The possibilities are $a=1/3$, $1/6$ and $1/12$. The last requires $b+c=1$. At $a=1/6$ both $b,c$ exceed $1/3$ and every single-half vertex uses all three $a$ labels. There are only $3\cdot21-21=42$ incidences of these labels in $D$, whereas those vertices require at least 45. At $a=1/3$ we have $b+c=1/2$, and neither is $1/6$ since the other would equal $a$. A no-half vertex can only use the three $a$ labels. At least fourteen such vertices would have identical memberships outside the unit, above the class bound eight. This exhausts the ten-column case.

## 5. Eleven columns

Let $g$ be the number of heavies and $h$ the number of halves. Each of the 63 required pair vertices consumes a heavy incidence or two half incidences. Thus $2g+h\ge6$. On the complement $D$ of the heavies, the range vector $2\mathbf1_D-F$ is nonnegative integral. Here $F$ denotes the sum of the half columns. Its mass $196-21(2g+h)$ is seven modulo 21 and hence at least 21. Consequently $6\le2g+h\le8$.

If $g=0$ there are six, seven or eight halves. With six all 126 half incidences are used at 63 pair vertices. The complementary size-35 range indicator contains every light and cannot contain a size-21 range indicator. With seven there are four lights of total weight $7/6$. As in Section 4, a single-half vertex uses precisely two lights, and a no-half vertex uses three. The half margins then give 63 pairs, 21 single-half vertices and fourteen no-half vertices, with the same forbidden indicator as in Section 4. With eight, all lights weigh only $2/3$ together. Every vertex uses a half and $2\mathbf1-F$ indicates 28 single-half vertices. Removing any light leaves a size-seven indicator.

If $g=2,h=2$, all half incidences must occur in pairs by the count of 63. The two columns would agree. With $g=3$ the argument of Section 3 applies. The remaining cases are $(g,h)=(1,4),(1,5),(1,6),(2,3),(2,4)$. Whenever $2g+h=8$, every light intersects a heavy. Indeed a light missing all heavies is supported where $2\mathbf1_D-F\ge1$, and subtracting it would leave nonnegative integral mass seven.

At $(1,4)$ the pair capacity is attained, and half the sum of the halves indicates 42 vertices. Its complementary 56-set contains the heavy $H$ and all six lights. Every light meets $H$ since a light missing $H$ would lie in the remaining 35-set and leave a forbidden fourteen there. Each intersection of a light with $H$ has size at least five by their ten successors each and fifteen successors in the surrounding 56-set. The six disjoint intersections cannot fit in $H$.

At $(1,6)$ let $1-c$ be the heavy weight. The four lights total $2/3+c$ and each is at most $c$, since it meets the heavy. Any three weigh at least $2/3$. Each heavy vertex uses one or two lights, and a single-half vertex uses exactly two. Outside the heavy, let $n,s,p$ count vertices on zero, one or two halves. Then $s+2n=28$ and $p=49+n$. If $s=0$, $(2\mathbf1_D-F)/2$ would indicate fourteen vertices. Hence $p<63$ and at least one heavy vertex is a pair, giving a light weight $c$. Each smaller light needs another smaller light summing with it to $c$ on a heavy vertex.

Four lights of weight $c$ give $c=2/9$ and do not supply a single-half vertex. Three copies leave one smaller light without a partner. With two copies the smaller pair sums to $c$, so $3c=2/3+c$ and $c=1/3$. A single-half vertex forces one smaller weight to be $1/6$, hence both. A no-half vertex uses all four. The sum $G$ of the two $1/3$ columns minus the two $1/6$ columns vanishes outside $H$, and takes values one or $-2$ on $H$. Thus $(\mathbf1_H-G)/3$ is an integral nonnegative range vector of mass seven.

With one copy of $c$, each of the three smaller lights has a partner among the other two. Relabel their weights $u,v,v$ with $u+v=c$. Their total gives $v=2/3-c$ and $u=2c-2/3$, so $c>1/3$. Each of the four lights exceeds $c-1/3$. The total exceeds one and omitting one brings it below one. Hence $n=0$ and $s=28$. A pair of light weights sums to $1/2$ only for $(c,u)$ at $c=7/18$ or $(v,v)$ at $c=5/12$. In either case the two labels are uniquely specified. Each of their size-21 columns would have to contain all 28 single-half vertices.

## 6. The finite eleven-column cases

We give the domains of the remaining enumerations. The supplement contains the integer algorithms, a reference output and the certificates for each weight list. The enumeration treats all real weights allowed by independence. No graph search is used.

Delete the known half-weight coordinates from the occupied membership equations and append the total weight equation. Eliminate the first heavy using the latter. A row containing that heavy has no halves or other heavy. After changing sign its new coefficients are the complements of its residual binary mask. Every remaining equation has binary coefficients and right side in $\frac16\mathbb Z$. For the three cases $(g,h)=(1,5),(2,4),(2,3)$ the numbers of remaining variables are $5,6,7$. They have full rank because a dependence would lift to a kernel vector of the original incidence rows, putting zero on the halves and the negative of the remaining sum on the eliminated coordinate.

For an $r$-dimensional binary minor $M$, border $J-2M$ with ones to obtain a sign matrix of absolute determinant $2^r|\det M|$. Hadamard's inequality gives upper bounds $6,14,32$ on $|\det M|$ in the three cases. By Cramer's rule the common reduced denominator divides $6|\det M|$. It is a multiple of six by the total weight and the occurrence of a half. Thus the largest denominators to consider are $36,84,192$.

For one heavy and five halves write $p,s,n$ for the counts outside the heavy having two, one and zero halves. Then $p=28+n$ and $s=49-2n$. Since $p\ge42$ and $s$ is odd, $14\le n\le24$. Hence $s,n>0$, and at least one heavy point is a pair. Writing $1-c$ for the heavy weight, remove one light of weight $c$. The remaining four weights sum to $7/6$. Enumerate their sorted positive numerators, restore the removed weight, and retain the reduced tuples with compatible full-rank memberships. The three possibilities are displayed below. ### A two-walk certificate for the remaining spectrum

Let $H,K$ have weights $5/6,2/3$, respectively. There are three halves with sum $F$, two sixths $U,V$ and four thirds. Let $D$ be the set with membership $K,U,V$ and let $T$ consist of the vertices on three thirds. The following identities can be checked directly on weight-one memberships. The range vector $Z=U+V-H$ has mass 21, values zero, one, two, and equals two on $D$. The vector $W=(F+Z)/2$ is an indicator of size 42, disjoint from $H$. Finally $E=\mathbf1-H-K-W=\mathbf1_T-\mathbf1_D$ has mass fourteen. Write $x=|D|$ and $t=|T|$, so $t=14+x$. If $x=0$ then $E$ itself is forbidden. At a vertex in $D$, the sets $W,K$ receive thirteen and ten successors within the complement of $H$, which receives eighteen. Their intersection is exactly $D$, so $x\ge5$.

There are at most eight entries of value two in any such $Z$. Ten doubles leave one single, whose loop makes its weighted successor sum odd instead of ten. For nine doubles let $M$ be their set. Each double has weighted successor count 17 and hence reaches at least seven doubles. A column in $M$ missed by one double has at most five predecessors in $M$ because a missing arc allows only three two-walks. At most eighteen incidences in $M\times M$ are missing. At least five columns therefore have all nine predecessors there. A single has weighted successor count ten, includes its loop, and can reach at most three singles. It must reach exactly two singles and four doubles. It misses at least one of those five full columns, yet reaches it through all four double successors. A missing arc permits only three intermediates. This yields $x\le8$ and $19\le t\le22$.

Each third column $C$ meets the complement of $T$. For $t=21$ containment would make $C=\mathbf1_T$ and give the size-seven indicator $C-E=\mathbf1_D$. For $t>21$, a vertex in $T$ outside $C$ would have at most $3+t-21$ successors in $T$, whereas $BE=7E+2\mathbf1$ requires at least nine. For $t<21$ containment is impossible by cardinality. Now take a vertex in $C$ outside $T$. There $E=0$. Outside $K$ at most three successors lie in $D\subset K$. Inside $K$ such a vertex has $Z=0$ and weighted successor count three in $Z$. Thus $BE=2$ gives at most five successors in $T$. Since this vertex has ten successors in $C$, $|C\cap T|\le16$.

If any one of the four possible triples of thirds is absent, its omitted label occurs throughout $T$, contradicting $t\ge19$. If all four triples occur, a vertex of one triple needs at least nine successors in $T$. Every other triple contains its missing label, which receives just three successors. Its own triple has at least six vertices. Four such triples give $t\ge24$. This completes the eleven-column proof. Transposition preserves the defining matrix equations and proves the assertion on rows.

## 7. A weight above one half in every minimum cover

At size twelve the cover columns span the full column range, so every fixed membership pattern determines one full row and has size at most eight. Suppose all weights are at most one half and let $h$ count the halves. Every vertex uses at least two labels, and the 252 incidences give at least 42 pair vertices. All pairs consist of two halves. It follows that $h\ge4$. As in Section 5, the mass of $2\mathbf1-F$ gives $h\le8$.

At $h=4$ all 84 half incidences occur in pairs. The union $Q$ of those pair cells is a 42-set with indicator $F/2$. The remaining 56 vertices all lie on triples of lights. Regard a pair of half labels as an edge of the complete graph on four labels. Every one of the six pair cells occurs since each half needs 21 vertices and each cell has at most eight. The four degree equations imply that opposite edges have equal populations. The populations of the three opposite pairs, counting one representative of each, therefore sum to 21. For any physical column, its value on cell $ij$ is $y_i+y_j$ for coefficient values $y_1,\ldots,y_4$ on the four halves. Thus the two binary values on opposite cells have the same sum across all three opposite pairs. Sum two would exceed the column's entire mass. Sum one uses exactly 21 points in $Q$. Every physical column meeting $Q$ is therefore supported there, and its loop puts its owner in $Q$. A source in $Q$ has no successors in the complement, although the range equation requires eight there. This excludes four halves.

At $h=8$ the four lights together weigh only $2/3$. Every vertex uses a half. The range vector $2\mathbf1-F$ indicates 28 vertices and contains each light column, leaving a forbidden difference of size seven.

Now suppose $h=5,6$ or $7$. Choose any $9-h$ light labels. Subtracting their indicators from $2\mathbf1-F$ gives an integral range vector of mass seven. It therefore takes a negative value. This can only occur at a single-half vertex using at least two chosen lights or at a no-half vertex using at least three. The chosen weights have sum at most $(8-h)/2$. If their sum exceeded that bound, each pair would weigh more than one half and, whenever there are at least three chosen labels, each triple would weigh more than one. Neither negative entry could occur. The other three lights therefore weigh at least $2/3$. Since the choice of labels was arbitrary, a single-half vertex uses exactly two lights. Five light weights total more than one whenever five or more lights are available, since the other $7-h$ lights each weigh less than one half and all lights together weigh $14/3-h/2$. A no-half vertex thus uses either three or four lights.

Write $C$ for the unweighted sum of all lights. The range vector $4\mathbf1-2F-C$ indicates precisely the no-half vertices with three lights. Its mass is $140-21h$. At $h=7$ this is negative, at $h=6$ it is fourteen, and at $h=5$ it is 35. Only five halves remain. Denote their 35 triple vertices by $T$. Each of the seven lights has at least five vertices outside $T$. Otherwise it would either be contained in $T$, leaving a size-fourteen indicator, or a source in its difference from $T$ would need five more successors in that difference than in the opposite difference. The seven outside populations sum to $7\cdot21-3\cdot35=42$. None can exceed twelve, so every light has at least nine points on $T$ and occurs on at least two distinct allowed triples by the class bound eight.

The seven independent light columns now determine their weights from a binary minor of order seven with right sides in $\frac12\mathbb Z$. The determinant bound is 32 as in Section 6. Their common denominator with the halves divides twice the determinant and is a multiple of six. The possible denominators are $6,12,\ldots,60$. Enumerate all sorted positive light numerators below $d/2$ with sum $13d/6$ and joint denominator $d$. The exact census, with the triple lower-weight and other necessary bounds documented in the supplement, checks 25,703 spectra. Only twenty give every label two allowable triples of weight one. In each of those twenty hypergraphs, a pair of light labels $i,j$ meets every allowed triple. Consequently $L_i+L_j-\mathbf1_T$ is a nonnegative integral range vector of mass seven. The certificate checks the two labels against all admissible triples, including ones which may be absent from the graph. This proves the claimed weight bound for size twelve and completes the theorem.

## 8. Odd covers and their real span

Let $j$ be the all-ones column. Over $\mathbb F_2$, the matrix $E=B+J$ is idempotent. Indeed $B^2=B+J$, both products with $J$ are $J$, and $J^2=0$. The integer matrix $J-B$ has eigenvalues $77,-7,0$ with multiplicities $1,11,86$. Its characteristic polynomial modulo two shows that $E$ has rank twelve. Also $E=B(I+J)$ with $I+J$ invertible in this field. Thus $B$ has binary rank twelve, and $Bj=j$ expresses $j$ as a sum of physical columns.

Let $m$ be the length of a shortest such expression. Its columns are independent, so $m\le12$. Both the parity of the vertex count and the odd column weights make $m$ even. Four columns cover at most 84 vertices. Six columns $X_i$ would yield the nonnegative integral range vector $(\sum X_i-j)/2$ of mass fourteen, contradicting Section 2. Hence $m\ge8$.

Write $n$ for the number of distinct physical column types and $S$ for the matrix of their representatives. Each type $X$ is a binary sum of other types. Indeed $B(j+X)=X$ modulo two. The sum on the left uses the physical columns whose owners lie outside $X$, while every owner of $X$ belongs to $X$ by its loop. Therefore every coordinate is nonzero on some element of $\ker_{\mathbb F_2}S$. In the uniform distribution on the solutions of $Se=j$ every coordinate of $e$ is one with probability one half. The expected cover length is $n/2$, so $n\ge2m$. This gives an analytic proof of the sixteen-class bound of [5]. If $m=12$, the selected columns are a binary basis whose sum is $j$. Each further column type uses an odd number of selected columns in its basis expansion. If it uses at least three, replacing them by that column shortens the expression for $j$. This would give $n=12$, in contradiction to $n\ge2m$. It follows that $m$ is eight or ten.

Multiplying the ten-column weighted cover by three and reducing modulo two leaves the unit column and the seven lights. At eleven columns it leaves the eight lights. Each gives an odd cover of minimum length eight. In either case every vertex in the 35-vertex triple region occurs three times, and each of the other 63 vertices occurs once. In particular, a graph with shortest odd cover ten must have minimum positive weighted cover size twelve.

We rule out $m=10$ below. Any sum of $t$ selected columns takes at least $t$ physical columns to represent. Otherwise substitute the shorter expression into the one for $j$. In any coset of their span, two distinct physical types differ on exactly two selected coordinates. Their difference has even weight in basis coordinates by parity, uses at most two generators, and is nonzero. Translate that coset's physical types by one of its members. The remaining types become distinct two-subsets of the ten coordinates which pairwise meet. Such a family lies in a star or consists of the three edges of a triangle. Indeed two edges meeting at one endpoint leave only their two other endpoints available for an edge missing it. If that third edge occurs, no further edge can meet all three.

### Pairing columns under binary shifts

Let $g$ be the binary difference of any two physical types, with support $H_g$. Each type $X$ for which $X+g$ is physical has $|X\cap H_g|=|H_g|/2$, since the two columns have equal size. Their real difference is a signed $7$-eigenvector. At a positive coordinate it needs at least seven positive successors, and its positive and negative supports have equal sizes. Consequently $14\le |H_g|\le42$. Summing incidences on $H_g$ over all 98 columns shows that at most 42 vertices own a type paired under $g$. At least 56 vertices, and hence at least seven types by the class size bound eight, are unpaired.

If exactly seven types are unpaired, each has eight owners. The odd cover given by the types of odd multiplicity uses only paired types. In its binary sum, a pair contributing both members can be replaced by $g$. Some pair contributes just one member, since $j$ is neither zero nor $g$. Any residual $g$ can be absorbed by changing the choice in one such pair. This yields an odd cover with at most $(n-7)/2$ columns. In particular if $m=10$ and $n=21$, at least eight types are unpaired under every shift.

**Subspace lemma.** In a square Boolean matrix with both margins $k$, fix any binary subspace $L$ of dimension $d$. At most $2dk$ vertices can own a column having a different column type in its coset of $L$.

Order the nonzero shifts $g$ in $L$ realized as differences of physical types by nondecreasing support size $h_g$. For each row position $v$ in their union set $w_v=1/h_g$ for the first support containing it, and set the remaining weights to zero. A support contributes to this sum only when it contains a previously uncovered position. Its shift is then independent of all preceding shifts, since the support of their sum is contained in the union of their supports. At most $d$ shifts introduce positions, and each contributes at most one to $\sum_v w_v$. If the column $X$ has a partner $X+g$, then $X$ contains exactly $h_g/2$ positions of that support, all of weight at least $1/h_g$. Thus its weight sum is at least one half. The total of these sums over all physical columns is $k\sum_v w_v\le kd$ by regularity, proving the claim. The bound is attained for general matrices by $2d$ disjoint all-ones square blocks of size $k$, pairing the blocks and taking the $d$ resulting differences as generators.

In particular for every binary plane at order 98 at least fourteen vertices own a type occurring alone in its coset. We call this consequence the plane lemma. It also gives an isolated type throughout the family of order $2q^2$ and closed degree $3q$ for $q\ge7$.

### Excluding a shortest cover of ten

Suppose now $m=10$, and identify the span $V$ of the selected columns with the coordinate space on ten unit vectors. The quotient by $V$ has dimension two. If there are exactly twenty distinct types, every odd cover has length ten by averaging. Label each type by its coordinate functional on the eight-dimensional binary kernel of the representatives. Within each nonzero label as many coordinates are selected as unselected in a fixed cover. Indeed the change of cover by a kernel word preserves the length, and orthogonality of the distinct characters gives this equality label by label. Pair selected and unselected types having the same label. All eight independent kernel relations use both or neither member of each pair. The ten pair differences therefore span dimension at most two. Every type has a partner in that space, contrary to the plane lemma. Averaging already gives $n\ge20$, so it remains to consider $n\ge21$.

In a star coset the physical types are $d+e_i$ for $i$ in a leaf set $I$, where $e_i$ denotes the selected unit column. In the triangle case there are four types $y+L_T$, where $T$ is a three-element set and $L_T$ is the subspace of even subsets of $T$. These descriptions include the translated origin. The zero coset contains just the ten selected types by the shortest-expression property. Write $k\ge l\ge r$ for the three other coset sizes, so $n=10+k+l+r$.

First suppose $k\ge5$. The largest coset is a star $d+e_i$. The shift $d$ pairs its $k$ types with selected columns, so $n-2k\ge7$. For $n=21$ the preceding parity argument improves this to $n-2k\ge8$. It follows in every case that $l+r=n-10-k\ge5$, so $l\ge3$.

If the second coset is a star $d'+e_j$ on a set $J$, write a third-coset type as $d+d'+z$. For each $i\in I,j\in J$ the sum of these three physical columns gives $|z+e_j+e_i|\le3$ in selected coordinates. The even word $z+e_j$ has weight at most two, since a word of weight four would have to contain all $k\ge5$ choices of $i$. Hence $z$ is a singleton, with the sole exception $z=J$ when $|J|=3$. If every $z$ is a singleton, all physical types have the form $e_i+g$ for $g$ in the plane spanned by $d,d'$. Each leaf has at least two physical types, since a singleton leaf would make its selected column independent of all the other types. This contradicts the plane lemma and also covers an empty third coset. If the exceptional $J$ occurs, each other $z$ is a singleton in $J$ since the distance from $J$ is two. Then $d$ pairs $k$ base leaves and $r-1$ second-coset leaves. At most $n-2(k+r-1)=28-n$ types are unpaired, too few for every $n\ge21$ by the shift and parity bounds.

If the second coset has the form $y+L_T$, write a third-coset type as $d+y+z$. The same argument gives $|z+t|\le2$ for every $t\in L_T$. The word $z$ is even. If it has an odd number of bits outside $T$, the translate contains all three bits inside $T$ in addition. If it has a positive even number outside, the translate contains two bits inside in addition. Thus $z\in L_T$. Now $d$ pairs $k$ base leaves and all $r$ third-coset types, again leaving at most $28-n$ unpaired.

For $k\le4$, the only remaining size lists are $(4,4,3)$ and $(4,4,4)$. In a list of three stars let $I,J,K$ be their leaf sets and $z$ the even sum of their centers in $V$. All the words $z+e_i+e_j+e_h$ with $(i,j,h)\in I\times J\times K$ have weight at most three. Hence $|z|\in\{0,2,4,6\}$. At zero the centers align in a binary plane as above. At two, three distinct representatives outside $z$ would give weight five. Two sets have at least two candidates there and the third at least one, so the marriage criterion forces their outside union to have size at most two. At four, two distinct outside coordinates from different sets would also give weight at least five by choosing a third coordinate different from both. Thus at most one set contains outside bits or all outside bits form a singleton. In either case at most eight coordinates are used, including $z$. In these two cases any unused coordinate gives a selected type independent of the other types, by using the first two centers to extend the selected basis. At six every choice must consist of three different coordinates of $z$, requiring three disjoint leaf sets totaling at least eleven inside six.

Suppose a four-element star on $I$ and a triangle coset on $T$ both occur. As before write a type of the third coset as $d+y+z$. For every $t\in L_T$ the even word $z+t$ has size at most two or equals $I$. With one bit outside $T$, the translate includes all three bits inside $T$, so the fixed outside bit is the unique point of $I\setminus T$ and $T\subset I$. With two outside bits, three distinct four-sets occur. With three or more, some word exceeds four. Hence either all third-coset words have $z\in L_T$, leaving at most $28-n$ types unpaired under $d$, or all their coordinate words are supported in the same four-set $I$ containing $T$. The latter leaves an unused selected coordinate.

For three triangle cosets, the sum of their representatives is some $z$ and all words in $z+L_T+L_{T'}+L_{T''}$ have weight at most three. On the union $D$ of the three triples each bit varies evenly. The mean weight is $|D|/2+|\operatorname{supp}(z)\setminus D|$, so at most six coordinates are used. Finally, consider two triangle cosets and a three-element star on $K$. Put $D=T\cup T'$ and let $z$ be the sum of the two triangle representatives and the star center. For each $h\in K$ the same average gives $|D|/2+|\operatorname{supp}(z+e_h)\setminus D|\le3$. If $|D|\ge5$, every latter outside set must be empty, which puts $K$ and $z$ inside $D$. If $|D|\le4$, each outside set has at most one bit and $z$ has at most two outside bits. Even after including all three bits of $K$, at most nine coordinates occur. In each case there is an unused selected coordinate. This exhausts the cosets and proves $m=8$.

### The nine-dimensional real span

For any odd cover $X_1,\ldots,X_8$ define
$$
 R=\frac{\sum_i X_i-j}{2}.
$$
Its mass is 35 and its value $\ell$ records a membership size $2\ell+1$. The negative entries of $R-X_i$ are all minus one and occur precisely at vertices covered by $X_i$ alone. The form has mass fourteen, so this singleton cell is nonempty. At any vertex of that cell the successor equation of Section 2 gives $-5$. Hence the cell has at least five vertices. The singleton cells force all coefficients to be one in any putative real expression for $j$ using the $X_i$. Since the 168 incidences include a nonsingleton vertex, $j$ adds a ninth real dimension.

Every binary-valued form on this nine-dimensional span has the expression $\sum_{i\in D}X_i-cR$ for a subset $D$ of the eight labels, by evaluation on the singleton cells. At a membership $T$ of size $2\ell+1$ it takes value $|T\cap D|-c\ell$. The positive values of $\ell$ that occur have greatest common divisor one. They belong to $\{1,2,3\}$ and their sum with multiplicity is 35. Thus $c$ is integral. A form of mass 21 satisfies $21|D|-35c=21$, giving either $|D|=1,c=0$ or $|D|=6,c=3$. In the latter case the form equals $j-R-X_i-X_j$ for the two omitted labels. Nonnegativity forces every nonsingleton to be a triple that misses both labels. There are then 35 triple vertices and 63 singleton vertices. Two entire columns on singleton vertices and at least five singletons for each of the six other labels would need 72 vertices. Hence the only size-21 indicators in the span are the original eight. All the odd-cover arguments transpose.

The integral vector $R$ also supplies a ninth independent direction modulo two. Choose twelve physical columns and twelve rows giving an odd minor, which is possible by binary rank twelve. Any integral vector in the real column range has rational coordinates of odd denominator on those columns, by restriction to that minor. Thus its reduction belongs to the binary column range. The vector $R$ vanishes on the eight nonempty singleton cells, so the only sum of the $X_i$ that could equal its reduction is zero. Its mass 35 is odd. Extending $X_1,\ldots,X_8,R$ by three physical columns over the binary field therefore supplies a basis over both fields.

In fact the binary coset of $R$ modulo the eight selected columns contains no size-21 indicator from the real range. Suppose $Z$ is one. Write its reduction as $R+\sum_{i\in D}X_i$. Parity of the masses makes $|D|$ even. On the singleton cells for $D$ the indicator is one, so $|D|\le4$. If $D$ is empty then $(R-Z)/2$ is nonnegative integral of mass seven. Otherwise put
$$
 H=\frac{Z+R-\sum_{i\in D}X_i}{2}.
$$
At a vertex in $2r+1$ selected columns, of which $a$ are in $D$, its value is $\lceil(r-a)/2\rceil$. At $|D|=2$, we have $a\le\min(2,2r+1)$, so $H$ is nonnegative integral of mass seven. At $|D|=4$ its mass is $-14$. On singletons it vanishes. At other vertices $a\ge\max(0,2r-3)$, so $r-a\le1$. A positive entry therefore equals one and requires $Z=1$. The four singleton cells already consume at least twenty points of $Z$, leaving at most one positive entry of $H$. If there is none, $-H$ violates the minimum integral mass. At a positive entry $BH=7H-2j$ requires five, exceeding the positive mass available. This proves the coset exclusion.

## 9. Excluding equality at sixteen neighborhoods

Suppose that $B$ has precisely sixteen distinct columns. Write $S$ for the matrix of representatives. The averaging argument in Section 8 shows that every solution to $Se=j$ over $\mathbb F_2$ has weight eight. There are sixteen solutions because $S$ has rank twelve. For each type $i$ let $m_i$ denote its class size. The vector $e=m\bmod 2$ is one of the covers since $Sm=21j$ over the integers.

Choose a basis of the binary kernel of $S$ and place it in the columns of $U$. Write $u_i$ for row $i$ of $U$. Each $u_i$ is nonzero by the alternative expression for each type in Section 8. In the cover $e+Uk$, coordinate $i$ changes precisely when $u_i\cdot k=1$. Consequently
$$
 0=16-2|e+Uk|=\sum_{i=1}^{16}(-1)^{e_i}(-1)^{u_i\cdot k}
 \qquad(k\in\mathbb F_2^4).
$$
Orthogonality of the characters implies that each label $u$ occurs equally often among the odd-size and even-size classes. Write $h_u$ for its multiplicity on either side. The $h_u$ sum to eight and their occupied labels span dimension four.

The following census ranges over all such histograms, up to the action of $\mathrm{GL}(4,2)$. For each histogram assign sizes from $\{1,3,5,7\}$ to its odd half and from $\{2,4,6,8\}$ to its even half. The upper limit eight follows from [5, Lemma 2]. Sorting within each parity of each label accounts for interchangeable coordinates. Retain the lexicographically first size assignment under the stabilizer of the histogram. The required total is 98, so the sum of the deficits from the coordinate maxima, divided by two, is eleven.

For a fixed assignment let a support be the set of column types selected by one row. Its size-weighted mass is 21, and its labels sum to zero because $SU=0$. A row selects at least three classes by the size cap. The nonnegative integral vector $d=S\mathbf1-3j$ has mass 42 and satisfies $Bd=7d+6j$. Each entry of $Bd$ is at most 42, so a row selects at most eight classes. At least 56 vertices select exactly three classes. The census enumerates supports under these necessary conditions. ## 10. Scope and reproduction

The proof determines weight spectra conditional on a minimum cover having size ten or eleven. At twelve the weight above one half is necessary. Its remaining weights and the existence of a graph are open. The standard eigenspace description of directed strongly regular graphs provides the ambient real linear geometry [4]. Here the decisive constraints use a common set of 98 vertices for the membership counts, integer range indicators and directed two-walks. Every feasible graph supplies all three.

The ten-column proof and the shortest odd-cover theorem are analytic. The supplement independently checks the one-heavy alternatives at ten and exhausts the three finite eleven-column domains, the five-half domain at twelve columns, and the class census of Section 9. Extract the archive and run the following command with Python 3.10 or later and a C++20 compiler. A clean replay used one CPU and took 348 seconds. The output includes the weight tuples, incidence counts, range-form certificates, exceptional walk checks and the class-census results.

python3 -B replay.py --check
