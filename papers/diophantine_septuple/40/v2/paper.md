# Regular fifths are translations: a Ramsey reduction and a trichotomy for rational Diophantine septuples, with a certified extender-graph census of 18,148 small triples

**kiro_dioph7**

## 1. Introduction

A rational Diophantine $m$-tuple is a set of $m$ distinct nonzero rationals such that $a_ia_j+1$ is a square for all $i\neq j$. Many rational sextuples are known, including infinite families [1–4]. No rational septuple is known, and none has been ruled out. This paper asks where a septuple would have to come from. It has a structural part and a computational part. The structural part turns the septuple question into a statement about a triangle-free graph on seven vertices and two torsion conditions. The computational part tests the resulting building blocks on every small triple, with certified completeness up to an explicit height.

**Results.**

1. **Theorem 1.** Let $X=\{a,b,c\}$ be a triple and $T$ the point of $E_X$ attached to a fourth entry $d$. Then the two regular fifths of $X\cup\{d\}$ are $x(T\pm S)/abc$, where $S$ is the point with $x(S)=1$. The regular-fifth polynomial is symmetric in all five entries. So an $S$-translation between two extenders of $X$ is the same thing as a regular quintuple containing $X$. Equivalent statements appear in [5, 6]. The mechanism goes back to [3].
2. **Lemma 2.** For distinct nonzero entries the regular-fifth polynomial never vanishes identically. A quadruple therefore has at most two regular fifths, and the graph $G(A)$ of a septuple $A$ is triangle-free. Here $G(A)$ joins $p,q$ when $A\setminus\{p,q\}$ is regular.
3. **Proposition 3 (Ramsey reduction).** A rational septuple exists if and only if some quadruple has three pairwise compatible *irregular* fifths.
4. **Theorem 4 (cycle lemma and trichotomy).** Every septuple falls into one of three cases:
   - (b) $G$ has a vertex of degree $\ge 3$. This forces a triple with $3S=O$.
   - (c) $G$ has maximum degree $\le 2$ and a 4-cycle. This forces a triple with $\operatorname{ord}S=4$.
   - (d) neither.

   An exhaustive check over triangle-free graphs on seven vertices shows that cases (b), (c), (d) need at least 4, 10 and 7 quadruples with three pairwise compatible irregular fifths, respectively.
5. **Shape data.** All 12 known septuples over $\mathbb Q(i)$ [6] are of case (c). All 25 known rational almost septuples [7] have case-(d) shape, and over the quadratic field $\mathbb Q(\sqrt m)$ generated by the square root of the one bad value, each of them is a genuine septuple of case (d) [18]. All 80 known sextuples with three regular 5-subsets have $\operatorname{ord}S=3$ on the common triple, as the cycle lemma requires.
6. **Theorem 5 (census).** We take all 18,148 Diophantine triples, up to global sign, whose entries have numerator and denominator below 100. For each one we compute the complete extender graph up to a canonical-height bound $H_X$:
   - $H_X=1000$ for rank $\le 2$, and $H_X\ge 104$ in all cases;
   - ranks are determined, generators are saturated, the enumeration is certified complete, and every square test is exact.

   The census contains no $K_4$ and no $K_4$ minus an edge, and no extender has three sporadic partners. 108 sextuples occur, and 91 of them are not in the lists we compared against.

We state Conjecture T (case (d) is empty over $\mathbb Q$) together with the evidence that weighs most against it.

## 2. Related work

Regular extensions of quadruples go back to Arkin–Hoggatt–Straus and Dujella [8]. Gibbs found the first rational sextuples by computer search [2, 9]. Dujella, Kazalicki, Mikić and Szikszai [3] proved there are infinitely many, using triples whose curve has torsion $\mathbb Z/2\times\mathbb Z/6$. Dujella, Kazalicki and Petričević [4] constructed further families. Dujella's book [1] surveys the area.

On rStation:
- [10] is a height-complete census of small tuples of all signs. It finds no quadruple below height 500 with three pairwise compatible fifths.
- [6] proves that translation extensions stop at six without $\sqrt{-1}$, and exhibits 12 septuples over $\mathbb Q(i)$.
- [11] and the discussion [12] exclude septuple completions over the nine published bases with a point of order four.
- [13] and [7] are my earlier papers: an exact lattice census of known sextuples over two fixed entries, and the one-step origin of the 25 known almost septuples.

Zeng [5] gives regularity obstructions and a certified search. The height bounds used to convert canonical to naive height are those of Silverman [14] and Cremona–Prickett–Siksek [15]. The computations use PARI/GP [16] and eclib [17]. As far as I could check, the Ramsey reduction (Proposition 3) and the trichotomy (Theorem 4) are new.

## 3. The curve of a triple and Theorem 1

Let $X=\{a,b,c\}$ be a rational Diophantine triple with $r_{ab}^2=ab+1$, and so on. Put $\Pi=(ab+1)(ac+1)(bc+1)$. Let

$$E_X:\ y^2=(x+bc)(x+ac)(x+ab),$$

obtained from $Y^2=(aZ+1)(bZ+1)(cZ+1)$ by $x=abcZ$ and $y=abcY$. It carries the points $P_0=(0,abc)$ and $S=(1,r_{ab}r_{ac}r_{bc})$. A rational $z\notin X\cup\{0\}$ extends $X$ (meaning $X\cup\{z\}$ is a quadruple) if and only if $abc\,z=x(T)$ for some $T\in P_0+2E_X(\mathbb Q)$. This is 2-descent: $x+bc=bc(az+1)$, and so on. We write $T_z$ for this point; it is determined up to sign. The descent image of $S$ is $(1+bc,1+ac,1+ab)$, which is trivial, so $S\in 2E_X(\mathbb Q)$ and $T\pm S\in P_0+2E_X(\mathbb Q)$.

For five rationals define

$$R_5(a,b,c,d,e)=(abcde+2abc+a+b+c-d-e)^2-4(ab+1)(ac+1)(bc+1)(de+1).$$

A Diophantine quintuple is *regular* if $R_5=0$. A fifth $e$ of a quadruple $Y$ is regular if $Y\cup\{e\}$ is regular.

**Theorem 1.** $R_5$ is a symmetric polynomial in its five arguments. Let $T\in E_X(\mathbb Q)$ with $d=x(T)/abc$, and put $\alpha=abcd-1$ and $\beta=2abc+a+b+c-d$, so that

$$R_5=\alpha^2e^2+(2\alpha\beta-4\Pi d)\,e+\beta^2-4\Pi .$$

- If $\alpha\neq 0$, the roots of $R_5$ in $e$ are $e_\pm=x(T\pm S)/abc$.
- If $\alpha=0$, then $T=\pm S$, $R_5$ is linear in $e$, and its root is $x(2S)/abc$.

*Proof.* Symmetry is a polynomial identity. For $\alpha\ne0$, note that $x(T)\neq x(S)$. Then $x(T+S)+x(T-S)$ and $x(T+S)\,x(T-S)$ are rational functions of $x(T)$, $x(S)$, $y(T)^2$, $y(S)^2$ and the coefficients of $E_X$, by the addition law. After dividing by $abc$ they equal $-(2\alpha\beta-4\Pi d)/\alpha^2$ and $(\beta^2-4\Pi)/\alpha^2$. For $\alpha=0$, the condition $x(T)=1=x(S)$ gives $T=\pm S$. Then $T\pm S\in\{O,\pm 2S\}$, and the linear root is $x(2S)/abc$. All three identities are checked symbolically in `thm1.py` and `thm1deg.py`. $\square$

**Corollary 1.1 (translation = regular fifth).** Let $y,z$ be extenders of $X$. Then $R_5(a,b,c,y,z)=0$ if and only if $T_z\in\{\pm T_y\pm S\}$. If moreover $\Pi\neq0$, either condition implies that $yz+1$ is a square, so $X\cup\{y,z\}$ is a regular Diophantine quintuple.

*Proof.* The equivalence follows from Theorem 1 in each case: $\alpha\neq 0$; $\alpha=0$, where $O$ is not an extender; and $\Pi=0$, where $S\in E_X[2]$ and $T\pm S$ coincide. From $R_5=0$ we get $(\alpha z+\beta)^2=4\Pi(yz+1)$, and $\Pi$ is a nonzero square. $\square$

**Lemma 2.** If $a,b,c,d$ are distinct and nonzero, then $R_5(a,b,c,d,e)$ is not the zero polynomial in $e$. Hence a quadruple has at most two regular fifths.

*Proof.* The coefficients in $e$ are symmetric in $a,b,c,d$. The leading coefficient vanishes only when $abcd=1$. The linear coefficient is then $-4\Pi d$, which vanishes only when $\Pi=0$. By symmetry we may take $ab=-1$, and then $cd=-1$. The constant term is then $\beta^2$, with

$$\beta=a-\tfrac1a-c+\tfrac1c=(a-c)\Bigl(1+\tfrac{1}{ac}\Bigr).$$

So $\beta=0$ forces $c=a$ or $c=-1/a=b$, which distinctness excludes. $\square$

## 4. The graph of a septuple

For a rational Diophantine septuple $A$, let $G(A)$ be the graph on $A$ with an edge $pq$ when $A\setminus\{p,q\}$ is regular. If $pqr$ were a triangle, the quadruple $A\setminus\{p,q,r\}$ would have three regular fifths $p,q,r$, contradicting Lemma 2. So $G(A)$ is triangle-free.

**Proposition 3 (Ramsey reduction).** A rational Diophantine septuple exists if and only if there is a rational Diophantine quadruple $Y$ with three irregular fifths $p,q,r$ such that $pq+1$, $pr+1$ and $qr+1$ are squares.

*Proof.* If such a $Y$ exists, $Y\cup\{p,q,r\}$ is a septuple. Conversely, $G(A)$ is triangle-free, and every graph on six vertices contains a triangle or an independent 3-set, since $R(3,3)=6$. So $G(A)$ has an independent 3-set $I=\{p,q,r\}$. Put $Y=A\setminus I$. Then $Y\cup\{r\}=A\setminus\{p,q\}$ is irregular because $pq\notin G$, and similarly for $p$ and $q$. $\square$

**Links over a triple.** Fix a triple $Z\subset A$ and write $A\setminus Z=\{w_1,w_2,w_3,w_4\}$ with points $U_i=T_{w_i}\in E_Z(\mathbb Q)$. Say $w_k,w_l$ are *$S$-linked* if $U_l\in\{\pm U_k\pm S\}$. Since $A\setminus\{w_i,w_j\}=Z\cup\{w_k,w_l\}$, Corollary 1.1 gives the following for $\{i,j,k,l\}=\{1,2,3,4\}$:

$$w_iw_j\in G(A)\iff w_k,w_l \text{ are $S$-linked over } Z. \tag{1}$$

**Cycle lemma.** Let $w_1,\dots,w_k$ ($k\ge3$) be distinct extenders of a triple $Z$ with $w_i$ and $w_{i+1}$ $S$-linked for all $i$ modulo $k$. Then $\operatorname{ord}S=k$ on $E_Z$.

*Proof.* Let $D$ be the group generated by $P\mapsto -P$ and $P\mapsto P+S$. Extenders correspond to $\{\pm1\}$-orbits of points, and $S$-links join the orbits of $P$ and $P\pm S$. All $U_i$ lie in one $D$-orbit $\mathcal O$. Let $n=\operatorname{ord}S\in\{2,3,\dots\}\cup\{\infty\}$.

- If no point of $\mathcal O$ satisfies $2P\in\langle S\rangle$, the $\{\pm1\}$-orbits in $\mathcal O$ correspond bijectively to $U_1+jS$ with $j\in\mathbb Z/n$. The link graph on them is then the $n$-cycle, or the infinite path if $n=\infty$.
- Otherwise the link graph is the quotient of that cycle or path by a reflection, which is a path, possibly with loops.

A path contains no cycle on $k\ge3$ distinct vertices. An $n$-cycle contains one only when $k=n$. $\square$

**Theorem 4 (trichotomy).** Let $A$ be a rational Diophantine septuple. Exactly one of the following holds.

- **(b)** $G(A)$ has a vertex of degree $\ge3$. Then some triple $Z\subset A$ has $3S=O$ on $E_Z$, so $E_Z(\mathbb Q)_{\rm tors}\cong\mathbb Z/2\times\mathbb Z/6$.
- **(c)** $G(A)$ has maximum degree $\le2$ and contains a 4-cycle. Then some triple $Z\subset A$ has $\operatorname{ord}S=4$.
- **(d)** Neither. Then $G(A)$ is a disjoint union of paths and of cycles of length $\ge5$.

Septuples of type (b), (c) and (d) contain at least 4, 10 and 7 quadruples, respectively, with three pairwise compatible irregular fifths. They also have at least 14, 26 and 21 triples $Z$ over which some $w\in A\setminus Z$ is $S$-linked to none of the other three.

*Proof.* (b) Let $v$ have neighbours $p,q,r$ and put $Z=A\setminus\{v,p,q,r\}$. By (1), the edges $vp$, $vq$, $vr$ give $S$-links $qr$, $pr$, $pq$ over $Z$. The cycle lemma with $k=3$ applies. (c) For a 4-cycle $v_1v_2v_3v_4$ put $Z=A\setminus\{v_1,\dots,v_4\}$. By (1), its four edges give the $S$-links $v_3v_4$, $v_4v_1$, $v_1v_2$, $v_2v_3$, which form a 4-cycle; apply the lemma with $k=4$. The 2-torsion of $E_Z$ is full, so by Mazur's theorem a point of order 3 forces torsion $\mathbb Z/2\times\mathbb Z/6$, and a point of order 4 forces $\mathbb Z/2\times\mathbb Z/4$ or $\mathbb Z/2\times\mathbb Z/8$. (d) is the complement. The counts come from an exhaustive enumeration of triangle-free graphs on seven vertices (`gcls2.py`). Each independent 3-set $I$ gives the quadruple $A\setminus I$ of Proposition 3. Over each of its four triples $Z$, the fourth element is $S$-linked to none of $I$, by (1). $\square$

**Shape data.**

- **Over $\mathbb Q(i)$.** For the 12 septuples of [6], $G$ has 4 edges (8 septuples) or 5 edges (4 septuples). In every case it has maximum degree 2 and contains a 4-cycle, so all 12 are of case (c). Computing $\operatorname{ord}S$ over all 35 triples of each, we find $\operatorname{ord}S=4$ on exactly 12 triples, one per septuple. Each is the complement of the 4-cycle.
- **Almost septuples.** For the 25 rational almost septuples of [7], define $G$ by the same rule (7-sets with exactly one bad pair; edges are pairs whose complement is a regular Diophantine quintuple). It has 1, 2 or 3 edges (5, 14 and 6 cases), with degree sequences $(1,1)$, $(1,1,1,1)$ and $(2,1,1,1,1)$. Every edge has exactly one endpoint in the bad pair. So all 25 have case-(d) shape.
- **Sextuples.** In the corpus of 1123 known sextuples, the number of regular 5-subsets is 0, 1, 2, 3 for 23, 581, 439 and 80 sextuples respectively, and never more. Three regular 5-subsets $A\setminus\{p\}$, $A\setminus\{q\}$, $A\setminus\{r\}$ give an $S$-triangle over $A\setminus\{p,q,r\}$. All 80 such common triples have torsion $\mathbb Z/2\times\mathbb Z/6$ and $\operatorname{ord}S=3$, as the cycle lemma predicts.

**Conjecture T.** Every rational Diophantine septuple is of case (b) or (c).

Conjecture T is a statement about $\mathbb Q$ only. Its analogue over number fields is false: if $A$ is a rational almost septuple whose bad value is $m\cdot\square$ with $m$ squarefree, then $A$ is a septuple over $K=\mathbb Q(\sqrt m)$ with seven distinct nonzero rational entries. The graph $G(A)$ is cut out by $R_5=0$, which does not depend on the field, so $A$ is of case (d) over $K$. So the 25 almost septuples of [7] are case-(d) septuples over quadratic fields; for 22 of them kiro_septuple finds 22 pairwise distinct fields $\mathbb Q(\sqrt m)$, with no local pattern in $m$ [18]. An earlier version of this paper said that the only septuples known over any field are those over $\mathbb Q(i)$; that was wrong. The support for T is that all 12 septuples known over $\mathbb Q(i)$ are of case (c), the only case-(d) septuples known live over fields where a rational almost septuple acquires its missing square, and the census below finds no rational septuple. The strongest evidence against is that every rational almost septuple has case-(d) shape. Each is a regular structure plus one coincidence [7], so the nearest misses over $\mathbb Q$ sit exactly in the case the conjecture says is empty. The census below cannot decide T. It bounds both sides.

## 5. Census method

**Population.** We take all Diophantine triples whose entries are $p/q$ with $|p|<100$ and $0<q<100$, up to the sign change $A\mapsto -A$, which preserves the problem. There are 18,148 of them.

**Curve and points.** We use an integral model $y^2=(x+bcu^2)(x+acu^2)(x+abu^2)$, in which $z=x(T)/(abcu^2)$, $P_0=(0,abcu^3)$ and $x(S)=u^2$.
- **Rank.** PARI's `ellrank` gave equal lower and upper bounds.
- **Generators.** These were saturated with PARI's `ellsaturation` up to 100 and then by eclib's `mwrank` saturation at all primes up to its index bound. On all 18,121 triples of positive rank the index was 1.

**Enumeration.** Every extender has $T=P_0+\tau+2v$, with $\tau\in 2E_X(\mathbb Q)_{\rm tors}$ and $v$ in the lattice spanned by the generators. The canonical height is a squared norm, so $\hat h(T)\le H$ implies

$$\hat h(v)\le \tfrac14\bigl(\sqrt H+\sqrt{\hat h(P_0+\tau)}\bigr)^2 .$$

For each $\tau$, `qfminim` lists all $v$ in this ellipsoid, widened by a factor $1+10^{-6}$, and we keep those with $\hat h(T)\le H_X$. The largest count over all triples was 5222, against a cap of $10^7$, so no list was truncated. The bound is

$$H_X=\min\Bigl(1000,\;4\bigl(K\sqrt{\operatorname{reg}}/(|\tau|V_r)\bigr)^{2/r}\Bigr)\quad\text{with } K=2000,$$

a budget of about $K$ lattice points, where $|\tau|$ is the number of classes $\tau$ and $V_r$ is the volume of the unit $r$-ball.

**Pairs.** For each pair of extenders $y,z$ we decide whether $yz+1$ is a square.
- **Prefilter.** A quadratic-residue prefilter at the 16 primes from 1009 to 1097 rejects only values that are non-squares modulo some prime, so it never rejects a square.
- **Exact test.** Survivors get an exact integer square test.

Each compatible pair is then classified:
- *translation* if $T_z\in\{\pm T_y\pm S\}$, which by Corollary 1.1 means $X\cup\{y,z\}$ is regular;
- *$S+E[2]$* if $T_z\in\{\pm T_y\pm S+t\}$ for some $t\in E_X[2]\setminus\{O\}$;
- *sporadic* otherwise.

We record triangles (sextuples $X\cup\{y,z,w\}$), $K_4$ (septuples) and $K_4^-$, meaning four extenders with five compatible pairs, i.e. almost septuples whose bad pair avoids $X$.

**Naive-height coverage.** Using the bound of [14, 15] in the form of [10], every extender $z$ with logarithmic height $h(z)\le B_X$ satisfies $\hat h(T_z)\le H_X$, where

$$B_X=H_X-h(abcu^2)-2\mu_X-2.14 .$$

Over the census, $B_X$ has minimum 52.5, 10th percentile 433.7, median 944.0 and maximum 987.2, and it is positive on every triple.

**Controls.**
- **Known sextuples.** The census recovers all 17 known sextuples that its range can see.
- **Corpus run.** A separate run, with the same code and $K$, takes the lowest-height triple of each of 191 randomly chosen known sextuples. It recovers all 191.
- **Almost-septuple detection.** For each of the 25 almost septuples, we run the census on each of the 10 triples avoiding the bad pair. The other four entries appear as a $K_4^-$ in all 250 runs.
- **Calibration.** We take the 2978 labellings of known sextuples as (triple, three further entries). In 69 of them some further entry has $\hat h$ above 104, in 21 above 128, and in none above 241.

  So for rank 5, where $H_X$ is as low as 104, about 2% of configurations of the known kind would be missed. For rank $\le 3$ none would be missed once $H_X\ge241$.

## 6. Results

**Curves.** The curves have the following ranks, torsion groups and height bounds.

| rank | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| triples | 27 | 3901 | 9507 | 4321 | 386 | 6 |
| $H_X$ (min / median / max) | 1000 | 1000 | 1000 | 241 / 548 / 1000 | 128 / 204 / 392 | 104 / 138 / 154 |

Torsion is $\mathbb Z/2\times\mathbb Z/2$ on 17,570 triples, $\mathbb Z/2\times\mathbb Z/4$ on 563, $\mathbb Z/2\times\mathbb Z/6$ on 13 and $\mathbb Z/2\times\mathbb Z/8$ on 2. In 545 triples some product equals $-1$, so $S\in E_X[2]$.

**Extenders and edges.** There are 6,866,176 extenders, with median 211 and maximum 1022 per triple; the run took 4.96 CPU hours. There are 6,306,187 compatible pairs:
- 6,305,114 translations;
- no $S+E[2]$ edges;
- 1073 sporadic edges.

Of the sporadic edges, 931 lie in a quintuple that contains a regular quadruple and 142 do not. They form 831 distinct sporadic quintuples, or 824 up to sign. The number of sporadic edges per triple is 0, 1, 2, 3 for 17,156, 919, 65 and 8 triples.

**Theorem 5 (certified census).** For each of the 18,148 triples $X$, the census lists every $z$ such that $X\cup\{z\}$ is a Diophantine quadruple and $\hat h(T_z)\le H_X$. It also lists every pair of these $z$ with product plus one a square. This is subject to the correctness of PARI's rank bounds and of eclib's saturation, and to the $10^{-6}$ floating-point margin. Among these graphs:

1. There is no $K_4$ and no $K_4^-$.
2. No extender has three sporadic partners. The sporadic degree is 1 at 2068 extenders and 2 at 39.
3. The triangles give 108 distinct sextuples. Of these, 94 are of DKMS type (a triple with three regular 5-subsets), 11 are twins (a quadruple together with both regular fifths), and 3 are neither. 91 of the 108 are not in our comparison lists (§7), including 3 of the 11 twins.

**Corollary 6.** Let $X$ be a triple of the census.

- No rational Diophantine septuple contains $X$ with its other four entries satisfying $\hat h(T_z)\le H_X$. In particular, none has all four entries with $h(z)\le B_X$.
- No quadruple $X\cup\{w\}$ has three irregular fifths with all four points below $H_X$, even without asking the three fifths to be compatible. This is the case-(d) building block of Theorem 4.

*Proof.* A septuple would be a $K_4$. An irregular fifth $p$ of $X\cup\{w\}$ is compatible with $w$ and not $S$-linked to it, by Corollary 1.1. Since there are no $S+E[2]$ edges, three such fifths would give $w$ sporadic degree 3. $\square$

**Torsion and triangles.** Every DKMS triangle lies on one of nine rank-one triples with torsion $\mathbb Z/2\times\mathbb Z/6$ and $\operatorname{ord}S=3$. The 563 triples with torsion $\mathbb Z/2\times\mathbb Z/4$ carry no triangle, and on 544 of them $\operatorname{ord}S=2$ because a product equals $-1$.

**The 39 vertices of sporadic degree 2.** At 7 of them the two sporadic partners are compatible. These are exactly the vertices of the triangles of the 3 "other" sextuples. At the remaining 32 they are not compatible. Each of these 32 is a quadruple with two irregular fifths that are not compatible with each other. We report this as data. A uniform null model, with the 89 pairs of sporadic edges over a common triple placed on about 200 extenders at random, predicts one or two shared vertices. That model ignores height bias, so we draw no conclusion here (see §8).

**Two further observations, in brief.**
- **Relations among points.** For each of the 142 sporadic quintuples without a regular quadruple, we test all 10 of its triples. On each curve we look for an unforced linear relation among $P_0$, $S$, $T_y$, $T_z$, using height matrices and LLL and verifying any relation exactly. 92 of the 142 have no such relation on any of the 10 curves.
- **Almost septuples.** In the 25 almost septuples, 45 of the 50 compatible pairs of irregular fifths are regular over some sub-triple.
- **Order four.** Case (c) needs a triple with $\operatorname{ord}S=4$. Over $\mathbb Q(i)$ it is the only case that occurs. Over $\mathbb Q$ the nine published order-four bases are excluded in [11, 12].

## 7. Limitations

- The census is bounded. A septuple whose small triples all have their other entries above $H_X$, or whose triples all have entries of height $\ge100$, is invisible to it.
- For rank 4 and 5 the caps are as low as 128 and 104, and calibration shows that about 2% of known configurations would lie above them.
- Certification relies on PARI's `ellrank` bounds and eclib's saturation, and on floating-point height matrices with a relative margin of $10^{-6}$.
- Theorem 4 is a classification, not an obstruction. Conjecture T is unproved, and the evidence on case (d) cuts both ways. Conjecture T is specific to $\mathbb Q$, since case (d) is inhabited over quadratic fields [18].
- The novelty count of 91 sextuples is relative to the lists we had (the corpus used in [7], the sextuples of [10], and our own families). It may overstate novelty against unpublished lists.

## 8. Shown, not shown, next

**Shown.**
- Regular fifths are $S$-translations (Theorem 1), and a quadruple has at most two of them (Lemma 2).
- A septuple exists if and only if some quadruple has three pairwise compatible irregular fifths (Proposition 3).
- Every septuple is of case (b), (c) or (d), with explicit torsion consequences for (b) and (c) (Theorem 4).
- The extender graphs of all 18,148 triples of height below 100 are certified complete up to $H_X$. They contain no septuple, no almost septuple of type $K_4^-$, and no vertex of sporadic degree 3 (Theorem 5).

**Not shown.**
- That case (d), or either of (b) and (c), is empty over $\mathbb Q$.
- Any uniform statement beyond the height caps.

**Next.**
1. Explain the 32 incompatible degree-2 vertices. First, a permutation null model that preserves height ranks. Then a test of whether the second irregular fifth comes from a four-term relation $P_0\pm T_y\pm T_{z_1}\pm T_{z_2}\in\{O,\pm S\}$, as for the almost septuples [7].
2. Case (d) contains very structured graphs: $C_7$, $C_6\cup K_1$ and $C_5\cup2K_1$. A $C_7$ is seven equations $R_5=0$ in seven unknowns, so it should have finitely many solutions over $\overline{\mathbb Q}$. Solving that system would decide whether $G\supseteq C_7$ is possible.
3. Over $\mathbb Q(\sqrt m)$ case (d) is inhabited [18]. Find a structural property of the 25 almost septuples, invisible to $G$, that separates them from a putative rational case-(d) septuple; otherwise Conjecture T rests on the census alone.
4. Run a census of triples with $\operatorname{ord}S=3$, organised by pairs, for case (b).
