## Introduction

The Konavle family [1] of rational almost Diophantine septuples is built from a seed triple by regular steps: both regular fourths of the triple, a regular fourth of a new triple, and two regular fifths [2, 3]. Regular steps of this kind are classical [4, 5, 7]; families that use both regular fourths followed by regular fifths were constructed in [6, Section 3]; the root relations and the regular closure behind the Konavle family are in [1, Sections 4–5]; and the identity for $1 + d_+d_-$ on which Section 2 rests is [10, Section 2, Lemma 1]. None of that machinery, and none of the squares it forces, is new here. What we add is the factorization of one root relation, the surface it defines, and a second curve on that surface:

- For an arbitrary triple of rational numbers on an explicit quadric, the next member of the construction is rational and one further square is explicit (Theorem 1).
- One linear relation among roots, which holds on the Konavle family, cuts the quadric in a cubic that splits into three rational factors (Theorem 2).
- One factor is a rational surface with two parameters, written explicitly, on which eight numbers satisfy twenty-two of the twenty-eight square conditions over a quadratic field (Theorem 3).
- On this surface there are two explicit rational curves. On the first, two more conditions hold; it is the Konavle family. On the second, a different condition holds, the one that the Konavle family lacks (Theorem 4).
- Inside the domain of the parametrization the two curves have exactly one common point, and it is degenerate (Theorem 5).

Theorem 4 gives sufficient conditions only. We do not prove that the remaining square conditions fail away from these curves, and we exclude nothing beyond the intersection.

Every statement is a polynomial identity and is proved by direct expansion. The supplement contains a certificate that verifies each identity symbolically.

## 1. Conventions

Let $a, b, c$ be elements of a field of characteristic zero, and let $r, s, t$ satisfy

$$r^2 = 1 + ab,\qquad s^2 = 1 + ac,\qquad t^2 = 1 + bc.$$

Put

$$S = a + b + c + 2abc,\qquad g = b^2 - ab - bc - 1,$$

$$d_+ = S + 2rst,\qquad d_- = S - 2rst.$$

Let $z$ satisfy $z^2 = 1 + d_+ d_-$ and put

$$e = 2S + b + 2b(z^2 - 1) + 2zg.$$

Let $y$ satisfy $y^2 = 1 + ea$. Put

$$\rho_+ = d_+(bs - rt) + z(bs + rt),\qquad \rho_- = d_-(bs + rt) + z(bs - rt),$$

$$m = e\,a\,d_+\,b,\qquad \Pi = y\cdot\rho_+\cdot(bz + g)\cdot(at + rs)\cdot r\cdot(bs + rt),$$

and, when $m \ne 1$,

$$(m - 1)^2\cdot f_+ = (e + a + d_+ + b)(m + 1) + 2(ead_+ + eab + ed_+b + ad_+b) + 2\Pi.$$

Finally $f_-$ is obtained from $f_+$ by changing the sign of $t$. The eight elements $a, b, c, d_+, d_-, e, f_+, f_-$ form the *octad* of the seed $(a, b, c)$. All signs are fixed by these formulas once $r, s, t, z, y$ are chosen.

**Lemma (regular steps).** *With the notation above, and for any $a, b, c, d$ with roots $r_{xy}$ of $1 + xy$:*

$$1 + ad_+ = (at + rs)^2,\qquad 1 + bd_+ = (bs + rt)^2,\qquad 1 + cd_+ = (cr + st)^2,$$

*and if $E$ is defined by $(abcd - 1)^2 E = (a + b + c + d)(abcd + 1) + 2(abc + abd + acd + bcd) + 2\,r_{ab}r_{ac}r_{ad}r_{bc}r_{bd}r_{cd}$, then*

$$(abcd - 1)^2 (1 + aE) = (a\,r_{bc}r_{bd}r_{cd} + r_{ab}r_{ac}r_{ad})^2,$$

*and likewise for $b$, $c$, $d$.*

These are the regular fourth and the regular fifth of [4, 7]. Both are polynomial identities modulo the relations $r_{xy}^2 = 1 + xy$.

## 2. The quadric of seeds

**Theorem 1.** *For all $a, b, c$:*

$$1 + d_+ d_- = a^2 + b^2 + c^2 - 2ab - 2ac - 2bc - 3.$$

*Hence $z^2$ is this quadratic form, and on the quadric it defines:*

$$1 + be = (bz + g)^2,\qquad 1 + ed_+ = \rho_+^2,\qquad 1 + ed_- = \rho_-^2,$$

*the quadruple $\{d_+, d_-, b, e\}$ is regular, and*

$$\rho_+ + \rho_- = 2s\,\bigl(b(S + z) - 2(1 + ab)(1 + bc)\bigr).$$

In particular $e$ and $\rho_+ + \rho_-$ do not involve $t$. If $a, b, c, z$ are rational then $e$ is rational, although $d_+$ and $d_-$ need not be. The first identity is [10, Section 2, Lemma 1]; see also [3]. When $ab = r^2 - 1$ and $ac = s^2 - 1$ the quadric becomes

$$(az)^2 = a^4 - (2r^2 + 2s^2 - 1)\,a^2 + (r^2 - s^2)^2.$$

## 3. One relation and a cubic

On the Konavle family the square $1 + ea$ is paid by a linear relation among roots [1, Theorem 2]. In the present notation it reads

$$r_{ea} + 2(bz + g) = 1 + \rho_+ + \rho_-.$$

By Theorem 1 the right-hand side lies in the field generated by $a, b, c, s, z$. Define $L = 1 - 2(bz + g) + \rho_+ + \rho_-$ and ask that $L^2 = 1 + ea$.

**Theorem 2.** *Let $ab = r^2 - 1$, $ac = s^2 - 1$ and $\zeta = az$. Then $a^4(L^2 - 1 - ea) = U + V\zeta$ with polynomials $U, V$ in $a, r, s$, and*

$$U^2 - V^2\zeta^2 = -(2s - 1)\,a^4\cdot F_1\cdot F_2\cdot F_3,$$

$$\begin{aligned}&F_1 = 3a^2 + 4(r^2 - 1)(s^2 - 1) \\ &F_2 = (2s - 3)\,a^2 - 4(s - 1)^2\,(r^2(2s - 3) + 1) \\ &F_3 = 4r^2a^2 + (r^2 - 1)(4r^2s^2 - 5r^2 + 1)\end{aligned}$$

So the relation can hold, for a suitable $z$, only where one of $F_1, F_2, F_3$ vanishes or $2s = 1$. On $F_1 = 0$ one has $1 + bc = 1/4$. In the examples we computed, the octads on $F_1 = 0$ and on $F_3 = 0$ are degenerate, and we do not study these two components here. The factor $F_2$ is the interesting one.

## 4. The surface

**Theorem 3.** *Let $v, \lambda$ be independent variables and put*

$$\begin{aligned}&2v\lambda \cdot a = (v + 1)(v + \lambda^2) \\ &2v\lambda(v + 1)(v + \lambda^2) \cdot b = (v - \lambda^2 - 2v\lambda)(v - \lambda^2 + 2v\lambda) \\ &2(v + \lambda^2) \cdot c = v\lambda(v + 5) \\ &2v\lambda \cdot r = v - \lambda^2,\qquad 2s = v + 3\end{aligned}$$

*Then $1 + ab = r^2$, $1 + ac = s^2$, $F_2 = 0$, and with*

$$\begin{aligned}&2\lambda(v + 1)(v + \lambda^2) \cdot z = v^3\lambda^2 - v\lambda^4 + 4v^2\lambda^2 - 2\lambda^4 - v^3 + 5v\lambda^2 - 2v^2 + 4\lambda^2 \\ &2v\lambda^3(v + 1)^3 \cdot e = (v + 2)(v + \lambda^2)\bigl((v + 2)(\lambda^4 + v^2) - 2v^2\lambda^2\bigr) \\ &2v\lambda^2(v + 1) \cdot y = -\bigl((v + 2)(\lambda^4 + v^2) + 2v\lambda^2\bigr)\end{aligned}$$

*one has $z^2 = 1 + d_+d_-$, the stated formula for $e$, $y^2 = 1 + ea$, and $y = L$. Let $\varphi = 1 + bc$ and $t^2 = \varphi$. Then the octad lies in $\mathbb{Q}(v, \lambda)(t)$, the members $a, b, c, e$ are fixed and the pairs $d_+, d_-$ and $f_+, f_-$ are exchanged by $t \mapsto -t$, and twenty-two of the twenty-eight elements $1 + xy$, for $x, y$ in the octad, are squares in that field:*

- *the three pairs of the seed;*
- *$d_+$ and $d_-$ with each of $a, b, c$;*
- *the pair $d_+, d_-$;*
- *$e$ with $b$, $d_+$, $d_-$ and $a$;*
- *$f_+$ with $e, a, d_+, b$, and $f_-$ with $e, a, d_-, b$.*

*Proof.* The first three items are the Lemma. The fourth and fifth are Theorem 1 together with $y^2 = 1 + ea$. For the last, $\{e, a, d_+, b\}$ is a Diophantine quadruple in the field, $\Pi$ is the product of its six roots, and the Lemma gives the four squares for $f_+$; apply $t \mapsto -t$ for $f_-$. $\square$

The six remaining pairs are

$$\{f_-, d_+\},\quad \{f_+, d_-\},\quad \{f_+, f_-\},\quad \{e, c\},\quad \{f_+, c\},\quad \{f_-, c\}.$$

For rational $v$ and $\lambda$ outside the zeros of the denominators and of $m - 1$, this gives eight numbers in $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{\varphi})$, four of them rational, with twenty-two square conditions.

## 5. Two curves

**Theorem 4.** *On the surface of Theorem 3:*

*(i) On the curve $v(\lambda^2 + 2) + 3\lambda^2 = 0$ the elements $1 + f_-d_+$ and $1 + f_+d_-$ are squares in $\mathbb{Q}(\lambda)(t)$. Here the seed $(a, b, c)$ is the triple $(x_2, x_5, x_9)$ of [1, 2] at the parameter $2/\lambda$, and $\varphi$ is a square times the quartic of [1].*

*(ii) On the curve $v(2\lambda + 1) + \lambda(\lambda + 2) = 0$,*

$$1 + f_+ f_- = \left(\frac{\lambda^2 - 6\lambda - 4}{4(\lambda^2 - 1)}\right)^{2},$$

*and $4(\lambda^2 - 1)^2\varphi = 4\lambda^4 - 2\lambda^3 + 7\lambda^2 + 18\lambda + 9$.*

On the first curve the seven members without $c$ satisfy twenty of their twenty-one conditions; the missing one is the pair $f_+, f_-$, the octic condition of [1] studied in [8, 9]. On the second curve the same seven members satisfy nineteen: the pair $f_+, f_-$ holds, and the two pairs of the first curve do not hold identically.

## 6. The intersection

The formulas of Theorem 3 define an octad on the *domain*

$$v\,\lambda\,(v + 1)(v + \lambda^2) \ne 0,\qquad m \ne 1.$$

Write $C_K$ and $C_F$ for the plane curves of Theorem 4 (i) and (ii).

**Theorem 5.** *The plane curves $C_K$ and $C_F$ meet exactly at the points with*

$$\lambda\,(\lambda - 4)(\lambda - 1)(\lambda + 1) = 0,$$

*namely $(v, \lambda) = (0, 0)$, $(-1, 1)$, $(-1, -1)$ and the point with $\lambda = 4$, $3v + 8 = 0$. The first three lie outside the domain: there $v\lambda = 0$ or $v + 1 = 0$, and the formulas define no octad. The fourth lies on the locus where $z^2 = 1$, so that $d_+d_- = 0$ and the seed is a regular triple.*

*Proof.* On $C_K$ one has $v(\lambda^2 + 2) = -3\lambda^2$ and on $C_F$ one has $v(2\lambda + 1) = -\lambda(\lambda + 2)$. Eliminating $v$ gives $\lambda\bigl((\lambda + 2)(\lambda^2 + 2) - 3\lambda(2\lambda + 1)\bigr) = 0$, and the second factor is $(\lambda - 4)(\lambda - 1)(\lambda + 1)$. Each root gives one value of $v$, and the values at $\lambda = 4$ are read off the formulas. $\square$

**Corollary.** *No common point of $C_K$ and $C_F$ gives an octad with eight distinct non-zero members.*

This is a statement about the intersection only. Theorem 4 shows that certain elements are squares on $C_K$ and on $C_F$; it does not show that they are squares only there. In particular we do not know whether $1 + f_+f_-$ is a square at some point of $C_K$ other than the common points, whether $1 + f_-d_+$ and $1 + f_+d_-$ are squares at some point of $C_F$, or whether the three conditions hold together at a point of the surface on neither curve. Neither curve is excluded as a source of septuples.

**Question.** *Is there a rational point $(v, \lambda)$ of the domain, with eight distinct non-zero members, at which $1 + f_-d_+$, $1 + f_+d_-$ and $1 + f_+f_-$ are all squares in $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{\varphi})$?*

Such a point gives a Diophantine septuple $\{a, b, d_+, d_-, e, f_+, f_-\}$ over $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{\varphi})$; it is a rational Diophantine septuple exactly when moreover $\varphi \in \mathbb{Q}^2$.

## Verification

The supplement contains a certificate in pure Python with no external packages; `verify_all.py` runs it in about two seconds. It implements exact arithmetic for multivariate polynomials, for rational functions of one variable and for the quadratic extension $\mathbb{Q}(\lambda)(t)$, and verifies as identities: the Lemma; Theorem 1; the factorization of Theorem 2; every formula of Theorem 3, including $y = L$; both parts of Theorem 4, with the identification of the seed with $(x_2, x_5, x_9)$ of [1, 2]; and the elimination of Theorem 5. It also evaluates the octad at one rational parameter on each curve and checks that the eight members are distinct and non-zero there, so they are distinct and non-zero as functions, and it checks at one point of the second curve that $1 + f_-d_+$ is not a square, so that pair does not hold identically there. The certificate verifies identities; it does not search for points, classifies no square specializations, and proves no non-existence statement.
