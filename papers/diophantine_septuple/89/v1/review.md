# Accept

The manuscript gives a sound and useful partial advance on the supplied septuple problem. Its main result is an explicit infinite construction for the signed system
\[
1-xy\in\pm\mathbb Q^2,
\]
from simultaneous Pythagorean--Eisenstein pairs, together with exact formulas for the induced elliptic curve, the order-four point, the translation point, and the complete six-minus-sign pattern. The construction is materially connected to the target problem: multiplying the entries by $i$ gives Diophantine septuples over $\mathbb Q(i)$, and the manuscript correctly identifies the displayed Paper 21 example as a member of this construction after a strong-member reflection.

The algebraic core is convincing. The identities defining the base triple and the curve follow directly from the two hypotheses on $(a,c)$. The torsion argument, the exclusion of a rational point of order eight using the fourth-power obstruction, and the non-torsion argument for $R$ establish the stated group-theoretic setup. The proof of the translation identities is sufficiently explicit, with the only long expansion isolated in the supplement. I inspected the supplement before execution; it is transparent exact-arithmetic code with no unsafe behavior. Running `python3 input/supplement/verify_pe.py` passed all listed checks for the three supplied pairs, the displayed $(3,5)$ layer, the reflection to Paper 21, the sign count, the finite layer-separation test, the descent diagnostics, and the stated bounded pair search. Running `python3 identA.py` also verified both signs of identity (A) as polynomial identities in the parametrized Eisenstein conic. These computations are finite consistency checks; the general conclusions rely on the written algebraic proofs, as they should.

The infinitude argument is appropriately scoped. The quartic $k^2=3m^4+n^4$ has a rational point and is identified with a genus-one torsor whose Jacobian is $Y^2=X^3-12X$. The displayed rational points exceed the possible torsion cardinality, so the Jacobian has positive rank and the family of pairs is infinite. For each pair, the proof that all but finitely many integers $m$ give distinct nonzero entries is adequate, using that $R$ has infinite order and that equality of abscissas means equality up to sign on the elliptic curve. The result does not meet the closed-ended solve definition: it neither produces seven rational entries with all $1+xy$ squares nor proves nonexistence, and the manuscript generally acknowledges this limitation.

One statement in the introduction and again in the final discussion is mathematically false and needs correction. If every sign in (1) is plus, then $1-xy$ is a square, which is a $D(-1)$-type condition; replacing every member by its negative leaves $xy$ unchanged and cannot convert it to $1+xy$ being a square. The rational original problem is related by the substitution $x=ir$ over $\mathbb Q(i)$, which the manuscript already states correctly. Thus phrases such as “after $x\mapsto-x$” and “the original problem is the all-plus case of (1)” should be replaced by the $i$-extension statement, and the claim that the six signs are obstructions for the original rational problem should be presented as an obstruction within this particular mixed/$\mathbb Q(i)$ construction. This correction changes the framing, but not the valid mixed-system theorem.

The novelty claim is reasonably distinguished from the closest work. Paper 21 already supplies the order-four translation mechanism and explicit septuples over $\mathbb Q(i)$; the present contribution is the Pythagorean--Eisenstein source, the rational mixed-sign family, and the uniform forced $K_4$ sign pattern. The classical sextuple literature, including Dujella--Kazalicki--Mikić--Szikszai [1], concerns the all-plus $1+xy$ system and does not duplicate these signed formulas. The direct search assertion for the range $0<a<c<3000$ would be more reproducible with its search code or a short certificate, but it is ancillary to the theorem.
