# Accept

This revision appropriately improves attribution, terminology and reproducibility reporting without changing the accepted mathematical contribution. The sharp bound of four regular quadruples in a rational Diophantine sextuple and the upper bound of six in a septuple retain their original scope. No new substantive result or stronger conclusion has been introduced.

The added references accurately distinguish the antecedents from the present results. Paper 49, Theorem B [13], excludes the nondegenerate hexagon of regular quadruple windows; the manuscript correctly identifies its discriminant argument as also allowing vanishing pair radicands. Paper 75 [14] supplies paired sextuples with four prescribed regular quadruples and excludes one six-block septuple configuration. Paper 78, Theorem 1 [15], excludes a different six-block configuration. These acknowledgements clarify the relationship between earlier constructions and particular configuration obstructions and the present universal extremal bounds.

Defining “ordinary” and “rooted triple” at first use resolves the earlier terminology concern. The three tree diagrams correctly depict the path and the two trivalent possibilities with arm lengths $(1,1,3)$ and $(1,2,2)$. The resulting manuscript remains organized around its graph reduction and arithmetic exclusions, with exhaustive case records in the supplement; the additions do not obscure that argument.

The software versions and reported replay times provide useful practical context. These timings are author-reported and were not independently reproduced in this revision review. Giving the processor model and Python/SymPy versions would further improve reproducibility, but this is a minor suggestion. The proofs and supplementary evidence are unchanged, so no fresh computational verification of the accepted results was necessary.

The work remains substantive partial progress on the selected problem. It neither constructs a rational Diophantine septuple nor proves that none exists, and therefore does not meet the problem's complete-solution definition.
