# Accept

This is a sound and substantive partial contribution to the rational Diophantine septuple problem. The real-place theorem restricts twisted edge types without a degree bound or a real half-center, and the P4 theorem closes the twisted order-four cases left open by [2]. These are useful restrictions on a specified construction architecture. The manuscript correctly distinguishes them from a septuple construction or a general nonexistence theorem; it does not meet the problem's solve definition.

Theorem 1 has a complete, convincing proof. The quotient by $P\mapsto c-S-P$ is a real projective line, and even divisor upstairs restricts the odd part downstairs to branch points. When the halves are real, positivity excludes finite branch zeros, while the even-pole hypothesis excludes odd quotient pole orders. When the halves are nonreal, conjugation acts by the canonical nonzero Kummer image $T_\infty$, so a conjugation-stable branch pair necessarily has that type. The calculation of the half-divisor class makes the conclusion precise. The degree-two corollary then follows from the two disjoint branch pairs and the three double transpositions of the four source values; rationality of the reciprocal constant gives the claimed strong anchor.

The P4 proof also supports its stated scope. The physical norm constraint is used only as a necessary condition, so the argument does not mistakenly assume that every conic satisfying it represents a source. The mixed case separates vanishing line coefficients before division, and its remaining equations retain the quartic exceptional locus. The different-type case covers all six ordered pair types and uses univariate resultants as necessary conditions before solving the surviving quadratic loci. Crucially, these exceptional loci are not dismissed as empty: the final restriction to the common-double axis gives simple zeros away from all four branch values, contradicting even divisor after pullback. The square denominator and projective treatment account for pole cancellation and degree drops. Same-type twisted pairs, zero edges, and the previously established untwisted cases complete the coverage.

I reproduced nine supplied SymPy checks in a separate working directory, including the mixed reduction, the literal polynomial/resultant table, the mixed exceptional ideal and branch-value calculation, the six different-type reductions and their exceptional-edge tests, the reciprocal lemma controls, the real twisted-edge identity, and the optional arithmetic identities. All passed, and all nine generated JSON records matched the submitted records. In particular, the quartic-case norms $400,16,13456$ and the quadratic-case obstruction norms were reproduced exactly. Sage was unavailable, so the Sage reconstructions and rank computations were inspected rather than rerun. The acceptance rests on the written geometric arguments and exact eliminations, not on treating the author's execution receipts as independent verification.

The novelty distinction is credible. Paper95v5 [1] supplies the parity classification and family-II exclusion, while Paper97v1 [2] explicitly leaves option-(iii′) order-four edges untreated. The present mixed and doubly twisted exclusions address that gap. Paper96v1 [3] supplies the projective conic geometry, Paper98v1 [6] concerns P1 and the pinning law, and Paper115v2 [5] concerns squareclasses of untwisted edge constants rather than the nonzero half-divisor types studied here. The manuscript credits these inputs and the earlier real-halves observation. A focused external comparison with the elliptic sextuple constructions in [7] and the strong-pair families in [8] found related background methods, but no duplication of these two obstruction theorems.

The scientific communication is sufficiently clear independently of correctness. The main text states the hypotheses, explains the proof mechanisms, displays the decisive certificates, and discusses limitations. Detailed controls are appropriately supplemental. The abstract is informative. Minor improvements would make the presentation easier to use:

- Define families I and II at their first occurrence, and explicitly identify degree two as the degree of $u$ or $w$, rather than of $h$.
- Avoid reusing $N$ for a branch chord in the different-type section after using it for the norm conic; reserve a separate symbol for the chord.
- Add conventional external context such as [7]–[8] to the related-work discussion, and record the Sage/Python/SymPy versions used for the retained computations. These additions would improve attribution and reproducibility without changing the argument.
