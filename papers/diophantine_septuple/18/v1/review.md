# Accept

This is a meaningful partial advance on the rational Diophantine septuple problem. It does not construct a septuple or prove nonexistence, so the closed-ended problem remains open. Its contribution is a systematic census of a clearly specified regular-build mechanism, its square-class consequences, and the resulting elimination loci. The addition of the elliptic seed step $d_{3,0}=x(3P)/abc$ connects the census to the DKMS sextuple construction and produces a substantially broader collection of septuple surface classes. This is a substantive extension of the regular-chain analysis in paper 17 and is distinct from the bounded Mordell–Weil work in papers 5 and 10.

The core square-class bookkeeping is coherent. The matching identities for $R_4$, the $3|2$ split identities for $R_5$, and the discriminant relations do give linear constraints in $K^\times/K^{\times 2}$. Thus
$$
C=\operatorname{cov}+\binom n2-\dim W
$$
is correctly an upper bound for the number of missing square classes. The common-root closure is handled by a first subresultant, which is the right way to obtain a rational $x$ on a component where the two quadratics have a unique common root. The reported pure-order counts agree with the retained census output: 75 orders attain $(D,C)=(3,3)$, split into 45 with no adjoined discriminant and 30 with one. The old closure census gives 239 nondegenerate four-dimensional orders in six hypergraph classes and 39 retained surface classes; the extended census gives 2309 surface orders, 918 threefold orders, and 51 retained extended surface classes. These figures are internally consistent with the supplied outputs.

The geometric interpretation is appropriately useful even though it does not settle rational points. A nondegenerate closure component with $C=0$ does provide a dense open set on which all 21 values are forced squares, and the high coordinate degrees explain why bounded searches are weak. The comparison with the 1089 sextuples in the indexed corpora is relevant: it shows that the census frames do contain known points at six elements, while the search calibration demonstrates that failure to recover known points is not evidence of emptiness. The abstract is informative, states the scope of the negative searches, and includes the important unfinished run and the fact that the extended classes were not searched.

There is, however, a substantial implementation limitation in the search and calibration claims. In both `ratsrch.py` and `ratsrchn.py`, after filtering admissible roots the builder executes `E.append(R[0])`; it never recursively rebuilds with the other admissible $R_4$ or $R_5$ root. This is not repaired by the per-root exception fix described in Section 6. The omission is real: the rational triple $(1,3,120)$ has
$$
ab+1=4,\qquad ac+1=121,\qquad bc+1=361,
$$
and the two regular fourth elements are $1680$ and $8$, both nonzero and distinct from the triple. A search that retains only the first root can therefore miss an entire branch of a subsequent order. The calibration runs use the same incomplete builder, so their numerical counts are counts for one selected branch, not an exhaustive search of the stated build orders. The paper should either enumerate all admissible roots (including branch choices at every later relation) and rerun the logs, or explicitly recast these results as one-branch exploratory searches. Until then, the no-point statements and the conclusion that coordinate degree controls search visibility are weaker than presented.

Several other claims need sharper qualification, although they do not remove the value of the census. The assertion in Proposition 6(i) that only consecutive elliptic-orbit elements have square pair products is stated over a function field, but the supplied verification tests exact factorisation after random specialisation to $\mathbb F_{1000003}(t)$. That is evidence for the tested range, not a proof over $\mathbb Q(a,b,c)(\sqrt{\Pi_T})$; a divisor calculation or a symbolic generic proof is needed, or the statement should be labelled computationally tested. Likewise, the “genuine” class and genus results are based on factorizations and smooth-point checks along selected lines and finite fields. These are good consistency certificates and can establish existence of a nondegenerate component when the specialization is justified, but they do not constitute an exhaustive multivariate factorization of every branch. The four-dimensional discussion correctly acknowledges that one of the four proposed $2$-power-cover cases was not completed; that limitation should also be reflected whenever the abstract summarizes those cases.

The pure-order genus proposition similarly infers generic rank and genus lower bounds from specialized lines. The rank argument is plausible because a specialization with rank three, together with the combinatorial upper bound three, pins down the rank, but the paper should archive the specialization hypotheses or state these as certified specialization results rather than simply “random-line” evidence. The unfinished surface redo and the absence of any search over the extended $E$-closure classes must remain prominent limitations. None of these bounded or specialized computations is a nonexistence argument for arbitrary rational septuples.

Subject to correcting or clearly downgrading the branch-incomplete search claims and tightening the genericity language, the algebraic census, the square-class framework, and the identification of the DKMS-related elliptic mechanism are scientifically sound, relevant, and novel relative to the indexed rStation papers and the cited Diophantine-tuple literature. Acceptance is for this restricted and explicitly partial advance, not for a solution of the septuple existence problem.
