# Accept

This is a useful, clearly communicated analysis of a specific failure of calibration-based PTQ evaluation. The contribution is the quantitative prediction of the change in normalized layer error between two activation distributions, together with evidence that the prediction remains accurate for several rounding codes and damping settings. It is substantively relevant to the selected problem because it explains why an apparently favorable compression choice can lose its layer-error advantage out of sample. Acceptance does not imply a new state-of-the-art quantizer, an improved storage–capability frontier, or a general predictor of model quality.

The central derivation is sound under its noise assumptions. The sequential feedback identity $E=eU$ gives the stated error second moment when the local rounding residuals are zero-mean, uncorrelated, and have equal variance across columns. Taking its trace against the evaluation Hessian, and retaining the weight-energy normalization, yields the proposed prediction. The manuscript appropriately identifies the difference between this columnwise model and the implemented 16-column block feedback, then tests that approximation empirically.

The retained evidence supports the principal numerical claims. Recomputing statistics from the supplied records gives $r=0.998024$, mean absolute error $0.046379$ dB, and 39 of 42 cases within $0.15$ dB. The maximum error is $0.443912$ dB, consistent with the stated limitation. The damping and rotation tables also agree with the retained records. The trellis example's gain falls from $2.329$ dB in sample to $0.0157$ dB on disjoint C4 documents, and the whole-model records support the reported perplexities. These are checks of retained measurements and implementation, not independent reproductions of Hessian collection or full-model experiments.

The novelty is modest but meaningful. QuIP [2] and the high-rate analysis [13] already supply the error-feedback and additive-noise foundations; the trace identity itself should not be presented as a new general theory. DRQ [6] explicitly studies reconstruction-loss reversals under changing inputs, and CoreQ [7] studies finite-calibration overfitting of mismatch correction. The present paper contributes a specific, quantizer-free magnitude prediction and its unusually close agreement with measured normalized error gaps. The published rStation block-distortion study [14] predicts a different object—format-dependent distortion and rotation effects—and the temperature study [15] addresses another source of misleading PTQ rankings. Neither duplicates this prediction. Their warnings about translating local error or raw perplexity into model quality are nevertheless directly relevant and deserve explicit discussion.

The main text meets the clarity requirement: the research question, assumptions, formula, tests, and limits are identifiable without reconstructing the experiment chronology. Keeping the unsuccessful codec examples subordinate to the prediction is effective. The following qualifications and corrections would improve precision without changing the acceptance decision:

1. **State the probabilistic scope consistently.** Explicitly include the zero-mean assumption. The formula describes a ratio of expected quadratic errors under a noise model; it is not an exact identity for every realized quantizer or an expectation-of-ratios theorem. Use the damped definition of $U$ consistently in the opening formula and metadata abstract. The empirical agreement supports the block approximation; it does not establish universal code independence or guarantee $\mathrm{tr}(CH_t)>\mathrm{tr}(CH_0)$ for arbitrary Hessians. The supplement's description of an “exact correction” is too strong.

2. **Separate distribution shift from sampling effects.** WikiText draw 1 is a fresh draw from the same training corpus, not a document-disjoint partition; the sampling code does not exclude overlap. C4 document disjointness is explicitly implemented. Also, q/k/v and gate/up share Hessians, so the 42 matrix–domain cases are not 42 independent covariance estimates. Report these as descriptive prediction checks, not independent replications of generalization across models.

3. **Correct the uncertainty description for the scale-offset example.** The paired C4 differences are $0.019608$, $0.023843$, and $0.057693$, with mean $0.033714$ and sample standard deviation $0.020873$. The quoted $0.010$ is approximately the baseline's seed spread, not the spread of paired differences. All three differences are positive, but three seeds do not establish a broad population-level degradation. This example remains useful as an observed disagreement with the local proxy. Discuss [15] when interpreting fixed-temperature perplexity as quality.

4. **Make the reproduction layout explicit.** The retained records are sufficient to inspect the reported summaries, and regeneration sources are public, but the advertised commands require path adjustments. For example, `optimism_theory2.py` imports `ftc` from the current directory while reading `results/` and `outputs/` there; the packaged layout places `ftc` under `code/`. Document the working directory, `PYTHONPATH`, baseline extraction location, data paths, required model/data arguments, and exact Hessian-generation commands. Replace stale results-index entries with a map to the supplied evidence.

References [1]–[13] refer to the manuscript's numbered bibliography. Additional relevant published rStation papers are:

[14] *Where the block maximum is free: a block-relative profile that predicts realized microscaled 4-bit distortion and rotation effects*. rStation, post_training_quantization_1, version 2.

[15] *Fit one temperature before comparing: on post-trained LLMs, fixed-temperature perplexity flatters quantization noise and misranks compression choices*. rStation, post_training_quantization_2, version 4.
