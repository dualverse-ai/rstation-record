# Residual Characters of Odd-Degree Fermat Fourfolds

**Hongmin Li** (Institute of Science Tokyo; The University of Tokyo)

## 1. Introduction

Write
$$
 X_m^n=\{x_0^m+\cdots+x_{n+1}^m=0\}\subset\mathbf P^{n+1}_{\mathbf C}.
$$
All cycle groups and cohomology groups in this paper have rational
coefficients unless another coefficient field is specified.

**Theorem A (positive residual classification).** For every odd
$m\ge3$, the orbits of six-entry Hodge characters outside
$\mathrm{Dec}\cup\mathrm{Std}_5$ are precisely the inflations
of the six orbits in (3.1) whose conductors divide $m$.
Equivalently, the primitive residual orbits consist of one at 33,
two at 39, one at 45 and two at 105.

Here $\mathrm{Dec}$ means an inverse pair or a contraction of
two surface Hodge quadruples, and $\mathrm{Std}_5$ is the exact
5-standard multiset; their definitions are given in Sections 2 and 7.
Section 7.8 proves Theorem A without computer enumeration
(Proposition 7.21). The residual census of Section 4 and the larger
finite checks through 2001 (Corollary 7.13) are independent
verifications of the classification, not inputs to its proof.

These predicates describe two direct constructions at the given degree:
contraction from surfaces and Aoki's single standard cycle. The theorem
identifies exactly when an additional operation is needed beyond these
constructions. Section 3 supplies it by a curve-product join, stabilization
by inverse pairs, or passage to degree 66. In particular,
$$
 |\mathcal R(m)|=\mathbf1_{33\mid m}+2\mathbf1_{39\mid m}
                   +\mathbf1_{45\mid m}+2\mathbf1_{105\mid m}.
$$
This counts orbits for the fixed positive criteria; it is not a dimension
formula for Aoki's integral gap group.

**Corollary 1.1 (algebraicity; compare [8] for $m\le199$ and [5]).** For every odd integer
$m\ge3$, the map
$$
 CH^2(X_m^4)_{\mathbf Q}\longrightarrow
 H^4(X_m^4,\mathbf Q)\cap H^{2,2}(X_m^4)
$$
is surjective. Consequently $X_m^4$ satisfies the rational Hodge
conjecture in every codimension.

The non-middle cohomology of a smooth hypersurface is generated by
hyperplane classes. Thus only the displayed map requires proof.
The corollary follows from Theorem A and Sections 2--3: the middle
ambient class $h^2$ is algebraic, the primitive Hodge classes are
spanned by the rational blocks of Hodge sextuples (Section 2), the two
positive criteria and each of the six seed blocks are algebraic, and Lemma 2.1
preserves algebraicity under inflation. It concerns individual
fourfolds and does not establish the conjecture for their arbitrary powers.

The following subfamily results organize the proof of Theorem A. Their
algebraicity conclusions are also covered by [11] and [5].

**Theorem 1.2.** The rational Hodge conjecture also holds for $X_{pq}^4$
whenever $p\ne q$ are odd primes, with no upper bound on $pq$.
It is proved in Section 7.

Theorem 7.3 and Proposition 7.4 further establish the exact positive
residual classification in squarefree semiprime and prime-power degree.
Theorem 7.6 adds every degree coprime to six, without a size bound.

**Theorem 1.3.** Let $q\ge7$ be prime and $a,b\ge1$.
The rational Hodge conjecture holds for $X_{3^a q^b}^4$.
Moreover its residual orbits outside $\mathrm{Dec}\cup\mathrm{Std}_5$
are exactly the inflations of the degree-33 seed if $q=11$,
of the two degree-39 seeds if $q=13$, and none otherwise.

Section 7.4 proves the positive classification, including both prime-power
depths, directly from primitive Fourier nonvanishing and the six-entry
length bound.

**Theorem 1.4.** The exact six-seed residual equality holds for every
odd degree with at most two distinct prime divisors and for every odd
degree prime to five. With arbitrary
prime support, a primitive residual conductor divisible by nine must be
45 and has the single degree-45 residual orbit.

Sections 7.5--7.7 prove these infinite assertions by classifying
small split functions and controlling the lower-order error. In particular
the arbitrary-support assertion concerns primitive conductors; inflated
residuals may occur at any odd multiple of a seed degree.
Section 7.8 completes the unrestricted classification of Theorem A.

**Contributions.** (i) the exact positive classification of residual characters
at every odd conductor (Theorem A and Theorem 7.20), whose six
primitive orbits were first isolated in [8] through degree 199,
so that the new content is completeness at every odd degree, by a
proof that uses no enumeration; (ii) an independent computer check
combining the exhaustive residual
census through degree 599 with an enumeration of the 103
remaining degrees divisible by 3 through 2001 (Section 4 and Corollary 7.13);
(iii) the explicit family on which the
tensor identity of Kang's product argument fails (Section 6).

**Prior and parallel work.** Da Silva [6, Propositions 3.1 and 3.4
in the published version] proved the conjecture for fourfolds of degree
at most 100 prime to six and exhibited a degree-33 Hodge class outside
the quasi-decomposable and standard criteria considered there.
The earlier arXiv version [6, v2, Theorem 3.3] states the
algebraicity conclusion for every degree prime to six; that unbounded
statement is absent from the published version. Neither version classifies the residual set for the two positive predicates fixed here.
Jumagulov [8] proved the conjecture for
odd degrees up to 199 and gave the constructions for the six seeds
(Theorems 1.2--1.4 there); we do not claim these constructions. His
Theorem 1.5 already gives a census through 199. At odd degree his
decomposable and quasi-decomposable predicates together coincide with
our $\mathrm{Dec}$: a splitting of $a\sqcup\{k,-k\}$ into two
Hodge quadruples [8, Lemma 6.1(ii)] either separates $k,-k$, which
is a surface contraction with distinguished sum $k\ne0$, or leaves an
inverse pair in $a$. His standard sextuples are exactly
$\mathrm{Std}_5$ [8, Proposition 1.9]. His thirteen orbits beyond
these criteria, namely the six seeds and their inflations at 99, 117,
135, 165 and 195, therefore agree with Theorem A through degree 199.
In particular the six primitive orbits of (3.1) were first isolated
in [8]; the contribution here is the proof that the list is complete
at every odd degree. The degree-45 and one degree-105
presentation are instances of Aoki's wider standard lattice calculus
[3, Lemma 4.1(iii), p. 185]; [8, Theorem 1.3] gives the explicit identities
used below. Peterson [13, Theorem 1.1(C)] gives a proof independent of
Kang for all dimensions at degrees $2^a3^b5^c7^d$ not divisible by
420, including 45 and 105. These are prior algebraicity results, not
the positive classification for the predicates fixed here.

An earlier manuscript of the author, dated 7 September 2026 and posted
on 8 September 2026 [11], stated the all-odd-degree conjecture as a theorem, with a compressed
conductor-descent argument.
Section 1.1 of [11] also records the failure of
the tensor identity of [9, Proposition 2.2(iv)] on an
$H^1\otimes H^1$ diagonal class. Bhattacharjee [5]
gives a proof of the Hodge conjecture for $X_m^4$ at every odd degree
by descent along additive-order profiles with terminal characters in degrees 21, 33 and 39; the all-odd
statement first appears in its version 1.2.0 of 10 October 2026, and
its Remark 3.21 records the same diagonal-class failure.

For $\gcd(m,6)=1$ the dichotomy between an inverse pair and an exact
5-standard tuple is [5, Theorem 3.9(ii)], stated there as new when
$5\mid m$ and machine-checked in Lean; Theorem 7.6 reproves it. For
$3\mid m$, [5] abandons classification for an induction on moves
(Theorem 3.27: a direct or lowering move, or an exceptional orbit at
21, 33 or 39). The degree-21 orbit lies in $\mathrm{Dec}$; the residual
set proved here also contains the second degree-39 orbit and the orbits at
45 and 105, which are not terminal in [5].

Theorem 1.2, the cases of Theorem 1.3,
the prime-to-six case (Theorem 7.6) and the all-dimensional consequences
for the supports $\{3,11\}$ and $\{3,13\}$ (Section 5) are also
covered by [5]; the present paper supplies their proofs from the
stated classical inputs. The parity detectors of Section 5 are
[5, Lemma 4.4], and the short split-function method used in
Section 7 is also present in [5].

Favero and Kelly [7, Theorem 1.8 and Theorem 4.8] give a second,
independent proof of the Hodge conjecture for the degree-33 Fermat
fourfold; the case $m=33$ is already contained in [8, Theorem 1.1],
posted earlier, which [7] does not cite. They
pull back classes of cubic scrolls on a cubic fourfold with a
symplectic automorphism of order eleven along a monomial rational map
[7, (4.3)--(4.4)], and detect the degree-33 character with their
residue formula for the primitive Chern character. In the printed
equation [7, (4.1)] the last monomial reads $x_3^3$; the map
[7, (4.4)], which sets $y_5=x_5^{11}$, requires $x_5^3$. Their method
differs from ours: the degree-33 seed here is made algebraic by the
level-66 contraction of [8, Theorem 1.4] (Section 3), and the
classification of Theorem A concerns every odd degree rather than one
character.

Section 6 realizes the tensor-identity failure on the Fermat product
$X_m^1\times X_m^3$ used in [10, (3.3)]. We do not claim the
general diagonal-class counterexample as new.

The geometric ingredients are classical. Shioda [16, Theorem II] gives
the inductive cohomological structure and its algebraic correspondences;
Aoki [2, Theorem 2-1] supplies the standard cycles. The six exceptional
orbits used here, their split and two-pair presentations, and their
level-66 construction already occur in Jumagulov [8, Theorems
1.2--1.4], whose Theorem 1.5 covers all odd degrees at most 199. Our
finite censuses use separately written enumerators and check the
classification at every odd degree through 599 and at 103 further
degrees through 2001; no proof in this paper depends on them.

Kang [10, Corollary 3.2] states the stronger generalized Hodge conjecture
in dimensions three and four in every degree. The proof uses a product
estimate from [9, Proposition 2.2(iv)]. Section 6 identifies a false
tensor identity in its proof; this does not disprove the product estimate
or Kang's final conclusions. Miranda, Movasati, Rufino and Villaflor
[12, Theorem 4.4] state an all-dimensional result for degrees
$d<65$, excluding $44,51,52$. Their Proposition 4.3 explicitly uses
[10, Corollary 3.2]. Corollary 1.1 uses neither of these inputs.

## 2. Characters and algebraic correspondences

Put $U_m=(\mathbf Z/m)^\times$. For a tuple $a$ of nonzero residues
whose sum is zero modulo $m$, define
$$
 \operatorname{age}_m(ta)=\frac1m\sum_i[ta_i]_m,
 \qquad [x]_m\in\{1,\ldots,m-1\}.
$$
If $a$ has $n+2$ entries, its primitive character space
$V_m(a)\subset H^n(X_m^n,\mathbf C)$ is one-dimensional and has Hodge
type $(p,q)$ with $q=\operatorname{age}_m(a)-1$ and $p=n-q$. Its rational
coefficient-Galois orbit is denoted $M_m(a)$. For $n$ even, it is of type
$(n/2,n/2)$ precisely when
$$
 \operatorname{age}_m(ta)=(n+2)/2\quad\text{for all }t\in U_m.
 \tag{2.1}
$$
These assertions are [16, Theorem I, p. 176] and [2, p. 385].
The rational orbit projector belongs to $\mathbf Q[G_m]$, where
$G_m=\mu_m^{n+2}/\mu_m$. Deck transformations and coordinate
permutations are algebraic automorphisms. Therefore one nonzero
projection of an algebraic cycle onto $V_m(a)$, together with the
rational orbit projector and deck translates, generates $M_m(a)$
by rational algebraic classes. This uses multiplicity one of the
irreducible rational deck representation, not rationality of a single
complex eigenline. The cycles need not be defined over $\mathbf Q$.

### 2.1. Power maps and non-coprime standard parameters

**Lemma 2.1 (inflation and descent).** Let $m=kd$, $n\ge0$, and
$$
 \pi_k:X_m^n\longrightarrow X_d^n,\qquad [x_i]\longmapsto[x_i^k].
$$
This is a finite flat morphism of degree $k^{n+1}$, and
$$
 \pi_k^*:M_d(a)\xrightarrow{\sim}M_m(ka).
 \tag{2.2}
$$
The two rational character blocks are algebraic simultaneously.

*Proof.* On the dense coordinate torus the kernel is
$\mu_k^{n+2}/\mu_k$, of order $k^{n+1}$. The power map on projective
space is finite, hence its restriction is finite; its image is all of
$X_d^n$. Since the source is Cohen--Macaulay and the target is regular
of the same dimension, the finite map is flat. The projection formula
gives
$$
 \pi_{k,*}\pi_k^*=k^{n+1}\operatorname{id}.
 \tag{2.3}
$$
Equivariance sends the character $a$ to $ka$. Pullback is injective,
and both complex character spaces are one-dimensional. Moreover the
reduction map $U_m\to U_d$ is surjective: a unit modulo $d$ can be
lifted to avoid the additional primes dividing $m$, by the Chinese
remainder theorem. Thus the entire orbit is the image in (2.2).
Pullback preserves algebraic classes. Conversely, for a class in the
lower block, push an algebraic representative of its pullback forward
and divide by $k^{n+1}$. This proves both implications. $\square$

The same power-map equivalence is stated, in weighted notation, in
[12, Proposition 3.2]; the proof above supplies the implication used
here independently of that preprint.

For an odd prime $p\mid m$, put $d=m/p$. Aoki's standard tuple is
$$
 \sigma_{p,i}=(i,i+d,\ldots,i+(p-1)d,-pi),\qquad
 d/\gcd(i,d)>2.
 \tag{2.4}
$$
The definition and its admissibility condition are [2, p. 387].
Theorem 2-1 on p. 388 is stated with $\gcd(i,d)=1$.
Since $m$ is odd, $d$ is odd, so $d/\gcd(i,d)$ is odd and exceeds
2 exactly when $d\nmid i$, that is, when $pi\ne0\pmod m$. This is
the admissibility condition used below whenever a tuple is said to be
exactly $p$-standard.

**Lemma 2.2.** Every tuple (2.4) has an algebraic rational character block,
including the case $\gcd(i,d)>1$.

*Proof.* Put $g=\gcd(i,d)$, $m'=m/g$, $d'=d/g$, $i'=i/g$.
Then $m'=pd'$, $d'>2$, $\gcd(i',d')=1$, and
$$
 \sigma_{p,i}^{(m)}=g\sigma_{p,i'}^{(m')}.
 \tag{2.5}
$$
Apply [2, Theorem 2-1] at degree $m'$, then Lemma 2.1 in dimension
$p-1$. $\square$

Thus the non-coprime case is a proved reduction, not an extra hypothesis
silently attributed to Aoki's Theorem 2-1.

### 2.2. The split structure correspondence

We record the part of Shioda's construction that permits iteration on
arbitrary tensors. For a hypersurface of dimension $e$, the primitive
cohomological projector is algebraic; with $h$ the hyperplane class,
$$
 \Pi_e=\Delta_{X_m^e}-\frac1m\sum_{i=0}^e h^{e-i}\times h^i.
 \tag{2.6}
$$
It removes all ambient cohomology, including the ambient middle line
when $e$ is even.

**Lemma 2.3 (split join).** For any $a,b\ge0$, there are algebraic
correspondences inducing maps
$$
 J_{a,b}:H^a_{\rm prim}(X_m^a)\otimes H^b_{\rm prim}(X_m^b)
 \longrightarrow H^{a+b+2}_{\rm prim}(X_m^{a+b+2}),
 \qquad R_{a,b}J_{a,b}=\operatorname{id}.
 \tag{2.7}
$$
Their Hodge types are $(1,1)$ and $(-1,-1)$, respectively.
The character of the image is the concatenation of the two characters.
No Hodge-class assumption on either input is required.

*Proof.* In the notation of [16, (2.1), pp. 177--179], set
$r=a+1$, $s=b+1$, $N=a+b+2$, and
$$
 P=X_m^{a+1}\times X_m^{b+1},\qquad
 Y=X_m^a\times X_m^b,\qquad Z=\operatorname{Bl}_Y P.
$$
Let $j:E\hookrightarrow Z$, $q:E\to Y$ be the exceptional divisor
and its projective-line bundle. The morphism
$\psi:Z\to X_m^N$ factors as a quotient of degree $m$, followed
by the blowdown in Shioda's diagram. Define, on the indicated primitive
summands,
$$
 J=\Pi_N\psi_*j_*q^*,\qquad
 R=-\frac1m(\Pi_a\otimes\Pi_b)q_*j^*\psi^*\Pi_N.
 \tag{2.8}
$$
These maps are algebraic correspondences. We check their composition.

Write $\psi=\bar\psi\circ\rho$, where
$\rho:Z\to W=Z/\mu_m$ is the finite quotient and
$\bar\psi:W\to X_m^N$ is the blowup of the two disjoint smooth
centres $X_m^a$ and $X_m^b$. The smoothness of $W$ and this
factorization are [17, Lemma 1.4 and Theorem 1.7, pp. 100--102]
and [16, (2.1)]. In particular $E$ is the exceptional divisor
of $Z\to P$; its image is not contracted by $\bar\psi$.

Use the covering deck group
$\widetilde G=\mu_m^{a+2}\times\mu_m^{b+2}$, acting on the
first $a+2$ and $b+2$ coordinates of the two factors of $P$
and fixing their last coordinates. It commutes with the quotient
action and makes all maps equivariant; its action on the target
factors through $G_m$. Working with this covering group avoids
assuming that the target deck group itself lifts to $Z$.
For an input eigenvector $z$ of character $(\alpha;\beta)$,
set $x=j_*q^*z$. All entries of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are
nonzero. The quotient group fixes $Y$ and acts by the same scalar
on its two normal directions in $P$, so it fixes
$E=\mathbf P(N_{Y/P})$ pointwise. Hence
$$
 \rho^*\rho_*x=\sum_{g\in\mu_m}g^*x=mx.
$$
The blowup decomposition of $H^N(W)$ consists of
$\bar\psi^*H^N(X_m^N)$ and the exceptional cohomology over
the two centres. In every summand over $X_m^a$, the entire
second coordinate block acts trivially: the projective-bundle
classes are invariant. Every summand over $X_m^b$ similarly
has trivial first block. Thus neither exceptional part contains
the character $(\alpha;\beta)$, and
$$
 \bar\psi^*\bar\psi_*\rho_*x=\rho_*x,
 \qquad \psi^*\psi_*x=mx.
$$
Also $\psi_*x$ has the same all-nonzero character, so is primitive.

Finally the exceptional normal bundle is $\mathcal O_E(-1)$, so,
writing $\xi=c_1(\mathcal O_E(1))$,
$$
 q_*j^*j_*q^*z=q_*(-\xi q^*z)=-z.
$$
Substitution in (2.8) gives $RJ=\operatorname{id}$. The types follow
from the exceptional Gysin map and its retraction. The concatenation
rule is [16, (2.12)--(2.14)]. The split join itself was first
introduced, in homology, by Ran [14, Section 4, Proposition 4.6 and
Corollary 4.7]; see [16, Remark 3, p. 179]. The argument uses the full graded
blowup and quotient decompositions, so also applies when $N$ is odd.
$\square$

**Proposition 2.4 (Shioda's product theorem, with the iteration explicit).**
Let $r_1,\ldots,r_k\ge0$. If the Hodge conjecture holds for
$X_m^e$ for every even
$$
 e\le r_1+\cdots+r_k+2(k-1),
 \tag{2.9}
$$
then it holds for $\prod_iX_m^{r_i}$.

*Proof.* By Künneth, (2.6), and the algebraicity of hyperplane classes,
it suffices to treat a Hodge class in a tensor of primitive middle
cohomologies, indexed by a subset $I$ of the factors. Let $|I|=s$
and $w=\sum_{i\in I}r_i$. Such a Hodge tensor has $w$ even.
For $s=1$ the assertion is a hypothesis. For $s\ge2$, compose
$$
 J_{r_{i_1},r_{i_2}}\otimes\operatorname{id},\quad
 J_{r_{i_1}+r_{i_2}+2,r_{i_3}}\otimes\operatorname{id},\quad\ldots.
$$
This gives an algebraic split injection of type $(s-1,s-1)$ into
$H^{w+2(s-1)}_{\rm prim}(X_m^{w+2(s-1)})$. The reverse composition
of the $R$'s is an algebraic retraction. Intermediate tensors may
have arbitrary Hodge type; Lemma 2.3 applies to their entire cohomology.
Only the final image is asserted to be a Hodge class. Its ambient
dimension is even and satisfies (2.9), so the hypothesis makes it
algebraic. The retraction makes the original tensor algebraic.
Reinsert the omitted hyperplane factors to finish. $\square$

For even target dimension, [16, Theorem II(c), p. 178] already
states algebraicity in both directions on the second summand,
without a Hodge assumption on its input. One may use just that
case in Proposition 2.4: join odd-dimensional factors in pairs
first, then join the even-dimensional factors. The detailed
retraction above also applies to odd target dimension.

This proves the statement of [16, Theorem IV, p. 182] for all $k$,
including $k\ge3$. The argument does not assume that the first two
factors of a Hodge tensor already form a Hodge tensor.

### 2.3. Surface contractions, curve products and cancellation

Two elementary consequences of [16, Theorem II] will be used. If a
Hodge sextuple contains an inverse pair, deleting it leaves a surface
Hodge tuple, which is algebraic by Lefschetz $(1,1)$. Joining its
block with the corresponding point block gives the sextuple.

If a sextuple splits into triples $B,C$ of sums $k,-k\ne0$, and
$(B,-k)$, $(k,C)$ are surface Hodge tuples, it is algebraic by the
first summand of Theorem II, with $r=s=2$; this is exactly
[16, Corollary (ii), p. 179]. The distinguished last
coordinates match with opposite signs; the finite-group invariant
projector is algebraic. We call these two cases $\mathrm{Dec}$.

Write $\mathrm{Std}_5$ for the sextuples equal as multisets to
(2.4) with $p=5$ and its stated admissibility condition. In
particular this means one exact standard tuple, not an integral sum
of standard tuples. Repeated entries in all tuples are allowed.
Let $\mathcal H_6(m)$ be the set of Hodge sextuples and define
$$
 \mathcal R(m)=
 \bigl(\mathcal H_6(m)\smallsetminus
       (\mathrm{Dec}\cup\mathrm{Std}_5)\bigr)/(U_m\times S_6).
$$
The content is $g=\gcd(m,a_0,\ldots,a_5)$; the primitive conductor
is $m/g$, equivalently the least common multiple of the six
additive orders. A tuple is called primitive in the conductor sense
when $g=1$. This usage is distinct from primitive cohomology.
For $d\mid m$, inflation is $a\mapsto(m/d)a$. Thus Theorem A
asserts the exact equality
$$
 \mathcal R(m)=
 \{[(m/d)a]:(d,a)\text{ occurs in (3.1)},\ d\mid m\}.
$$

If instead the sums of the two triples vanish, the triples are curve
characters. The sextuple condition says that their diagonal Galois
orbit is of type $(1,1)$ on $X_m^1\times X_m^1$. Lefschetz
$(1,1)$ and $J_{1,1}$ make the sextuple algebraic. This is already
[16, Section 4, Lemma 1, p. 183]. In its printed proof the substitution
$r=s=1$ should read $r=s=2$ in the notation of Theorem II.

Concatenation preserves algebraicity by Lemma 2.3. Inverse pairs can
be cancelled by the Shioda--Ran cancellation rule recorded in [2, Theorem 1-4(ii), p. 388] (cf. [16, Section 4, Lemma 3, p. 183] and [14]). More generally, a known
algebraic Hodge factor can be cancelled: apply the algebraic join
retraction and contract the resulting product class against an
algebraic dual basis of that factor. The intersection form on its
primitive Hodge classes is nondegenerate by the Hodge--Riemann
relations, so the dual basis has rational algebraic classes. This is
[16, Section 4, Lemma 3, p. 183].

## 3. The six primitive residuals

The six orbits are represented by
$$
\begin{array}{c|l}
33&(1,4,16,22,25,31)\\
39&(1,7,16,22,34,37),\ (1,14,16,22,29,35)\\
45&(1,19,20,28,30,37)\\
105&(1,22,43,64,90,95),\ (3,24,50,66,85,87).
\end{array}
\tag{3.1}
$$
The arithmetic identities below are also checked directly over all
units by the accompanying independent Python verification script.

### 3.1. Degree 33

Put $w=(1,4,16,22,25,31)$, $a=2w$, at degree 66. Let
$$
 Q=(1,25,44,62),\quad S=(2,8,32,41,50,65),
$$
and let $\beta=(2,32,33,65)$, $\gamma=(8,33,41,50)$.
For every $t\in U_{66}$, the ages of $Q,\beta,\gamma$ equal two,
and those of $a,S$ equal three. The distinguished coordinates 33 of
$\beta$ and $\gamma$ are opposite modulo 66. Shioda's
Corollary (ii) applies at every degree, including even degree; it permits
a nonzero distinguished residue equal to its own negative, as here.
Their surface contraction therefore gives the algebraicity of $S$. The equality
of multisets
$$
 a\sqcup(1,65,25,41)=Q\sqcup S
 \tag{3.2}
$$
and inverse-pair cancellation give the algebraicity of $a$.
Lemma 2.1 descends it to $w$. This uses only the displayed surface
blocks, not the Hodge conjecture for every class at degree 66.
The same identity and construction are [8, Theorem 1.4].

### 3.2. Degree 39

The two sextuples have the splits
$$
 (1,16,22)\sqcup(7,34,37),\qquad
 (1,16,22)\sqcup(14,29,35).
 \tag{3.3}
$$
Each triple has sum zero modulo 39. Every simultaneous unit translate
has total age three, so the curve-product criterion in Section 2.3
applies. These are also the degree-39 applications of [8,
Theorem 1.2].

### 3.3. Degrees 45 and 105

At degree 45 the multiset identity is
$$
 (1,19,20,28,30,37)\sqcup(5,40,10,35)
 =(1,10,19,28,37,40)\sqcup(5,20,30,35).
 \tag{3.4}
$$
The first right-hand block is $\sigma_{5,1}$; the second is a surface
Hodge tuple (also $\sigma_{3,5}$). At degree 105 the identities are
$$
\begin{split}
 (3,24,50,66,85,87)\sqcup(15,90,45,60)
  &=(3,24,45,66,87,90)\sqcup(15,50,60,85),\\
 (1,22,43,64,90,95)\sqcup(85,20,100,5)
  &=(1,22,43,64,85,100)\sqcup(5,20,90,95).
\end{split}
\tag{3.5}
$$
The first blocks are $\sigma_{5,3}$ and $\sigma_{5,1}$, respectively.
The second blocks are surface Hodge tuples. Each left-hand padding
consists of two inverse pairs. Lemma 2.2, surface algebraicity,
concatenation, and pair cancellation prove all three assertions.
These two-pair presentations are included in [8, Theorem 1.3].

For clarity, the non-coprime standard parameters appearing in these
identities reduce as follows:
$$
 (m,p,i,g,m/g)=(45,3,5,5,9),\quad(105,5,3,3,35),\quad
 (105,3,15,5,21).
 \tag{3.6}
$$
In each reduced case $\gcd(i/g,(m/p)/g)=1$, exactly as required by
[2, Theorem 2-1].

## 4. Finite censuses

**Proposition 4.1 (computer census through degree 105).** At the 52 odd
degrees from 3 through 105 there are, in total, 57 pair-free Hodge
orbits. Of these, 15 are exactly 5-standard and 35 of the remaining
orbits admit a surface contraction. The seven residual orbits are the
six primitive orbits in (3.1) and the degree-33 inflation at 99.

*Proof.* Both independent enumerators described in the Appendix check
all 52 degrees, including those covered by Aoki's theorem. The orbit
normalization and exhaustive loop bounds are justified below. Each
candidate is checked at every unit; classification tests all ten
three-three splits with nonzero distinguished sum and all admissible
standard parameters. The implementations use different enumeration
algorithms and separate closure predicates. Rebuilding and running both
gives equal canonical orbit sets in each of the three disjoint categories
at every degree, with the totals just stated. Every raw candidate from

The direct implementation enumerates the divisor-normalized candidates by four nested

Its Python classification uses the definitions of Section 2.3, with

The content of a sextuple is $\gcd(m,a_0,\ldots,a_5)$. If this is
$g>1$, division by $g$ gives a Hodge sextuple at degree $m/g$,
by the surjectivity of the unit reduction map. It suffices to classify
content-one tuples and then use Lemma 2.1.

When $\gcd(m,30)=1$, [1, Theorem A, p. 24] says that every Hodge
sextuple is a union of inverse pairs: every prime divisor of $m$
exceeds six. Thus 159 of the 299 odd degrees between 3 and 599 require
no enumeration. Aoki's 1984 erratum, p. 572, replaces the statement
of Theorem B on p. 24; it does not change Theorem A or the character
nonvanishing and generation results used here.

For each of the remaining 140 degrees, the program
`primitive_residual.c` enumerates all content-one Hodge sextuples
outside $\mathrm{Dec}$ and the exact family (2.4) for $p=5$.
The enumeration has the following completeness proof. For any nonzero
coordinate $x$, put $g=\gcd(x,m)$. There is a unit $t$ such that
$tx=g\pmod m$: solve $t(x/g)=1\pmod{m/g}$, and use unit
surjectivity to lift $t$ modulo $m$. Therefore each orbit has a
representative with its first coordinate a proper positive divisor
$g$ of $m$. After fixing that coordinate, sort the remaining five.
Enumerate their first four entries, and recover the fifth from
$\sum_i a_i=3m$. The loop bounds impose only this sum and this sorting
condition, so no Hodge representative is excluded. For each candidate,
the program checks (2.1) at every unit. It then checks the precise
surface-splitting and standard criteria, rejects nonprimitive content,
and takes the integer lexicographic minimum under the unit action.

The full output consists of one completion row for each of these 140
degrees, with the six residual rows (3.1). The largest tested degree
with $3\mid m$ or $5\mid m$ is 597. The run used GCC 11.5.0 on
Linux, four processes, and approximately 107 seconds of elapsed time.
Its completion rows, the complete source, and a separate exact
arithmetic checker are supplied with this paper.

Inflation preserves and reflects $\mathrm{Dec}$. An inverse pair
divides to an inverse pair. For a surface split, its distinguished
residue is the sum of three original coordinates, so is divisible
by the content $g$; division gives the lower-degree split and
preserves every age equation. The forward direction is the same
calculation. Inflation also
preserves and reflects the exact 5-standard family. In the converse
direction, a standard progression of step $m/5$ all of whose
coordinates are divisible by $g$ requires $g\mid m/5$, so
$5\mid m/g$, and divides to a standard progression at degree
$m/g$. Consequently the full residual set through 599 consists
exactly of the 38 odd inflations of (3.1). In the following proof the
exhaustive input is the primitive census, not this count.

*Algebraicity through degree 599, independently of Theorem A.*
If $\gcd(m,30)=1$, every Hodge sextuple
is a union of inverse pairs [1, Theorem A], hence is generated
by linear subspaces [2, Theorem 1-1]. In every other degree,
reduce the content. A primitive
tuple is in $\mathrm{Dec}$, is a 5-standard tuple, or is in (3.1).

## 5. All dimensions and products for two prime-support families

**Theorem 5.1.** Fix $q\in\{11,13\}$. If
$m=3^a q^b\ge3$, $a,b\ge0$, then the rational Hodge conjecture
holds for $X_m^n$ in every dimension. It also holds for every finite
product $\prod_i X_{m_i}^{n_i}$ with all $m_i$ in this fixed
prime-support family.

*Proof.* For a tuple $T$ at degree $m$, write $\nu(T)=(n_x)_{x\ne0}$
for its vector of multiplicities. Let $B_m$ be Aoki's integral Hodge
group of such multiplicity vectors, and $S_m\subseteq B_m$ the subgroup
generated by inverse pairs and standards. The gap-group order in [4, Theorem 3.1,
p. 107], which [4] derives from Yamamoto's theorem and [1, Theorem D], gives $B_m/S_m\cong\mathbf Z/2$ at degrees 33 and 39.
Define the parity detectors on multiplicity vectors $n=(n_x)$,
$$
 \lambda_{33}(n)=n_1+n_{10}+n_{23}+n_{32},\qquad
 \lambda_{39}(n)=n_1+n_{14}+n_{25}+n_{38}\pmod2.
 \tag{5.1}
$$
Their support is the unit set
$D_{3q}=\{x\in U_{3q}:x\equiv\pm1\pmod q\}$, which satisfies
$-D_{3q}=D_{3q}$; hence every inverse pair meets $D_{3q}$ in $0$
or $2$ entries. In an admissible $\sigma_{3,i}$, the entry $-3i$
is a nonunit, and the progression $i,i+q,i+2q$ is constant modulo
$q$ while its residues modulo 3 run through $\mathbf Z/3\mathbf Z$;
it meets $D_{3q}$ in two entries if $i\equiv\pm1\pmod q$ and in
none otherwise. In an admissible $\sigma_{q,i}$, so $3\nmid i$,
the entry $-qi$ is a nonunit, and the progression
$i,i+3,\ldots,i+3(q-1)$ consists of units modulo 3 whose residues
modulo $q$ run through $\mathbf Z/q\mathbf Z$; it meets $D_{3q}$
exactly in its two entries congruent to $\pm1\pmod q$. Hence
$\lambda_{3q}$ vanishes on $S_{3q}$. The first degree-33 and first
degree-39 tuples in (3.1), $w_{33}=(1,4,16,22,25,31)$ and
$w_{39}=(1,7,16,22,34,37)$, meet $D_{3q}$ only in the entry 1, so
$\lambda_{3q}(\nu(w_{3q}))=1$. Thus the respective tuples generate the nontrivial integral
gap classes, and
$$
 B_{3q}=S_{3q}+\mathbf Z\nu(w_{3q}).
 \tag{5.2}
$$
Both $w_{3q}$ are algebraic by Section 3.
The detectors (5.1), their vanishing on $S_{3q}$ and the resulting
equality (5.2) are [5, Lemma 4.4 and Corollary 4.6], which are applied
there to the same two tuples in the proof of [5, Theorem A]. Since
$D_{3q}$ is the subgroup of units with square one, for an odd squarefree
semiprime degree $m'$ the underlying criterion, that a Hodge element lies
outside the subgroup $S_{m'}$ generated by inverse pairs and standard
elements exactly when some coset of $\{\pm1\}\times\{\pm1\}$
contains an odd number of its entries, is [15, Theorem A(a)], which
attributes it, in other terms, to Kubert. We include the argument only
in the notation used here.

For $m=3^a q^b$ with both exponents positive, [1, Theorem D,
p. 38] generates $B_m$, as a $U_m$-module, by standards, pairs,
and inflations of fixed $\xi_d\in B_d$, with $d\mid m$,
$\mu'(d)=1$. Unit translates preserve algebraicity by the algebraic
orbit projectors described in Section 2. At odd level, the modified
Möbius function is zero unless the divisor is squarefree, and on a
squarefree divisor it is $\mu'(d)=(-1)^{\#\{p:p\mid d\}}$
[1, (3.3), p. 32]. Indeed, writing $d=\prod_{p\mid d}p^{e_p}$,
the definition requires every $e_p=1$ for a nonzero value;
the convention is $\mu'(1)=1$. The same odd-level identification
with the usual Möbius function is stated in [3, p. 181].
The squarefree divisors of $3^a q^b$
are $1,3,q,3q$, so the only nontrivial one with
$\mu'(d)=1$ is $d=3q$. The divisor one contributes no
generator; equivalently, the same generation statement is
printed with $d>1$ in [3, Theorem 2.1, p. 181]. By (5.2), the fixed $\xi_{3q}$, whatever its particular
construction, is an integral combination of standards, pairs and
$w_{3q}$. Their inflations remain algebraic by Lemmas 2.1 and 2.2.
Indeed, at odd degree Aoki's standard admissibility $pi\ne0\pmod m$
[1, p. 36] is equivalent to $d/\gcd(i,d)>2$, since this
odd quotient differs from one exactly when $d\nmid i$.
For every inflation factor $k$, the multiset identity
$k\sigma_{p,i}^{(m)}=\sigma_{p,ki}^{(km)}$ preserves that
admissibility. Thus higher prime powers introduce no extra standard
parameter hypothesis in this argument.

To pass from an integral identity to algebraicity, move negative
coefficients to the other side. For a Hodge tuple $T$, this gives
an equality of nonnegative multiplicity vectors
$T\sqcup T_-=T_+$, where $T_-$ and $T_+$ concatenate known
algebraic Hodge tuples. Join the factors and cancel $T_-$ by
Section 2.3. This proves the assertion in every even dimension;
non-middle classes in odd dimensions are ambient. If an exponent
vanishes, [2, Corollary 2-3, p. 388] gives all dimensions for
every prime-power degree, with no bound on its exponent. Its printed
proof invokes Theorem 2-1, which assumes $\gcd(i,d)=1$; the
standard elements with $\gcd(i,d)>1$ are covered by Lemma 2.2.
Proposition 2.4 now proves products at one common
degree. For different degrees, take their least common multiple $M$
within this same fixed family, pull back to the product of degree-$M$
varieties, and push forward divided by the product degree. $\square$

The argument does not assert mixed products involving both an
11-supported and a 13-supported degree: their least common multiple
can have three distinct prime divisors. No all-powers assertion for
every odd degree follows from the fourfold classification alone.

## 6. A tensor filtration identity on a Fermat curve times a threefold

For a rational pure Hodge structure $H$, write $F^p_{\mathbf Q}H$
for its largest rational sub-Hodge structure contained in the complex
Hodge filtration $F^pH_{\mathbf C}$. This is the rational level
filtration of [9, pp. 519--520].

**Proposition 6.1.** For every odd $m\ge5$, put
$C=X_m^1$, $Y=X_m^3$, and
$H=H^1(C)\otimes H^3_{\rm prim}(Y)$. Then
$$
 H^1(C)\otimes F^1_{\mathbf Q}H^3_{\rm prim}(Y)
 \ \subsetneq\ F^1_{\mathbf Q}H.
 \tag{6.1}
$$

*Proof.* Let $g\in G_m$ act on the line of character $v$ by
$g^*=\chi_v(g)$, and put
$p(v)=\operatorname{age}_m(v)-1$, the second Hodge index of the
character line [16, Theorem I(ii), p. 176]. Take
$$
 A=(1,1,m-2),\qquad B=(2,2,2,m-5,m-1)
 \tag{6.2}
$$
for $m\ge7$. The diagonal coefficient-Galois orbit of the pair
$(A,B)$ is a rational sub-Hodge structure in $H$. If
$1\le t<m/2$, then $p(tA)=0$, and direct residue summation gives
$$
 p(tB)=1+\lfloor5t/m\rfloor\in\{1,2,3\}.
 \tag{6.3}
$$
There is no zero coordinate: $m\nmid5t$ for a unit $t$ when
$m\ge7$. Complex conjugation replaces the total second index by
four minus that index. Thus all lines of this orbit have both Hodge
indices at least one, and the entire rational orbit lies in
$F^1_{\mathbf Q}H$.

On the other hand, $t=(m+1)/2$ is a unit and transforms $B$ to
$$
 (1,1,1,(m-5)/2,(m-1)/2),
$$
of age one. The orbit of $B$ therefore contains an extreme
$(3,0)$-line. It is stable under $t\mapsto-t$, so it also contains
the conjugate $(0,3)$-line, which does not lie in $F^1$. Hence it
is excluded from $F^1_{\mathbf Q}H^3_{\rm prim}(Y)$. The rational filtration is
deck-stable; its complexification is a sum of character lines, so
this exclusion also excludes the displayed tensor orbit from the
left side of (6.1).

For $m=5$, use $A=(1,1,3)$, $B=(2,2,2,2,2)$. As
$t=1,2,3,4$, the two second-index lists are
$(0,0,1,1)$ and $(1,3,0,2)$; their sums are
$(1,3,1,3)$. The same conclusion follows. $\square$

The original homological notation in [9, proof of Proposition
2.2(iv), p. 522] gives the second equality
$$
 \bigoplus_{j+\ell=i}\mathcal F_h
 (W_{-j}H_j(X_1,\mathbf Q)\otimes W_{-\ell}H_\ell(X_2,\mathbf Q))
 =\bigoplus_{j+\ell=i}\ \bigoplus_{t+s=h}
 \mathcal F_tW_{-j}H_j(X_1,\mathbf Q)
 \otimes\mathcal F_sW_{-\ell}H_\ell(X_2,\mathbf Q).
 \tag{6.4}
$$
We write $h$ for the source's filtration index $m$, to distinguish
it from the Fermat degree. The calligraphic $\mathcal F$ is the
rational level filtration, as defined on pp. 519--521, not the complex
Hodge filtration. For a smooth projective variety of dimension $d$,
Poincaré duality gives
$H_j=H^{2d-j}(d)$. Since [9, p. 521] defines
$\mathcal F_mW_{-j}H_j=\mathcal F^{-m}W_{-j}H_j$ and a Tate twist
$(d)$ shifts the filtration index by $d$, this gives
$\mathcal F_tH_j=F^{d-t}_{\mathbf Q}H^{2d-j}(d)$, in agreement
with [9, (2.1), p. 521].
Apply (6.4) to the Künneth summand $(j,\ell)=(1,3)$ for
$(X_1,X_2)=(C,Y)$, with $i=4$, $h=3$, and remove
the common Tate twist $(4)$. Since
$F^1_{\mathbf Q}H^1(C)=0$ and
$F^2_{\mathbf Q}H^3(Y)=0$, the only nonzero factor convolution
is $H^1(C)\otimes F^1_{\mathbf Q}H^3(Y)$. Applying the
primitive projector on $Y$ would identify the two sides of (6.1).
Proposition 6.1 disproves that equality on the actual Fermat product
used in [10, equation (3.3), p. 227]. It does
not assert a counterexample to the generalized Hodge conjecture.

The displayed character pair need not survive the particular invariant
projection used to construct a fourfold from that product. The
counterexample concerns the product identity and its proof; no
additional assertion about its image under that projection is needed
or made. A replacement argument for the product estimate could still
establish conclusions for which this printed proof is insufficient.

## 7. Positive classification by conductor

The finite residual classification in Section 4 suggests the stronger
statement that, at every odd degree, all sextuples outside
$\mathrm{Dec}$ and the exact 5-standard family are inflations of
(3.1). This is Theorem A, proved in Section 7.8 as Theorem 7.20.
We first prove the subfamily results used in that reduction.
For squarefree semiprimes we work in the integral
character group and retain a length estimate. The argument below
is a consequence of Aoki's character kernel; the resulting family is
also covered by [11] and [5]. Lemmas 7.1 and 7.2 are not new.
For an odd squarefree semiprime degree, the decomposition (7.2)--(7.3)
is [15, Theorem 3.1], and (7.5) is the lower bound of [15, Theorem B].
The local term of that bound at each of the two primes equals the
corresponding term $\delta_p(q)$ or $\delta_q(p)$ of (7.5), because
$-1$ is a power of $q$ modulo $p$ exactly when $\operatorname{ord}_p(q)$
is even. [15] states the bound for every Hodge element outside the
subgroup generated by inverse pairs and standard elements, without
restriction to sextuples, and proves that it is
attained, by constructions it attributes to [4, Lemmas 8.1 and 8.3];
[15] appeared on 22 October 2026, before the present version of these
lemmas. We include short proofs in the notation used below; only the
consequence for sextuples, $L(p,q)\le6$ in (7.5), is used.

Put $m=pq$, with $p<q$ odd primes. For a tuple $T$, write
$n_x$ for its multiplicities and
$$
 F(x)=n_x-n_{-x}.
$$
Thus $F$ is an integer odd function, and
$$
 |T|\ \ge\ \frac12\sum_{x\ne0}|F(x)|.
 \tag{7.1}
$$
The Hodge equations are equivalent to
$\sum_xF(x)b_m(tx)=0$ for every unit $t$, where
$b_m(x)=[x]_m/m-1/2$. An integer function $F$ on
$\mathbf Z/m\mathbf Z\smallsetminus\{0\}$, or a multiset with multiplicity
function $F$, satisfying these equations is called *balanced*; this
term is used in this sense throughout Section 7. At $t=1$ a balanced
multiset $T$ has $\sum_{x\in T}[x]_m=|T|m/2$, so a balanced multiset
of even length has zero sum modulo $m$.

**Lemma 7.1.** In Chinese remainder coordinates on $U_m$, every
integer-valued odd balanced function $F$, not necessarily coming
from a multiset, has the unique form
$$
 F(a,b)=u(a)+v(b),\qquad a\in U_p,\ b\in U_q,
 \tag{7.2}
$$
where $u,v$ are rational odd functions. On the other two strata,
$$
 F(qy)=u(qy)-u(y),\qquad F(pz)=v(pz)-v(z).
 \tag{7.3}
$$
Either both functions are integer-valued or both are valued in
$\mathbf Z+\tfrac12$. Define $\lambda(F)=0$ or 1 accordingly.
Then $\lambda(F)=0$ if and only if $F$, modulo inverse pairs,
is an integral combination of prime-standard tuples.

*Proof.* Let $\chi=(\chi_p,\chi_q)$, with both local components
nonprincipal. It is primitive of conductor exactly $m$. For odd
$\chi$, its Fourier coefficient is
$$
 \sum_{x\in U_m}\chi(x)b_m(x)
 =\frac1m\sum_{x\in U_m}\chi(x)[x]_m=S(\chi)\ne0
$$
by [1, Lemma 2.3, p. 29]. In the multiplicative Fourier transform
of the balance equations the order-$p$ and order-$q$ strata
have support only on $(\chi_p,1)$ and $(1,\chi_q)$,
respectively. Thus the unit-stratum correlation has coefficient
$(\sum_xF(x)\bar\chi(x))S(\chi)=0$. For even $\chi$,
oddness of $F$ already makes its coefficient zero. Hence the
Fourier coefficients of the unit matrix vanish whenever both local
characters are nonprincipal. The remaining Fourier modes are exactly
the sum of a row function and a column function. Taking averages in
each coordinate gives unique zero-mean functions $u,v$; oddness of
$F$ makes each odd.

For fixed $a\in U_p$, the distribution identity is
$$
 \sum_{b\in U_q}b_m(\operatorname{CRT}(a,b))
  =b_p(a)-b_p(q^{-1}a).
 \tag{7.4}
$$
Indeed, the sum over all $q$ lifts of $a$ is $b_p(a)$,
and the omitted lift divisible by $q$ contributes
$b_p(q^{-1}a)$. Substituting (7.2) into the balance equations,
(7.4) gives (7.3): the remainder is a sum of a function of the
$p$-unit and a function of the $q$-unit. More explicitly, put
$D(y)=F(qy)+u(y)-u(qy)$. Its correlation
$f_p(t)=\sum_yD(y)b_p(ty)$ is odd and independent of the
$q$-unit. Since $f_p(t_p)+f_q(t_q)=0$ for independently
varying units, both functions are constant, hence zero by oddness.
Every odd character modulo the prime $p$ is primitive;
the same nonvanishing therefore gives $D=0$. The argument
on the other stratum is identical. This proves (7.3).

The equalities
$F(a,b)+F(a,-b)=2u(a)$ and
$F(a,b)-F(a',b)=u(a)-u(a')$ show that $2u$ is integral and all
values of $u$ have the same fractional part. Oddness makes that
part zero or one half. The same holds for $v$, and the two parts
agree because $F(a,b)$ is integral.

The unit part of a $q$-standard tuple with parameter congruent to
$a_0\pmod p$ has
$u=\mathbf1_{a_0}-\mathbf1_{-a_0}$, $v=0$. These are admissible
standards since $p>2$ and the parameter is nonzero modulo $p$.
Likewise $p$-standards generate all integer odd column functions.
Equations (7.3) then determine the lower strata. Thus integer-valued
$u,v$ are exactly the integral span of standards in the quotient
by inverse pairs, proving the final assertion. $\square$

Here equality modulo inverse pairs is equality in the free abelian
group on nonzero residues modulo $(x)+(-x)=0$. Thus an integral
relation may have signed coefficients. Moving its negative pair
coefficients across the equality yields an equality of nonnegative
multisets after replacing negative standards by their conjugates.
Oddness of $m$ ensures that no nonzero residue is its own negative.

For a prime $r$ and an integer $s$ prime to it, set
$$
 \delta_r(s)=
 \begin{cases}
 (r-1)/\operatorname{ord}_r(s),&\operatorname{ord}_r(s)\text{ even},\\
 0,&\operatorname{ord}_r(s)\text{ odd}.
 \end{cases}
$$

**Lemma 7.2.** If $\lambda(F)=1$, then
$$
 |T|\ge L(p,q):=\frac{(p-1)(q-1)}4+\delta_p(q)+\delta_q(p).
 \tag{7.5}
$$

*Proof.* For each pair of antipodal pairs $\{a,-a\}$,
$\{b,-b\}$, the contribution of their four unit entries to the
right side of (7.1) is
$$
 |u(a)+v(b)|+|u(a)-v(b)|
 =2\max(|u(a)|,|v(b)|)\ge1.
$$
There are $(p-1)(q-1)/4$ such blocks. On the order-$p$ stratum,
(7.3) contributes
$\frac12\sum_y|u(qy)-u(y)|$. If the order of $q$ modulo $p$
is even, each multiplication-by-$q$ cycle contains $y$ and
$-y$. The half-integral odd values include opposite nonzero values,
so total variation around each cycle is at least two. There are
$(p-1)/\operatorname{ord}_p(q)$ cycles, giving $\delta_p(q)$.
If that order is odd the asserted lower bound on this stratum is zero.
The order-$q$ stratum gives the remaining term. $\square$

*Proof of Theorem 1.2.* If $p\ge5$ and $(p,q)\ne(5,7)$, the
first term of (7.5) already exceeds six. For $(5,7)$, both orders
are even and $L(5,7)=8$. If $p=3$, then the first term exceeds
six as soon as $q\ge17$. Thus a Hodge sextuple with
$\lambda=1$ is possible only at degrees $15,21,33,39$.

If $\lambda=0$, Lemma 7.1 expresses the tuple as an integral
combination of algebraic standard tuples modulo inverse pairs. Such
an identity implies algebraicity: replace any negative standard by
its coordinatewise negative, which is a Galois conjugate and the
negative of its class modulo pairs. The resulting nonnegative
concatenation and the given tuple differ by a signed sum of inverse
pairs. Put the negative pairs on the other side, join the known
algebraic factors, and cancel the remaining known pair factors as
in Section 2.3.

At degrees 15 and 21 the surface Hodge tuples
$(1,7,10,12)$ and $(1,10,15,16)$ have $\lambda=1$.
These reference tuples have length four, whereas the tuple being
treated has length six; the joins and cancellations take place in
a larger auxiliary dimension, so their lengths need not agree.
At 33 and 39 use the first tuples in (3.1), algebraic by Section 3.
In the coordinates of (7.2), the points $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$
are the residues 1 and 4, 1 and 13, 1 and 10, 1 and 25 at degrees
15, 21, 33, 39 respectively. Each reference tuple contains the first
of these residues and not the second, nor the negative of either, so
$\lambda=1$ for it. The second degree-39 seed has $\lambda=0$ and is
not used. These parity assertions follow either from (7.2) or from the formula
$$
 \lambda(F)=F(1,1)+F(1,-1)\pmod2.
$$
Subtracting the corresponding known algebraic tuple from a
$\lambda=1$ sextuple gives an integer-potential balanced function,
which is in the standard span by Lemma 7.1. Apply the same integral
realization argument, now allowing the one additional known tuple.
This proves the theorem. $\square$

The estimate and its normalization have independent finite checks:
all half-sign potentials for $(3,5),(3,7),(3,11),(3,13),(3,17),(5,7)$
give integral balanced functions with the predicted stratum equations;
the minimum lengths are respectively $4,4,6,6,10,8$. These are
checks of the formulas, not the proof of their unrestricted range.

### 7.1. Positive classification in semiprime degree

Integral generation alone does not imply a positive decomposition of
a sextuple. The length-six constraint supplies the additional argument
in semiprime degree.

**Theorem 7.3.** If $m=pq$, with $p<q$ odd primes, every Hodge
sextuple outside $\mathrm{Dec}\cup\mathrm{Std}_5$, modulo units
and coordinate permutations, is the degree-33 tuple or one of the two
degree-39 tuples in (3.1). In particular, no new primitive residual
occurs at any squarefree odd semiprime degree.

*Proof.* A tuple containing an inverse pair is already in
$\mathrm{Dec}$. Assume it is pair-free. Then $F$ determines
the tuple uniquely as its positive part, and (7.1) is an equality.
Write $A=(p-1)/2$, $B=(q-1)/2$, and choose representatives
$a_i,b_j$ of the antipodal pairs in the two prime unit groups.
The unit stratum contributes exactly
$$
 N_U=2\sum_{i=1}^A\sum_{j=1}^B
             \max(|u(a_i)|,|v(b_j)|)
 \tag{7.6}
$$
to the length. For $p\ge7$, [1, Theorem A] already makes
every Hodge sextuple a union of inverse pairs.

Suppose $p=5$. Lemma 7.2 gives $\lambda=0$: its bound
is eight at $q=7$ and exceeds six for $q\ge11$.
If $q\ge11$, a nonzero integer $u$ would give
$N_U\ge q-1>6$, so $u=0$. At $q=7$, a nonzero
$u$ forces $v=0$ and precisely one nonzero antipodal value
of $u$, equal to $\pm1$; otherwise (7.6) is at least
eight. But multiplication by $7=2\pmod5$ moves its antipodal
support, so the order-5 stratum contributes two more entries,
again impossible. Thus $u=0$ in this case as well.
Equation (7.6) now forces $v=e_b$ for one nonzero $b$, where
$e_b=\mathbf1_b-\mathbf1_{-b}$. This is exactly the odd
multiplicity function of a 5-standard tuple. Its two lower-stratum
entries do not cancel, because $5\not\equiv\pm1\pmod q$.
Consequently that standard tuple is pair-free of length six, and
uniqueness of the positive part identifies it with the original tuple.

Suppose $p=3$, $q\ge17$. Lemma 7.2 again gives
$\lambda=0$, and (7.6) gives $u=0$. Set
$$
 r=\sum_{j=1}^B|v(b_j)|,\qquad
 d(v)=\frac12\sum_{z\in U_q}|v(3z)-v(z)|.
$$
Then $6=2r+d(v)$, so $1\le r\le3$.
If $r=1$, then $v=e_b$ and $d(v)=2$, giving length
four rather than six. If $r=3$, then $d(v)=0$.
The positive support of a nonzero invariant $v$ is a union of
multiplication-by-3 cycles of total multiplicity at most three.
Thus $\operatorname{ord}_q(3)\le3$, which would force
$q\mid3^j-1$ for $j\le3$; no prime $q\ge17$ does so.

It remains that $r=2$, $d(v)=2$. A value of absolute size
two at a single antipodal pair would instead give $d(v)=4$.
Hence $v=e_b+e_c$, $b\ne\pm c$. The four signed atoms
$e_{b/3},e_{c/3},-e_b,-e_c$ in $v(3z)-v(z)$ have half-norm less
than four only if two of them cancel. The cancellations
$b/3=b$ and $c/3=c$ force $3\equiv1\pmod q$, and
$b/3=-c/3$ or $b=-c$ contradict $b\ne\pm c$. The only
remaining cancellations are $b/3=c$ and $c/3=b$, that is
$b=3c$ or $c=3b$ (both together would force $9\equiv1$). Relabel so that $v=e_b+e_{3b}$.
Choose a 3-standard tuple with parameter congruent to $b\pmod q$,
and another with parameter congruent to $3b\pmod q$.
Each is a surface Hodge quadruple. The last coordinate $-3i$
of the first cancels the unique coordinate divisible by 3 in the
progression of the second: both have opposite residues modulo $q$
and vanish modulo 3. Removing this inverse pair leaves six entries
with the required odd function. Their norm is six, so they are
pair-free and equal to the original tuple. This is precisely a
surface contraction, hence belongs to $\mathrm{Dec}$.

The remaining cases are $p=3$, $q\in\{5,7,11,13\}$, that is, the
degrees $15,21,33,39$. Here $A=1$; put $\mu=u(1)=-u(-1)$.
By (7.3) the order-3 stratum contributes $N_3=0$ if
$q\equiv1\pmod3$ and $N_3=2|\mu|$ if $q\equiv2\pmod3$.
With $d(v)$ as above, now for possibly half-integral $v$,
$$
 6=N_U+N_3+d(v),\qquad
 N_U=2\sum_{j=1}^B\max(|\mu|,|v(b_j)|).
 \tag{7.6a}
$$
Throughout, $3$ generates $U_5$ and $U_7$, has order five
modulo 11 and order three modulo 13, and $3\not\equiv\pm1$,
$9\not\equiv1\pmod q$.

*Case $\lambda=0$, $\mu=0$.* Then $N_3=0$, $N_U=2r$, and the
argument given for $q\ge17$ applies without change: $r=1$ gives
length four, $r=2$ gives a surface contraction, and $r=3$ requires
$q\mid3^j-1$ with $j\le3$, so $q=13$. At $q=13$ the positive
support of $v$ is one of the four cosets of $\langle3\rangle=\{1,3,9\}$;
the unit congruent to 1 modulo 3 and to 2 modulo 13 permutes them
transitively, since 2 generates $U_{13}/\langle3\rangle$. Thus
$v=e_1+e_3+e_9$ up to units, $F(\pm1,b)=v(b)$, and the tuple is
the second degree-39 tuple $(1,14,16,22,29,35)$ of (3.1).

*Case $\lambda=0$, $\mu\ne0$.* Now $N_U\ge(q-1)|\mu|$, so
$q\in\{5,7\}$, $|\mu|=1$, and after multiplication by $-1$,
$\mu=1$. At $q=5$, $N_U\ge4$ and $N_3=2$; at $q=7$,
$N_U\ge6$ and $N_3=0$. In both cases $d(v)=0$, so $v$ is
invariant under multiplication by a generator of $U_q$, hence
constant, hence zero by oddness. The unit entries are the $q-1$
units congruent to 1 modulo 3, and (7.3) supplies the order-3 entries.
At 15 this is $(1,4,7,10,10,13)=\sigma_{5,1}$. At 21 it is
$(1,4,10,13,16,19)$, the contraction of the surface tuples
$(1,10,16,15)$ and $(6,4,13,19)=13\cdot(1,10,15,16)$ along the
distinguished residues $15,6$, with $1+10+16\equiv6\pmod{21}$.

*Case $\lambda=1$.* Since $N_U\ge(q-1)|\mu|$, the value
$|\mu|\ge\tfrac32$ would give $N_U+N_3\ge9>6$. Hence
$|\mu|=\tfrac12$, and after multiplication by $-1$, $\mu=\tfrac12$.
Every $|v(b)|\ge\tfrac12$, so $N_U\ge(q-1)/2$, with equality
exactly when $|v|=\tfrac12$ everywhere. At $q=13$, (7.6a) forces
equality, $N_3=0$ and $d(v)=0$. Thus $v=\pm\tfrac12$ is constant
on the cosets of $\langle3\rangle$. The four sign patterns form one
orbit under the unit used above, which sends the values
$(\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2)$ on $\{1,3,9\}$ and $\{2,5,6\}$ to
$(-\varepsilon_2,\varepsilon_1)$. For $v=\tfrac12$ on
$\{1,3,9\}\cup\{7,8,11\}$, the function $F(1,b)=\tfrac12+v(b)$
is the indicator of this set and the lower strata vanish, giving
$(1,7,16,22,34,37)$. At $q=11$, $N_3=1$, so $N_U=5$ and
$d(v)=0$. Now $\langle3\rangle$ is the group of squares, of index
two, and its two sign patterns are exchanged by the unit congruent to
1 modulo 3 and to 2 modulo 11. For $v=\tfrac12$ on the squares the
unit entries are the five units congruent to 1 modulo 3 whose residues
modulo 11 are squares, and (7.3) gives $F(11)=u(-1)-u(1)=-1$, that
is, the entry 22. This is $(1,4,16,22,25,31)$.

At $q=5,7$, let $a_k=v(3^k)$, so that
$a_{k+(q-1)/2}=-a_k$ and
$d(v)=\sum_{k=0}^{(q-3)/2}|a_{k+1}-a_k|$. The triangle inequality,
applied from each starting index, gives $d(v)\ge2\max_k|a_k|$, and
$N_U=2\sum_j|v(b_j)|$. At $q=5$ this makes the length
$1+2(|a_0|+|a_1|)+2\max(|a_0|,|a_1|)$, which is four when
$|a_0|=|a_1|=\tfrac12$ and at least eight otherwise; there is no
sextuple. At $q=7$, $N_3=0$, and a value of absolute size at least
$\tfrac32$ would give length at least eight. Hence
$|a_0|=|a_1|=|a_2|=\tfrac12$ and $d(v)=3$, which forces
$a_1=-a_0$, $a_2=a_0$. Multiplication by the unit congruent to 1
modulo 3 and to 3 modulo 7 exchanges the two choices of $a_0$. For
$a_0=\tfrac12$ the tuple is $(1,4,9,15,16,18)$, the contraction of
$(1,15,16,10)$ and $\sigma_{3,4}=(11,4,9,18)$ along the
distinguished residues $10,11$. It lies in $\mathrm{Dec}$.

Hence the pair-free Hodge sextuples outside $\mathrm{Dec}\cup\mathrm{Std}_5$
at these four degrees lie in the three stated orbits. They are
outside $\mathrm{Dec}$: at $m=3q$, $q\in\{11,13\}$, apply the same
analysis to a pair-free balanced quadruple. Lemma 7.2 gives
$L(3,q)=6>4$, so $\lambda=0$; $N_U\ge q-1$ forces $\mu=0$; and
$4=2r+d(v)$ leaves $r=1$, because $r=2$, $d(v)=0$ would need
$q\mid3^j-1$ with $j\le2$. Hence every pair-free surface Hodge
quadruple at $33$ or $39$ has exactly two unit entries. A balanced
quadruple containing an inverse pair is a union of two inverse pairs,
so a pair-free sextuple in $\mathrm{Dec}$ is a contraction of two
pair-free quadruples and has at most four unit entries. The three
tuples have five, six and six unit entries. They are not 5-standard,
as $5\nmid m$. The two degree-39 orbits are distinct, because the
units preserve $\lambda$, which is $1$ for the first and $0$ for
the second. $\square$

### 7.2. Positive classification at prime powers

**Proposition 7.4.** Let $m=p^a$ be an odd prime power.
Every Hodge sextuple lies in
$\mathrm{Dec}\cup\mathrm{Std}_5$.

*Proof.* At a prime degree $p$ every nonzero residue is a unit and every
odd character is primitive, so [1, Lemma 2.3] and the balance equations
make all odd Fourier coefficients of the odd multiplicity function of a
pair-free tuple vanish; that function is then zero, which is impossible.
Thus every Hodge sextuple at prime degree contains an inverse pair; this is
also the case $n=4$ of [1, Theorem A(i)]. Reduce content
and discard the case of an inverse pair. It suffices to consider a
primitive pair-free tuple at $a\ge2$. At a primitive character
modulo $p^a$, every lower-order stratum vanishes from the Fourier
transform of the balance equations. Primitive odd nonvanishing
[1, Lemma 2.3] and oddness therefore force the unit-stratum
function $F$ to have Fourier support of conductor at most
$p^{a-1}$. It is constant on each fibre of
$U_{p^a}\to U_{p^{a-1}}$, of size $p$.
Pair-freeness implies the same constancy for positive multiplicities.
Thus the unit entries are complete progressions
$$
 P_i=(i,i+d,\ldots,i+(p-1)d),\qquad d=p^{a-1},\quad p\nmid i,
$$
counted with multiplicity. At least one occurs by primitiveness.
For every unit $t$, the distribution identity is
$$
 \sum_{x\in P_i}b_m(tx)=b_m(tpi).
 \tag{7.7}
$$
Replacing a progression by $pi$ preserves balance and decreases
length by $p-1$; all resulting entries are nonzero and divisible
by $p$.

If $p\ge7$, a progression is already too long. If $p=5$,
there is exactly one progression and one other entry. Replacement
gives a balanced pair, necessarily inverse, so the original sextuple
is exactly $\sigma_{5,i}$.
If $p=3$ and there are two progressions, replacement gives a
balanced pair $(3i,3j)$. Therefore $i+j=0\pmod d$, making
the two progressions inverse to each other, contrary to pair-freeness
(a repeated progression would require $2i=0\pmod d$).
If there is just one progression, the other three entries, together
with $3i$, form a surface Hodge quadruple by (7.7).
Its contraction with the standard surface tuple
$(P_i,-3i)$ recovers the sextuple, proving $\mathrm{Dec}$.
Content inflation preserves both criteria, as proved in Section 4.
$\square$

Theorem 7.3 and Proposition 7.4 establish the positive six-seed
classification on these two infinite conductor classes. Sections 7.3--7.4
extend it to further conductor classes, before Section 7.8 proves the
unrestricted six-seed statement.

### 7.3. Degrees coprime to six

The prime-to-six branch is also treated in [5, subsection "Fermat
fourfolds of degree prime to six"], whose finite-product argument is the
same in spirit; the case is also covered by [11]. We give a
self-contained proof of the exact positive classification used here;
neither all-odd theorem is a premise.

Call a function on a finite product $C=C_1\times\cdots\times C_r$
split if it is a sum of functions, each independent of at least one
coordinate. No integrality of the summands is assumed.

**Lemma 7.5.** Suppose $|C_i|\ge4$, with at most one factor
of size four. An integer-valued, nonzero split function $f$
with $\sum_C|f|\le6$ is $1$ or $-1$ on one coordinate
line and zero elsewhere.

*Proof.* Induct on the number of factors. There is no nonzero function
for zero factors; on one factor split means constant. Slice along
the first factor.
Differences of two slices are split on the remaining factors.
If one slice vanishes, every slice is split on the remaining factors.
By induction, each nonzero slice has mass at least four and is a
signed line. At most one such slice can occur, proving the assertion.
If all slices agree, invariance in the first coordinate and the mass
bound give one line with value $\pm1$.

It remains that every slice is nonzero and they do not all agree.
Some slice has mass one, since $6<2|C_1|$. If all slices had
mass one, their pairwise differences would have mass at most two;
induction would make every difference zero. Therefore a slice of
mass $M\ge2$ exists. Its difference from a mass-one slice is
nonzero and split, so induction gives $M+1\ge|C_j|\ge4$
for some $j>1$. Hence
$6\ge M+|C_1|-1\ge3+3$. Equality forces both
$|C_1|=4$ and $|C_j|=4$, contrary to the hypothesis.
$\square$

**Theorem 7.6 (prime-to-six classification).** If $\gcd(m,6)=1$,
every Hodge sextuple contains an inverse pair or is exactly 5-standard.
In particular the rational Hodge conjecture holds for $X_m^4$,
and $\mathcal R(m)=\varnothing$ for the residual set in Section 2.3.

*Proof.* Let $T$ be pair-free, let $e$ be its largest additive
order, and identify the subgroup of order $e$ in $\mathbf Z/m$
by $\kappa(z)=(m/e)z$. On $U_e$ put
$F(z)=n_{\kappa(z)}-n_{-\kappa(z)}$. Then $F$ is odd,
nonzero, and has mass at most twelve. A primitive odd character
of conductor $e$ annihilates every other order stratum in the
Fourier transform of balance: an order smaller than $e$ cannot
be a multiple of $e$. Nonvanishing [1, Lemma 2.3] therefore
makes every primitive Fourier coefficient of $F$ zero; even
coefficients vanish by oddness.

For each prime $p\mid e$, let
$K_p=\ker(U_e\to U_{e/p})$. These groups have direct product
$H$, and sizes $p$ if $p^2\mid e$, $p-1$ otherwise.
A nonprimitive character is trivial on some $K_p$, so Fourier
inversion makes $F$ split on every coset of $H$. Every size
is at least four, and only $K_5$ can have size four.
If $e$ is prime, split means constant on $U_e$, contradicting
oddness and nonvanishing. Thus $e$ is composite.

We first show that $F\ge1$ on a line $wK_p$ with
$p\in\{5,7\}$, apart from a possible full-mass case at $e=35$.
If $-1\notin H$, each coset and its negative have the same mass,
at most six each. Lemma 7.5 gives a signed line; negating the coset
if needed makes its sign positive. Its size is at most six, forcing
$p=5$ or 7. If $-1\in H$, then $e$ is squarefree:
a repeated prime would make all of $H$ congruent to one at that
prime. Thus $H=U_e$.

Suppose a prime $p\ge11$ divides this squarefree $e$.
There is at least one other prime, since a split odd function on
a single factor is zero. Slice along $K_p$, of size at least ten.
Oddness pairs the slices, preserving their masses. If a slice is
zero, restriction to any other slice is split in the remaining
factors and has mass at most six; Lemma 7.5 again gives the desired
line. If every slice is nonzero, the total mass (at most twelve, and even)
and pairing force every slice mass to be at most two, with some mass one.
The difference between that slice and any other has mass at most
three, so Lemma 7.5 forces it to vanish. Hence all slices have
one equal signed atom: the support is one $K_p$-line, with
constant sign. Its fixed coordinate in the remaining factors differs
from its negative, because those factors have odd residue characteristic.
Oddness would require another supported line there, a contradiction.

The remaining squarefree composite is $e=35$. Its split odd
matrix has the form $u(a)+v(b)$ on $U_5\times U_7$, with
odd potentials simultaneously integral or half-integral, by the
row-and-column argument of Lemma 7.1. In the integral case, (7.6)
shows that both potentials cannot be nonzero: that would cost at
least eight entries. A single nonzero potential must be one signed
antipodal atom, giving the required positive line. In the
half-integral case the unit contribution is at least six, hence
the full mass is twelve and all six entries of $T$ have order 35.
They divide via $\kappa$ to a pair-free all-unit Hodge sextuple
at degree 35. The proof of Theorem 7.3 at $(p,q)=(5,7)$ excludes
this: every pair-free sextuple there is 5-standard, and a 5-standard
sextuple at 35 has two nonzero entries divisible by 5, so it is
not all-unit. Thus this case is impossible.

Consider the positive line obtained above. For $p=7$ its size
cannot be seven, and size six consumes the whole mass twelve.
Thus all six entries have order $e$, $F$ is 1 on the line
and -1 on its negative. They are disjoint, since oddness forbids
$-1\in K_p$. The zero sum of the tuple gives
$\sum_zF(z)z=0\pmod e$, whereas reduction modulo $e/7$
gives $12w$, since every $k\in K_7$ is one modulo $e/7$.
Here $e/7>1$ is coprime to six, so cannot divide twelve.
Therefore $p=5$.

Put $x=\kappa(w)$, and
$P_x=(x,x+m/5,\ldots,x+4m/5)$. The line supplies either five
or four entries of this progression. In the first case the sixth
entry is $-5x$, so the tuple is exactly standard.
In the second, write $T\sqcup\{y\}=P_x\sqcup\{b,c\}$.
We have $5x\ne0$, because $e\ne5$; and $y\ne5x$,
since otherwise $20x=0$ would force $5x=0$ at odd degree.
The distribution identity for $P_x$ shows that
$Q=(5x,b,c,-y)$ is a nonzero surface Hodge quadruple.
Every prime divisor of $m$ exceeds four, so [1, Theorem A,
with hypersurface dimension $n=2$]
makes $Q$ a union of inverse pairs. Its pair cannot be
$(5x,-y)$, or $(b,c)$, by the two exclusions just made
and the pair-freeness of $T$. Any other pair, together with
the zero sum, gives $\{b,c\}=\{y,-5x\}$.
Thus $T=P_x\sqcup\{-5x\}$ in every case. Since $5x\ne0$, the
tuple is admissible by the remark after (2.4), and Lemma 2.2 gives
its algebraicity. $\square$

The last theorem and Proposition 7.4 classify conductors prime to
six and pure prime-power depths. The next subsection treats two-prime depths containing
3, except for support $\{3,5\}$. Bhattacharjee [5,
subsection "Fermat fourfolds of odd degree divisible by three"] states a stronger profile descent
whose terminal orbits are at 21, 33 and 39. We do not use that
unreviewed all-odd theorem as a premise. Its profile descent also
differs from the exact residual classification proved here. For example,
the second degree-39 seed contains $1,14$ of the 3-progression
$\{1,14,27\}$. It admits a profile-lowering move in the sense of
[5], although it lies outside $\mathrm{Dec}\cup\mathrm{Std}_5$.

### 7.4. Two-prime depths with a prime at least seven

We make the conductor calculation explicit. This is the character
relation of [5, Lemma 3.28], proved there from
$B_{1,\chi}\ne0$; we derive it from the classical nonvanishing input
[1, Lemma 2.3] and include its proof and normalization.

**Lemma (conductor Fourier relation).** Let $T$ be a balanced multiset
of nonzero residues modulo odd $m$. Write an entry as
$a=(m/f_a)z_a$, where $f_a$ is its additive order and
$z_a\in U_{f_a}$. For an odd primitive Dirichlet character
$\chi$ of conductor $c\mid m$,
$$
 \sum_{a\in T,\ c\mid f_a}
 \frac{\overline{\chi(z_a)}}{\varphi(f_a)}
 \prod_{\substack{p\mid f_a\\p\nmid c}}(1-\chi(p))=0.
 \tag{7.7a}
$$
Primes, rather than prime powers, index the product.

*Proof.* Put $B_{1,\chi}=c^{-1}\sum_{r=1}^c r\chi(r)$.
This is nonzero for a primitive odd character by [1, Lemma 2.3].
For every multiple $f$ of $c$, periodicity and
$\sum_{r=1}^c\chi(r)=0$ give
$\sum_{r=1}^{f}r\chi(r)=fB_{1,\chi}$. Excluding the primes
dividing $f$ but not $c$ by inclusion and exclusion therefore gives
$$
 \sum_{r\in U_f}r\chi(r)
   =fB_{1,\chi}\prod_{p\mid f,\ p\nmid c}(1-\chi(p)).
$$
Here $r$ denotes its representative in $\{1,\ldots,f-1\}$.
If $c\nmid f_a$, the character $\chi$ is nontrivial on the
kernel of $U_m\to U_{f_a}$, so averaging $\chi(t)[ta]_m$
over that kernel gives zero. Indeed, choose a prime with
$v_p(f_a)<v_p(c)$. Primitivity makes the local character
nontrivial on the kernel of reduction to this smaller prime-power
modulus. Lift such a unit to $U_m$, taking its other prime
components to be one; the lift is one modulo $f_a$ and has
nontrivial character value. Otherwise unit reduction is surjective
and changing variables by $z_a$ gives
$$
 \sum_{t\in U_m}\chi(t)[ta]_m
 =\frac{m\varphi(m)B_{1,\chi}}{\varphi(f_a)}
   \overline{\chi(z_a)}
   \prod_{p\mid f_a,\ p\nmid c}(1-\chi(p)).
$$
The balance equation makes the sum over $a$ of the left side zero.
Divide by the common nonzero factor to obtain (7.7a). $\square$

An entry order is called maximal if no entry has order a proper
multiple of it. The entries of a maximal order $e$ are called the
*tops* at $e$. At such an order $e$, equation (7.7a) with $c=e$
involves only that stratum. Let
$F_e(z)=n_{(m/e)z}-n_{-(m/e)z}$. Its odd primitive Fourier
coefficients vanish; its even coefficients vanish by oddness.
For $K_p=\ker(U_e\to U_{e/p})$, a character is nonprimitive
exactly when it is trivial on at least one $K_p$. Thus Fourier
inversion writes $F_e$ as a sum of functions invariant under
one of the $K_p$. Restricting to a coset of their direct product
$H$ makes it split in the precise sense of Section 7.3. This proves
the maximal-order assertion for arbitrary prime support and exponents,
without assuming nonvanishing for imprimitive characters.

We will also use the following elementary correspondence between these
lines and positive progressions. Every $k\in K_p$ is one modulo
$e/p$, so $(m/e)wk-(m/e)w$ is a multiple of $m/p$.
Consequently a positive $wK_p$-line gives $p$ distinct points
of a $p$-progression if $p^2\mid e$, and $p-1$ if
$p\parallel e$. In the latter case the omitted point is the
unique one whose order loses the $p$-factor. This is the fibre-line
observation in [5, Lemma 3.29].

**Lemma 7.7.** Let $C$ have $s\ge6$ elements. An integer split
function on $\{1,2,3\}\times C$, of mass at most six, is constant
in the first coordinate if $s>6$. If $s=6$, either it is constant
in that coordinate or it equals one signed atom in the first coordinate,
repeated over all of $C$.

*Proof.* Write its entries as $f(i,c)=u_i+v_c$. If two rows differ,
their difference is a nonzero integer, so each column has mass at least
one. This is impossible for $s>6$. For $s=6$, all columns must
have mass one. Two such integer column vectors differ by a constant
vector. A nonzero constant difference would have mass at least three,
whereas the difference of two mass-one vectors has mass at most two.
Consequently all columns are equal to the same signed atom. $\square$

**Theorem 7.8.** A primitive Hodge sextuple at degree
$m=3^a q^b$, with $q\ge7$ prime and $a,b\ge1$,
$\max(a,b)>1$, belongs to $\mathrm{Dec}$.

*Proof.* Assume the tuple $T$ is pair-free. First let $a\ge2$.
Choose a maximal order $e=3^a q^c$ among entries with full 3-primary
order; primitiveness guarantees such an entry. On its order stratum
let $F$ be the nonzero odd multiplicity function. Its mass is at most
twelve. If $c=0$, primitive Fourier vanishing makes $F$ constant
on the three-element fibres $U_e\to U_{e/3}$. A positive fibre is
a complete 3-progression in $T$.

If $c>0$, put
$$
 K_3=\ker(U_e\to U_{e/3}),\qquad
 K_q=\ker(U_e\to U_{e/q}),\qquad H=K_3K_q.
$$
Their orders are three and $s$, where $s=q-1$ for $c=1$
and $s=q$ otherwise. Fourier inversion makes $F$ split on
every coset of $H=K_3\times K_q$. Since $9\mid e$,
$-1\notin H$; a coset and its negative therefore each have mass
at most six. Lemma 7.7 gives a positive complete $K_3$-line
unless $q=7,c=1$ and the entire positive support is a single
$K_7$-line of length six. In that exceptional case all entries
have order $e$. Write them as $(m/e)wk$, $k\in K_7$.
The zero sum of $T$, reduced modulo $e/7=3^a$, would give
$6w=0$, impossible since $a\ge2$ and $w$ is a unit.

Thus $T$ contains a full progression
$P=(x,x+m/3,x+2m/3)$, with $3x\ne0$. Replacing $P$
by $3x$ gives a surface Hodge quadruple by the distribution identity
(7.7). Its contraction with $(P,-3x)$ recovers $T$, proving
$T\in\mathrm{Dec}$.

It remains that $a=1,b\ge2$. Put $Q=q^b$, and on the unit
stratum write $F(s,u)$, with $s\in U_3$, $u\in U_Q$.
Set $D(u)=F(1,u)-F(-1,u)$. This is even. A primitive character
at degree $3Q$ is the product of the nontrivial character modulo
3 and a primitive character modulo $Q$. The odd such products
use the even primitive characters of $Q$. Their vanishing says
that every primitive Fourier coefficient of $D$ is zero;
its odd coefficients already vanish by evenness. Hence $D$ is
constant on fibres of $U_Q\to U_{Q/q}$, of size $q$.
Each fibre is disjoint from its negative. A nonzero even function
constant on these fibres has mass at least $2q>12$, whereas
$\|D\|_1\le\|F\|_1\le12$. Thus
$$
 F(1,u)=F(-1,u)=v(u),\qquad v(-u)=-v(u),\quad v(u)\in\mathbf Z.
 \tag{7.8}
$$
This conclusion also holds if the unit stratum was initially empty.

Let $G(y)=n_{3y}-n_{-3y}$ on $U_Q$, describing the order-$Q$
stratum. Primitive odd Fourier coefficients at conductor $Q$ give
$$
 G(y)=v(3y)-v(y).
 \tag{7.9}
$$
Here is why this is an equality, including the possible imprimitive
Fourier modes. The distribution identity
$$
 \sum_{s\in U_3}b_{3Q}(\operatorname{CRT}(s,u))
       =b_Q(u)-b_Q(3^{-1}u)
 \tag{7.10}
$$
shows that $E=G+v-v(3\,\cdot)$ has zero primitive odd Fourier
coefficients. All other order strata have smaller $q$-primary
order and contribute zero to those coefficients. Oddness removes
the even coefficients, so $E$ is fibre-constant as above. If
$N_U,N_Q$ are the numbers of entries in the two strata, then
$$
 \|E\|_1\le\|G\|_1+2\|v\|_1
             =2N_Q+2N_U\le12.
$$
A nonzero odd fibre-constant function also has mass at least $2q$.
Thus $E=0$, proving (7.9).

Equations (7.8)--(7.10) define by their positive parts a balanced
submultiset $B\subseteq T$, entirely in these two strata. Indeed
the correlations of the two odd functions cancel identically in
(7.10). If $v=0$, then $G=0$ as well, and every entry has
order dividing $3q^{b-1}$, contrary to primitiveness. Put
$$
 r=\tfrac12\|v\|_1\ge1,\qquad
 d=\tfrac12\sum_{y\in U_Q}|v(3y)-v(y)|.
$$
Then $|B|=2r+d\le6$, so $r\le3$.

For $r=1$, write $v=e_z$, where
$e_z=\mathbf1_z-\mathbf1_{-z}$. Since $3\not\equiv\pm1\pmod Q$,
$d=2$ and $|B|=4$. The remaining two entries would form a
balanced pair, contradicting pair-freeness of $T$.
For $r=3$, the length bound forces $d=0$. Its positive
support would be a union of multiplication-by-3 cycles of total
multiplicity three. Consequently $Q\mid3^j-1$ for some
$j\le3$, impossible since $Q\ge49$.

For $r=2$, a repeated atom $v=2e_z$ gives $d=4$, which
is too long. Otherwise $v=e_z+e_w$, $w\ne\pm z$. An odd
function of norm four cannot be invariant under multiplication by 3
at $Q\ge49$; hence $d>0$. Cancellation in
$$
 e_{3^{-1}z}+e_{3^{-1}w}-e_z-e_w
$$
is possible only if $w=3z$ or $z=3w$: the self-cancellation
possibilities would require $3\equiv\pm1\pmod Q$.
With no cancellation its half-norm is four, exceeding the bound
$d\le2$. Relabel so that $w=3z$. The two 3-standard surface
tuples whose parameters are congruent to $z$ and $3z$ modulo
$Q$ have one distinguished inverse pair: the last coordinate
of the first and the progression coordinate divisible by 3 of the
second. Contracting that pair gives a sextuple with exactly (7.8)--(7.9).
Its length is six, so it is pair-free and equals $B=T$. This
is a surface contraction, as required. $\square$

*Proof of Theorem 1.3.* Divide out the content of a residual sextuple.
Proposition 7.4 handles a remaining prime power, Theorem 7.8 excludes
primitive residuals at positive depths with $\max(a,b)>1$, and
Theorem 7.3 classifies the remaining squarefree semiprime. Content
inflation preserves and reflects both closure criteria, by Section 4.
Thus exactly the stated seed inflations remain. Their algebraicity
and that of the other tuples follow from Sections 2--3. $\square$

The proof uses the maximal-order and short-support method also present
in [5], but does not assume its no-move theorem or claim that a
profile-lowering move preserves $\mathrm{Dec}$. In this subfamily
we obtain the positive surface decomposition itself. Finite checks
enumerate all mass-at-most-six split matrices of sizes $3\times6$
and $3\times7$, and all normalized potentials of norm at most
six at six prime-power calibration levels, with direct age and
surface-split checks. These verify the formulas rather than replace
the proof for arbitrary exponents.

The inequalities used above do not cover $|K_5|\in\{4,5\}$.
We next classify these small shapes and their lower-order errors.

### 7.5. Arbitrary prime support at repeated three-primary depth

**Lemma 7.9 (three slices).** Let $C=\{1,2,3\}\times C'$,
where $C'$ is a product of sets of sizes at least four, with at
most one size four and at most one size at most five. Suppose an
integer split function $f\ne0$ has mass at most six, and no
three-point line carries three values of the same nonzero sign.
Then, after permuting the three slices, one of the following holds:

1. One slice is a signed coordinate line of size four, five or six;
   the other two vanish.
2. For a coordinate line $L\subset C'$ of size $l\in\{4,5\}$
   and a nonempty proper subset $A\subset L$, the slices are
   $-\sigma\mathbf1_A,-\sigma\mathbf1_A,
   \sigma\mathbf1_{L\smallsetminus A}$, where $\sigma=\pm1$.
   Their mass is $l+|A|$, so $(l,|A|)=(4,1),(4,2),(5,1)$.

*Proof.* Write $f_i$ for the slices and $\mu_i=\|f_i\|_1$.
Differences of slices are split on $C'$. If a slice vanishes,
Lemma 7.5 makes each nonzero slice one signed line of mass at least
four. Only one can occur, giving (1).

Otherwise $\mu_i\ge1$, and each difference has mass at most five.
By Lemma 7.5 a nonzero difference is one signed line of size four
or five. There is at most one factor of size at most five, so all
such lines have the same coordinate direction. Three
pairwise different slices would give a sum of two signed parallel
line indicators equal to a third one. Distinct parallel lines are
disjoint, and equal lines give coefficients zero or two, so this is
impossible. Two slices therefore agree, say with value $h$, and
the third has value $k$, with $k-h=\sigma\mathbf1_L$.
Off $L$, the no-same-sign condition forces $h=k=0$.
On $L$, it forces $(h,k)=(-\sigma,0)$ or $(0,\sigma)$.
This proves (2). If all slices agreed, their nonzero values would
violate that condition. The empty product case likewise forces a
same-sign three-line. $\square$

**Lemma 7.10 (surface depth).** A pair-free balanced quadruple whose
primitive conductor is divisible by nine is exactly a 3-standard
quadruple $(y,y+m/3,y+2m/3,-3y)$.

*Proof.* Divide out the content and put $3^a\parallel m$, $a\ge2$.
Choose a maximal order $e$ containing $3^a$, and use its odd
multiplicity function on cosets of $H=\prod_{p\mid e}K_p$.
Each coset has mass at most four, since $-1\notin H$.
Primitive Fourier nonvanishing gives the same split property as
in Theorem 7.8. If no same-sign $K_3$-line exists, a zero slice
would force one $K_5$-line of size four by Lemma 7.5. It consumes
all four entries, whose sum modulo $e/5$ is $4w$, with $w$
a unit; this is impossible since $9\mid e/5$.
If all three slices are nonzero, their differences have mass at
most three and vanish by Lemma 7.5. Equal slices give a same-sign
$K_3$-line after all. Thus the quadruple contains a complete
3-progression $P_y$, with $3y\ne0$. Replacing it by $3y$
leaves a balanced pair, necessarily inverse. $\square$

**Theorem 7.11.** At any odd primitive conductor divisible by nine,
a residual sextuple exists only at conductor 45, and is the single
degree-45 orbit in (3.1). There is no restriction on the number or
exponents of the other prime divisors.

*Proof.* Let $T$ be primitive, pair-free, outside
$\mathrm{Dec}\cup\mathrm{Std}_5$, and put
$3^a\parallel m$, $a\ge2$. Choose a maximal order $e$
containing $3^a$. A same-sign $K_3$-line in its odd function
would give a full positive 3-progression, hence a surface contraction
as in Theorem 7.8. None exists. Lemma 7.9 applies on each nonzero
coset, since $|K_3|=3$ and the other factor sizes satisfy its
hypotheses. A coset and its negative together account for as many
entries of $T$ as the mass on either coset.

In case (1), a size-six line must be a $K_7$-line. It consumes
all six entries and their sum modulo $e/7$ is $6w$, impossible
since $9\mid e/7$. A size-five line is a full 5-progression,
so the sixth entry makes $T$ exactly 5-standard.

Consider case (2) in coordinates in $\mathbf Z/e$. Choose $w$
on the distinguished row's $K_5$-line $L$, and parametrize the
$K_3$-coordinate so that this third, distinguished slice has coordinate
one. Thus the two other slices are the nonidentity $K_3$-translates
of this row.
All its points, including those of $A$, are $w$ modulo
$e/5$. The coordinate sum of a complete $K_3$-line through
$u\in A$ is $3u$ (indeed $3uk\equiv3u\pmod e$ for $k\in K_3$, so
this holds for every point of the line). For $l=4$, the sum on $L$ is
$5w-z$, where $z$ is the unique missing element of the
5-progression and is divisible by 5; for $l=5$ it is $5w$.
Consequently the signed sum of the top entries is
$\sigma(l-3|A|)w$ modulo $e/5$.
For $(l,|A|)=(4,2),(5,1)$, all six entries are tops and this
sum is $\mp2w$, impossible since $3\mid e/5$.
For $(4,1)$, there are five tops. Their remaining entry lies
in the subgroup of order $e$ by zero sum; its residue modulo
$e/5$ is $-\sigma w$, and its residue modulo 5 is
$3\sigma u\ne0$, where $A=\{u\}$. It too has order
$e$. It cannot belong to the already specified coset pair;
another pair would have mass one. A nonzero split function on
$K_3\times C'$ has mass at least three: either its three slices
are nonzero, or its nonzero slices are split on $C'$ and have
mass at least four. This excludes the extra top.

Only case (1) with a size-four $K_5$-line remains. In the original
group write
$$
 T=(P_x\smallsetminus\{z\})\sqcup\{b,c\},\qquad
 P_x=\{x,x+m/5,\ldots,x+4m/5\}.
$$
Here $5\parallel e$, $x$ has order $e$, and $z$
has order $e/5$. The distribution identity makes
$Q=(5x,b,c,-z)$ balanced. Its entries are nonzero. If $Q$
has an inverse pair, it is a union of two inverse pairs. The pair
$(5x,-z)$ would imply $20x=0$, impossible at this depth;
$(b,c)$ is forbidden by pair-freeness. Any cross-pair gives
$\{b,c\}=\{z,-5x\}$, making $T$ standard.

Thus $Q$ is pair-free. Its two entries $5x,-z$ have full
3-primary depth $a$. Lemma 7.10 makes $Q$ 3-standard.
Both entries must belong to its progression: its fourth entry
$-3y$ has 3-primary depth at most $a-1$, since $y$ has depth at most $a$. Hence $3(5x+z)=0$.
Using $5z=5x$ gives $90x=0$, so $e\mid90$.
As $9\mid e$, $5\parallel e$ and $e$ is odd, $e=45$.
The two progressions and their distinguished entries all lie in
the subgroup of order 45, so $T$ does too. Primitivity gives
$m=45$. It remains to identify $T$. Here $x$ has order 45; after
multiplication by a unit, $x=1$ and $P_1=(1,10,19,28,37)$. The
entries $5$ and $-z$ of the progression of $Q$ are distinct, so
$5+z\in\{15,30\}$; with $z\in P_1$ this gives $z=10$. The
progression of $Q$ is therefore $(5,20,35)$, its fourth entry is
$-15=30$, and $\{b,c\}=\{20,30\}$. Thus
$T=(1,19,20,28,30,37)$, the degree-45 orbit in (3.1). It is not in
$\mathrm{Dec}$ by Lemma 7.18, since $15x\ne0$, and it is not
5-standard, because only four of its entries are congruent modulo 9.
$\square$

### 7.6. The remaining two-prime support

**Theorem 7.12.** For every $a,b\ge1$, the residual orbits at
degree $3^a5^b$ are exactly the inflation of the degree-45 seed
when $a\ge2$, and the empty set when $a=1$.

*Proof.* By content reduction, Theorem 7.11, Proposition 7.4 and
Theorem 7.3 at degree 15, only primitive degree $m=3Q$, $Q=5^b$,
$b\ge2$, remains. Use $F(s,u)$ on $U_3\times U_Q$, and
$D(u)=F(1,u)-F(-1,u)$, as in Theorem 7.8. Primitive Fourier
vanishing makes $D$ even and constant on the five-point fibres
of $U_Q\to U_{Q/5}$. Its mass is at most twelve. A nonzero
such function must therefore be
$$
 D=\sigma(\mathbf1_C+\mathbf1_{-C}),\qquad \sigma=\pm1,
 \tag{7.11}
$$
on one pair of distinct fibres, of total mass ten.

First suppose $D\ne0$. Oddness of $F$ gives
$\|F\|_1-\|D\|_1\in4\mathbf Z_{\ge0}$ (with $\alpha=F(1,u)$,
$\beta=F(-1,u)$, the contribution $|\alpha|+|\beta|-|\alpha-\beta|$
at $u$ equals that at $-u$ and is even); since
$\|F\|_1\le12$, equality holds. The five unit entries all
have the same 3-coordinate $\sigma$. Zero sum forces the sixth
entry to have that same nonzero 3-coordinate, so there is no entry
of order $Q$. Put $W(u)=F(1,u)+F(-1,u)$. It is odd,
supported on $C\cup(-C)$, and equals $\pm1$ there.
At a primitive odd character of $Q$, the balance equation gives
the Fourier coefficient of $W$ multiplied by
$1-\chi(3)$, by (7.10). This factor is nonzero: 3 generates
$U_{5^b}$, since $3^4=1+16\cdot5$, and binomial induction
gives order $4\cdot5^{b-1}$; in particular a power of 3 is
$-1$, where an odd character has value $-1$.
Thus $W$ is fibre-constant as well. Equations (7.11) and the
constant values of $W$ force all five unit entries into one
5-progression. The sextuple is exactly 5-standard.

Now let $D=0$, so $F(1,u)=F(-1,u)=v(u)$, with $v$
integer and odd. Let $G(y)=n_{3y}-n_{-3y}$ on $U_Q$.
Primitive Fourier vanishing makes
$$
 E=G+v-v(3\,\cdot)
 \tag{7.12}
$$
odd and fibre-constant, with
$\|E\|_1\le2N_Q+2N_U\le12$, where
$N_U=\|v\|_1=2r$ and $N_Q=\|G\|_1/2$.
Suppose $E\ne0$. It equals
$\sigma(\mathbf1_C-\mathbf1_{-C})$, of mass ten.
On the lattice of integer odd functions, write
$\epsilon(h)=\|h\|_1/2\pmod2$. This is additive, since it
is the sum of the coefficients modulo two on any choice of one
representative of each antipodal pair. Multiplication by 3 preserves
$\epsilon$. Hence $1=\epsilon(E)=\epsilon(G)=N_Q\pmod2$.
Thus $N_U+N_Q$ is odd and at most six, while the norm bound
forces it to be at least five. It equals five, and equality holds
in the triangle inequality for (7.12). In particular both $v$
and $v(3\,\cdot)$ are supported on $C\cup(-C)$.
But this set is disjoint from its multiple by 3: such an intersection
would require $3\equiv\pm1\pmod{5^{b-1}}$, impossible.
Therefore $v=0$, $N_Q=5$, and the sixth entry is also divisible
by 3 by zero sum. This contradicts primitivity. We have proved $E=0$.

Consequently the exact equations (7.8)--(7.9) hold with $Q=5^b$.
Their positive parts form a balanced block of length $2r+d\le6$,
as in Theorem 7.8. Primitivity excludes $v=0$. For $r=1$
the block has length four and leaves a forbidden balanced pair.
For $r=3$, invariance would require $Q\mid3^j-1$ for
$j\le3$, impossible at $Q\ge25$. For $r=2$, the
same cancellation calculation forces $v=e_z+e_{3z}$, giving
the explicit contraction of two 3-standard surface quadruples.
This is $\mathrm{Dec}$. Content reduction now proves the
asserted equality of residual sets. $\square$

It follows that every degree with at most two distinct odd prime
divisors has the exact six-seed classification. At an arbitrary odd
primitive conductor, the only remaining case is
$3\parallel m$ with at least three distinct prime divisors.
Theorem 7.11 does not assert that residuals at a degree divisible by
nine are absent: the degree-33, degree-39 or other seeds can still
inflate there. Its conclusion concerns the primitive conductor.

**Corollary 7.13 (computer verification through 2001).** The six-seed
equality holds for every odd $3\le m\le2001$. Theorem A contains this
statement; the proof below is independent of Sections 7.5--7.8.

*Proof.* Section 4 handles $m\le599$. Above this bound, the
proved infinite families leave exactly 103 odd degrees
$601\le m\le2001$ divisible by 3 and supported on
$\{3,5\}$ (the four degrees 675, 1125, 1215, 1875) or on at least
three primes (99 degrees), when the selection is made using the earlier
Theorem 7.8 alone.
The unchanged independent enumerator of the supplementary bundle
enumerated all their nonpair Hodge orbits, including nonprimitive
ones. Its output, checked by direct full-unit Python predicates,

The other degrees are covered by Theorems 7.3, 7.6, 7.8 and

### 7.7. Arbitrary prime support without five

We use the short split-function distinction of [5, Step 5 of the
odd-divisible-by-three subsection], now without assuming the absence
of a lowering move. Its simplification when 5 is absent gives a
positive classification on arbitrary prime support.

**Lemma 7.14.** Suppose $N>1$ has all prime divisors at least seven.
Let $h:U_N\to\mathbf Z$ have mass at most twelve and be split on
cosets of $\prod_{p\mid N}K_p$, where $K_p=\ker(U_N\to U_{N/p})$.
If $h$ is even and nonzero,
either $N$ is a prime $q\in\{7,11,13\}$ and $h$ is
constant, or
$$
 h=\sigma(\mathbf1_L+\mathbf1_{-L}),\qquad
 L=wK_7,\quad 7\parallel N,\quad N/7>1.
 \tag{7.13}
$$
If $h$ is odd and nonzero, its only possibility is the same
line pair with a minus sign between the indicators.

*Proof.* For prime $N$, split means constant and the assertion
follows from parity and $N-1\le12$. If $N$ is not squarefree,
opposite cosets are distinct and each has mass at most six.
Lemma 7.5 leaves only a size-six $K_7$-line, and its opposite
already consumes all twelve units of mass.

If $N$ is squarefree composite, one of its primes $p$ is
at least eleven. Slice along $U_p$, whose size is at least ten.
Opposite slices have equal mass. If all slices are nonzero, each
has mass at most two and one has mass one. Every difference from
that slice has mass at most three, hence vanishes by Lemma 7.5.
All slices would be the same signed atom. Its fixed coordinates
in the other factors are distinct from their negatives, contradicting
either parity. There is therefore a zero slice. Each other slice
is split on the remaining factors and has mass at most six. Again
Lemma 7.5 gives the stated pair of size-six lines. $\square$

**Theorem 7.15.** If $m$ is odd and $5\nmid m$, its residual
orbits are exactly the inflations of the degree-33 and two degree-39
seeds whose conductors divide $m$. There is no bound on prime
support or exponents.

*Proof.* Reduce content, discard inverse pairs, and suppose that
$T$ is residual. Theorem 7.6 excludes $3\nmid m$, and
Theorem 7.11 excludes $9\mid m$. Thus $3\parallel m$.

Every maximal entry order is divisible by 3. Indeed, a maximal
order $e$ prime to three has all primes at least seven. Its
odd function has no primitive Fourier modes, so Lemma 7.14 makes
it a pair of $K_7$-lines. All six entries then have order $e$
and lie on a positive line. Their sum modulo $e/7$ is $6w$,
impossible because $e/7>1$ is coprime to six.

Fix a maximal order $e=3N$. The case $N=1$ is impossible:
split on $U_3$ means constant, whereas its odd function is nonzero.
If an entry has order $f$ divisible
by $N$, it divides a maximal order $e'$ divisible by 3.
Then $e\mid e'$, so maximality gives $e'=e$ and
$f\in\{N,3N\}$. Thus primitive character equations at $N$
involve only these two strata. Use $F(s,u)$ and $D=F(1,\cdot)-
F(-1,\cdot)$ as before; $D$ is even and split.

Suppose $D\ne0$ and $N$ is composite. Lemma 7.14 gives
(7.13), of mass twelve, so all six entries are tops at $e$,
$m=e$, and their 3-coordinate is one common sign. Write
$N=7R$, where $R>1$ has primes at least eleven. The other
coordinates of the tops are $\varepsilon_k wk$, for
$k\in U_7$, with $\varepsilon_k=\pm1$. Zero sum modulo
$R\ge11$ forces three plus and three minus signs. Zero sum
modulo 7 then makes the plus set $\{1,2,4\}$ or
$\{3,5,6\}$, after factoring out $w$. Thus
$\varepsilon_k=\tau\lambda_7(k)$, with $\lambda_7$
the odd quadratic character of $U_7$.

There is an even primitive character $\psi$ of $R$ with
$\psi(3)\ne-1$. To see this, at each prime power factor choose
the second power of a faithful character; replacing that choice
at one factor by its fourth power preserves evenness and
primitivity, and changes its value at 3, since $9\ne1$ there.
For $\chi=\lambda_7\psi$, the primitive odd character equation
at $N$ has the nonzero factor $1-\chi(3)=1+\psi(3)$.
Its remaining sum is a nonzero scalar times
$\sum_{k\in U_7}\varepsilon_k\lambda_7(k)=6\tau$, a
contradiction. This is the seven-line argument of [5, Step 7 of
the odd-divisible-by-three subsection];
the equal sign counts here follow from zero sum and $R\ge11$.

Next suppose $D=0$, so $F(1,u)=F(-1,u)=v(u)$ is odd,
and put $G(y)=n_{(m/N)y}-n_{-(m/N)y}$ on the order-$N$
stratum. Formula (7.10), in the subgroup of order $3N$, makes
$E=G+v-v(3\,\cdot)$ odd with zero primitive Fourier modes.
Its mass is at most $2(N_{3N}+N_N)\le12$. If $E\ne0$,
Lemma 7.14 makes it a seven-line pair of mass twelve. Equality
then holds in the triangle inequality, so $v$ and
$v(3\,\cdot)$ are both supported on $L\cup(-L)$.
This set and its multiple by 3 are disjoint, because
$N/7\ge11$ cannot divide $3-1$ or $3+1$.
Hence $v=0$, contradicting the presence of tops at $3N$.
Therefore $E=0$.

The positive balanced block now has length $2r+d\le6$,
with $r=\|v\|_1/2\ge1$. The argument of Theorem 7.8
excludes $r=1$ and makes $r=2$ a surface contraction; its
invariance step uses only that a positive support of two points
stable under multiplication by 3 would force $N\mid3^j-1$ with
$j\le2$, impossible for $N\ge7$.
For $r=3$ it requires $N\mid3^j-1$ for some $j\le3$.
As the primes of $N$ are at least seven, its only possibility
is $N=13$. The positive support of $v$ is then one
multiplication-by-3 orbit of length three. Up to units the sextuple
is $(1,14,16,22,29,35)$ at 39. It exhausts $T$, so its
primitive conductor is 39.

It remains that every maximal order has nonzero $D$ and
$N=q\in\{7,11,13\}$. Their numbers of tops are at least
$(q-1)/2$, respectively three, five and six. Two distinct
such maximal orders would require at least eight entries. There
is only one, and primitivity gives $m=3q$. The complete
semiprime classification in Theorem 7.3 finishes the proof.
Inflation both preserves and reflects the positive predicates.
$\square$

Theorems 7.11, 7.12 and 7.15 leave the primitive conductors satisfying
$3\parallel m$, $5\mid m$, with at least three distinct prime
divisors. The next subsection completes the positive classification
on this domain as well.

### 7.8. Positive classification at arbitrary odd conductor

The proof treats the remaining primitive conductors, those with
$3\parallel m$, $5\mid m$ and at least three distinct prime
divisors. Its inputs are Theorem 7.6, the repeated-three reduction in
Theorem 7.11 and the two-prime results above; no finite census is used.
We first record the
small-kernel classification needed for the remaining case. As in
[5, Proposition 3.18 and Lemma 3.31], these are statements
about split functions before a no-move assumption is imposed.
The dependence is sequential: Lemma 7.16 classifies the Fourier
kernels; Lemma 7.17 uses it to classify surfaces; Lemma 7.18 applies
that surface classification to a partial five-progression; and
Lemma 7.19 proves equality of the entire lower-stratum functions.
Theorem 7.20 then treats the nonzero row difference. None of these
lemmas assumes Theorem A or any finite census.

**Lemma 7.16 (short kernels prime to six).** Let $N>1$ be prime
to six, and let $h:U_N\to\mathbf Z$ be nonzero, split on the
cosets of $\prod_{p\mid N}K_p$, of mass at most twelve.
In the line formulas below $\sigma\in\{1,-1\}$.

If $h$ is odd, either
$$
 h=\sigma(\mathbf1_L-\mathbf1_{-L}),\quad
 L=wK_p,\quad |K_p|\in\{4,5,6\},\quad L\cap(-L)=\varnothing,
 \tag{7.14}
$$
or $N=35$ and $\|h\|_1=12$.
If $h$ is even, either it has the analogous form with a plus
sign, or $N=q\in\{5,7,11,13\}$ and $h$ is constant,
or $N=35$. In the last even case, unless it is already a
line pair, its mass is twelve and it has eight values of one sign
and four of the other, all of absolute value one.

*Proof.* At prime $N$, split means constant. Oddness excludes
this case for an odd function; for an even function its mass is
$(N-1)|h(1)|\le12$, giving exactly the stated prime possibilities.

If $N$ is not squarefree, choose $p^a\parallel N$, $a\ge2$.
Every element of $H=\prod K_p$ is one modulo $p^{a-1}$,
whereas $-1$ is not. Thus a coset and its negative are distinct.
Parity pairs their masses, so each has mass at most six. The kernel
factor sizes are $p$ for repeated primes and $p-1$ otherwise;
all are at least four and only $K_5$ can have size four.
Lemma 7.5 gives precisely one signed line in each nonzero coset.
That line has mass at least four. Its opposite consumes at least four
more, so no second pair of nonzero cosets fits in mass twelve.
This proves (7.14) and its even counterpart for every exponent pattern.

If $N$ is squarefree composite with a prime $p\ge11$,
slice along $U_p$, of size at least ten. Opposite slices have equal
mass. If all are
nonzero, they have mass at most two, with at least one of mass one.
All their differences from that slice have mass at most three and
vanish by Lemma 7.5. All slices would equal one signed atom at the
same point in the other coordinates. That point differs from its
negative because at least one other odd prime factor is present.
This contradicts either parity. A zero slice therefore exists.
Every nonzero slice is split in the other factors, of mass at most
six, hence one signed line. Its opposite already consumes at least
eight units; no other pair of slices can occur.

The only remaining squarefree composite is 35: any squarefree
integer prime to six with at least three prime factors has a factor
at least eleven. For odd $h$, its matrix
on $U_5\times U_7$ is $u(a)+v(b)$, with odd potentials
simultaneously integral or half-integral. Formula (7.6) shows that
two nonzero integral potentials cost at least
$2(2+3-1)=8$ positive entries, exceeding six: one nonzero
antipodal value in each potential already occupies one row and one
column of the $2\times3$ array of maxima. One integral potential gives a single line
pair. Half-integral potentials cost at least six positive entries,
so have exactly mass twelve.

For even $h$, both potentials may be chosen even: their
differences under negation are equal constants, which change sign
under negation and hence vanish. Its values form an additive
$2\times3$ integer matrix $M$, each value repeated four
times, with $\sum|M_{ij}|\le3$. Its row difference is an
integer $\delta$. If $\delta=0$, there is one signed
nonzero column, a $K_5$-line pair. If $|\delta|\ge2$,
the three columns already cost at least six, impossible. For
$|\delta|=1$, each column is $(\delta,0)$ or
$(0,-\delta)$. Equal columns give a $K_7$-line pair;
otherwise the nonzero signs occur on eight and four points.
$\square$

We fix the row and stratum conventions for the next three arguments.
Suppose $3\parallel m$, all maximal orders are divisible by three,
and $e=3N$ is maximal. The order $e=3$ cannot occur: its only
odd character is primitive, and (7.7a) would force its nonzero odd
multiplicity function to vanish. Thus $N>1$ and $\gcd(N,6)=1$.
If an entry order $f$ is divisible by $N$, choose a maximal
order $e'$ divisible by $f$. Since $3\mid e'$, we have
$3N\mid e'$, whence $e'=e$. It follows that
$f\in\{N,3N\}$. This proves the two-stratum isolation used below.

On the order-$3N$ stratum identify an entry with
$(\sigma,u)\in U_3\times U_N$ through the subgroup embedding
$z\mapsto(m/(3N))z$, and write its odd multiplicity as $F$.
Put $D(u)=F(1,u)-F(-1,u)$ and $W(u)=F(1,u)+F(-1,u)$.
Oddness of $F$ makes $D$ even and $W$ odd.
The primitive odd characters at $3N$ are the product of the
nontrivial character at 3 with an even primitive character at $N$.
Equation (7.7a) therefore kills every primitive Fourier coefficient of
$D$, so $D$ is split on the kernel-product cosets.
If $N_e$ denotes the number of entries of order $e$, pair-freeness
gives $\|F\|_1=2N_e$. Directly on each pair $\{u,-u\}$,
$$
 \|F\|_1-\|D\|_1\in4\mathbf Z_{\ge0},\qquad
 \sum_{\operatorname{ord}(a)=3N}\sigma_a
     =\tfrac12\sum_{u\in U_N}D(u).
 \tag{7.14a}
$$
Here $\sigma_a\in U_3$ is the 3-coordinate of the entry $a$. The first
assertion follows from $|\alpha|+|\beta|-|\alpha-\beta|\in2\mathbf Z_{\ge0}$
for $\alpha=F(1,u)$, $\beta=F(-1,u)$, with an additional factor two
from $u,-u$; the second is the count of the order-$3N$ entries by
their 3-coordinates.

If $D=0$, write the two equal rows as the integer odd function
$v$, and let $G(y)=n_{(m/N)y}-n_{-(m/N)y}$ be the order-$N$
odd function. Equation (7.10) and (7.7a), with conductor $N$, give
$$
 E=G+v-v(3\,\cdot),\qquad
 \widehat E(\chi)=0\quad(\chi\text{ primitive modulo }N),
 \tag{7.14b}
$$
where $\widehat E(\chi)=\sum_{u\in U_N}E(u)\overline{\chi(u)}$.
Oddness supplies the even-character vanishings, so Lemma 7.16 applies
to $E$. Put $B_0=N_{3N}+N_N$. Then
$$
 N_{3N}=\|v\|_1\in2\mathbf Z,\qquad
 \|E\|_1\le\|G\|_1+2\|v\|_1=2B_0.
 \tag{7.14c}
$$
There are two distinct uses of this formula. If the entire tuple lies
in these strata, (7.10) gives, for every $t\in U_N$,
$$
 0=\tfrac12\sum_{u\in U_N}E(u)b_N(tu).
 \tag{7.14d}
$$
Thus the positive part of $E$ is itself balanced, since
$b_N(-x)=-b_N(x)$. If instead $E=0$ is proved as an equality
of functions, the positive parts of $F,G$ form a balanced
submultiset of the original tuple. We do not infer (7.14d) merely
from vanishing of primitive Fourier coefficients.

**Lemma 7.17 (odd surface classification).** A pair-free balanced
quadruple at odd degree is either 3-standard or has primitive
conductor 15 or 21.

*Proof.* Reduce content. If $3\nmid m$, [1, Theorem A,
$n=2$] makes the quadruple a union of inverse pairs. If
$9\mid m$, use Lemma 7.10. Assume $3\parallel m$.
A maximal order $e$ prime to three has an odd split function
of mass at most eight. Lemma 7.16 makes it a $K_5$-line pair
of mass eight. Its four positive entries have sum $4w$ modulo
$e/5>1$, impossible as $e/5$ is prime to six.
All maximal orders are therefore divisible by three.

Fix one such order $3N$. As in Theorem 7.15, only the order
strata $3N,N$ can contribute at primitive characters of $N$.
If its even row difference $D$ vanishes, put
$F(1,u)=F(-1,u)=v(u)$ and $E=G+v-v(3\,\cdot)$.
The odd error has mass at most eight. If nonzero, Lemma 7.16
makes it a four-point $K_5$-line pair and forces equality in
the norm bound. Both $v$ and $v(3\,\cdot)$ are then
supported on $L\cup(-L)$, disjoint from its multiple by 3
because $N/5>1$ is prime to six. This contradicts $v\ne0$.
Thus $E=0$. The positive balanced block has length $2r+d\le4$.
The case $r=1$ is a 3-standard quadruple exhausting the original
one; $r=2,d=0$ would require $N\mid3^j-1$ with
$j\le2$, impossible at odd $N\ge5$.

If $D\ne0$, its mass is at most eight. Lemma 7.16 leaves
either a four-point $K_5$-line pair, or a constant at
$N=5,7$. The line-pair case uses all four tops, with the same
3-coordinate, contradicting zero sum modulo 3. Thus each maximal
order is 15 or 21, requiring respectively at least two or three
tops. They cannot coexist in a quadruple. Primitivity makes the
conductor 15 or 21. $\square$

**Lemma 7.18 (four points of a five-progression).** If a primitive
pair-free residual sextuple contains four distinct points of
$P_x=\{x,x+m/5,\ldots,x+4m/5\}$, with $5x\ne0$,
then $m$ divides 45 or 105; if $m=105$, it is one of the two
degree-105 orbits in (3.1). Conversely, a pair-free sextuple that
contains four distinct points of $P_x$, with $15x\ne0$, is not
in $\mathrm{Dec}$.

*Proof.* Five points would make the tuple 5-standard. Otherwise
write $T=(P_x\smallsetminus\{z\})\sqcup\{b,c\}$.
The quadruple $Q=(5x,b,c,-z)$ is balanced, because
$\sum_{y\in P_x}b_m(ty)=b_m(5tx)$ (distribution identity over the
five lifts of $x\bmod m/5$) and $b_m(-tz)=-b_m(tz)$. Its entries
are nonzero: $5x\ne0$, and $z=0$ would give $5x=5z=0$.
It is pair-free. The pair $\{b,c\}$ is excluded because $T$ is
pair-free. The pair $\{5x,-z\}$ would give $z=5x$, hence
$5x=5z=25x$, $20x=0$ and $5x=0$ at odd degree. A pair
$\{b,-z\}$ or $\{c,-z\}$ would put $z$ back in $T$, giving five
points of $P_x$. A pair $\{5x,b\}$ leaves the balanced pair
$(c,-z)$, so $c=z$, again five points; $\{5x,c\}$ is symmetric.

If $Q$ has primitive conductor $f_Q=15$ or 21, all its
entries lie in the subgroup of order $f_Q$. Since
$x=z-jm/5$ for some $j$, every entry of $T$ lies in
the subgroup of order $\operatorname{lcm}(f_Q,5)$. Primitivity
gives $m\mid15$ or $m\mid105$.

Otherwise Lemma 7.17 makes $Q$ 3-standard. If $5x,-z$
both lie in its progression, then $3(5x+z)=0$, and
$5z=5x$ gives $90x=0$. If $5x$ is its distinguished
fourth entry, the same identities give $3z=5x$, hence
$10x=0$, impossible since $5x\ne0$. If $-z$ is
the distinguished entry, they give $z=15x$, hence
$70x=0$. In the possible cases all entries are generated by
$x,m/3,m/5$. Their group has order dividing 45 or 105,
respectively. Primitivity proves the first assertion.

Now let $m=105$. The first 3-standard case is excluded by
primitivity, since its group has order dividing 15, and the second is
impossible. If $Q$ is not 3-standard, Lemma 7.17 gives $f_Q=15$ or
21, and primitivity excludes 15. Then $Q=5Q_0$ for a pair-free
balanced quadruple $Q_0$ at degree 21 which is not 3-standard. Apply
the analysis of Theorem 7.3 at $(3,7)$ to $Q_0$, with total length
four. If $\lambda=0$, then $N_U\ge6|\mu|$ forces $\mu=0$ and
$r=1$, which makes $Q_0$ 3-standard. Hence $\lambda=1$, and
$N_U\ge3$, $d(v)\ge1$ force $N_U=3$, $d(v)=1$: the values of
$v$ along the cycle of 3 are
$\pm(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12,-\tfrac12,-\tfrac12,-\tfrac12)$ up to
cyclic shift, and $Q_0$ lies in the unit orbit of
$(1,10,15,16)$. Write $5x=5\alpha$ and $-z=5\zeta$ with
$\alpha,\zeta$ distinct entries of $Q_0$. As $z\in P_x$ means
$z\equiv x\pmod{21}$, we get $\alpha+5\zeta\equiv0\pmod{21}$. After a
unit, $Q_0=(1,10,15,16)$, and the only solution is
$(\alpha,\zeta)=(16,1)$. Thus $P_x$ is the set of lifts of 16
modulo 21, $z=100$, $\{b,c\}=\{50,75\}$, and
$T=(16,37,50,58,75,79)=16\cdot(1,22,43,64,90,95)$.
In the 3-standard case $z=15x$, and $z\equiv x\pmod{21}$
gives $3\mid x$; $5x\ne0$ gives $7\nmid x$. The data
$P_x$, $z$ and $Q$ depend only on $x\bmod21$, so after a unit
$x=3$, $z=45$, $Q=(15,50,85,60)$ and
$T=(3,24,50,66,85,87)$.

For the converse, suppose $T=B\sqcup C$ is a surface contraction.
Since $T$ is pair-free, both quadruples $Q_1=(B,-k)$ and
$Q_2=(C,k)$, with $k$ the distinguished sum, are pair-free, because a balanced quadruple containing an
inverse pair is a union of two inverse pairs. One of $B,C$, say $B$,
contains two of the four points of $P_x$, whose difference has order
five. Every point $x'$ of $P_x$ satisfies $15x'=15x\ne0$. By
Lemma 7.17, $Q_1$ is 3-standard or has primitive conductor 15 or 21.
The subgroup of order 21 contains no element of order five, and the
subgroup of order 15 is killed by 15, contrary to $15x'\ne0$. If $Q_1=\sigma_{3,y}$, two
progression entries differ by a multiple of $m/3$, so the two points
are $-3y$ and $y+jm/3$. Their difference $4y+jm/3$ has order
five, whence $20y\equiv0\pmod{m/3}$, $5y\equiv0\pmod{m/3}$ as $m$ is
odd, and $15y=0$. Then 15 kills every entry of $Q_1$, again
contradicting $15x'\ne0$. $\square$

**Lemma 7.19 (zero row difference).** Suppose $T$ is a primitive
pair-free balanced sextuple at $3\parallel m$, and every maximal entry
order is divisible by three. If its row difference vanishes at
one maximal order $3N$, then $T\in\mathrm{Dec}$ or its
primitive conductor is 39 and it is the second degree-39 seed.

*Proof.* Only orders $3N,N$ contribute at conductor $N$,
by the isolation argument above. Use $v,G,E$ in (7.14b), with
$v\ne0$ since this maximal stratum is present, and put
$B_0=N_{3N}+N_N\le6$. For an integer odd function define
$\epsilon(h)=\|h\|_1/2\pmod2$. Choosing one representative
of each antipodal pair expresses it as the sum of the coefficients
modulo two. It is therefore additive and is preserved by multiplication
by 3. Consequently
$$
 \epsilon(E)=\epsilon(G)=N_N\pmod2.
$$
Since $N_{3N}=\|v\|_1$ is even, this is exactly
$B_0\equiv\|E\|_1/2\pmod2$. Lemma 7.16 and (7.14c)
leave masses eight, ten or twelve for a nonzero $E$.

At mass eight, either $B_0=4$, giving equality in the norm
bound, or $B_0=6$. In the equality case the support of each
summand of $E=G+v-v(3\,\cdot)$ is contained in
$S=L\cup(-L)$, where $L$ is a four-point $K_5$-line.
Thus $\operatorname{supp}(v)\subset S\cap3S$. But points of
$S$ are congruent to $\pm w\pmod{N/5}$, whereas points of
$3S$ are congruent to $\pm3w$. An intersection would force
$N/5\mid2$ or $N/5\mid4$, impossible for $N/5>1$
prime to six. This contradicts $v\ne0$.
If $B_0=6$, the entire sextuple lies in the two strata, so
(7.14d) makes the positive part of $E$ a balanced quadruple entirely
in $U_N$, pair-free because $E$ is odd. This contradicts [1, Theorem A, $n=2$].
At mass ten, parity forces $B_0=5$, again giving equality and
the same support contradiction; here $5^2\mid N$, so
$N/5\ge5$. This argument does not require all entries to lie
in the two strata.
At mass twelve, $B_0=6$, and (7.14d) makes the positive part of $E$
a balanced all-unit sextuple at $N$, pair-free because $E$ is odd.
Theorem 7.6 would make it 5-standard, whose entry $-5x$ is a nonunit; this too is
impossible. Therefore $E=0$.

Since $E=0$, the positive parts form a balanced block $B\subseteq T$.
Set $r=\|v\|_1/2\ge1$ and
$d=\|v(3\,\cdot)-v\|_1/2$. Then $|B|=2r+d\le6$.
Write $e_z=\mathbf1_z-\mathbf1_{-z}$.

If $r=1$, then $v=e_z$. As $N\ge5$ and
$3\not\equiv\pm1\pmod N$, we have $d=2$ and
$|B|=4$. Its complement is a balanced pair and hence an inverse
pair, contradicting pair-freeness of $T$.

If $r=2$, a repeated atom $2e_z$ gives $d=4$, too large.
Otherwise $v=e_z+e_w$, with $z\ne\pm w$. Invariance under
multiplication by 3 would require a positive orbit of length at most
two, forcing $N\mid3^j-1$ for $j\le2$, impossible for
$N>1$ prime to six. Thus $0<d\le2$. In
$$
 v(3\,\cdot)-v=e_{3^{-1}z}+e_{3^{-1}w}-e_z-e_w
$$
the norm can drop below eight only by cancellation of two oppositely
signed atoms. Apart from the excluded self coincidences, this requires
$w=3z$ or $z=3w$; negative coincidences reinforce rather than
cancel. Relabel to obtain $v=e_z+e_{3z}$. Now
$G=e_{3^{-1}z}-e_{3z}$ has half-norm two: the two residues are
distinct, since equality would imply $N\mid8$. If they are negatives
of each other, then $N\mid10$, so $N=5$ and $G=2e_{3^{-1}z}$
still has half-norm two. The two order-$N$ entries then coincide,
which is allowed since entries may repeat. Hence $d=2$.
Take 3-standard surface
quadruples with parameters congruent to $z,3z\pmod N$, in the
subgroup of order $3N$. The distinguished entry of the first and
the progression entry divisible by 3 of the second are inverse.
Their contraction has the prescribed top rows and lower function
$G=v(3\,\cdot)-v$. More explicitly, choose
$x\equiv z\pmod N$ in $\mathbf Z/(3N)$ and put
$\kappa=m/(3N)$. The two surfaces are $\sigma_{3,x}$ and
$\sigma_{3,3x}$ at degree $3N$. Remove their entries
$-3x,3x$. The remaining multiset is
$$
 T=\kappa\{x,x+N,x+2N,3x+N,3x+2N,-9x\}.
 \tag{7.14e}
$$
Each parameter is a unit modulo $N$, so both standard tuples
are admissible and the removed entry is nonzero. Their remaining
triples have respective sums $3x,-3x$, precisely the nonzero
distinguished sums in the definition of $\mathrm{Dec}$.
The top rows are $e_z+e_{3z}$, and the lower function is
$e_{3^{-1}z}-e_{3z}$. These functions already have positive
mass six, so no further inverse cancellation occurs. The multiset
is therefore $T$, proving $T\in\mathrm{Dec}$.

If $r=3$, then $d=0$. The positive support of $v$ is a
union of multiplication-by-3 orbits of total multiplicity three.
Every point is a unit, so an orbit of size $j\le3$ implies
$N\mid3^j-1$. The only divisor greater than one prime to six
among these three integers is 13. Hence $N=13$, and the positive
support is one orbit of length three. Up to units, take
$v=e_1+e_3+e_9$; its two equal positive rows give
$(1,14,16,22,29,35)$ at 39. This block exhausts $T$, so
primitivity makes its conductor 39. $\square$

**Theorem 7.20 (unrestricted positive classification).** For every
odd degree, the residual orbits outside
$\mathrm{Dec}\cup\mathrm{Std}_5$ are exactly the inflations
of the six seeds in (3.1).

We shall use one elementary local character fact in its proof. At an
odd prime power $p^s$, take a faithful character $\theta$ of the
cyclic unit group. For $p\ge7$, the powers $\theta,\theta^3$
are two primitive odd characters, and $\theta^2,\theta^4$ are two
primitive even characters. The same is true at $5^s$ for $s\ge2$.
Indeed, at prime level these powers are nonprincipal; at higher level
their exponents are prime to $p$, so restriction to the last
$p$-kernel is nontrivial. The two values at 3 in either pair differ:
equality would give $\theta(9)=1$, whence $9=1\pmod{p^s}$,
which is impossible. At $5^1$ there is just one primitive even
character, the quadratic character, whose value at 3 is $-1$.
Products of the local primitive characters are primitive at the product
modulus. In particular, at a modulus greater than one whose primes are at least seven,
either prescribed parity has at least two possible values at 3.

*Proof.* Content reduction reflects both predicates, so it suffices to
determine the primitive residual orbits. Theorems 7.3, 7.6, 7.8,
7.11, 7.12 and 7.15 and Proposition 7.4 settle every primitive conductor
with at most two distinct prime divisors, every conductor prime to five
and every conductor divisible by nine. Let $T$ be a primitive residual
at any remaining degree $m$. Then $3\parallel m$, $5\mid m$, and
$m$ has at least three distinct prime divisors; in particular
$m\ge105$ and $m\nmid45$. By Lemma 7.18, if $T$ contains four
points of a 5-progression with nonzero distinguished entry, then
$m=105$ and $T$ is one of the two degree-105 orbits in (3.1). We
show that every other configuration is impossible.

Every maximal order is divisible by three. At a maximal order
$e$ prime to three, Lemma 7.16 gives either a positive
$K_5$-line of length four or five, or mass twelve (a $K_7$-line
pair or the exceptional case $e=35$). Four points are
the case just settled, and five points make $T$ exactly 5-standard
by zero sum. The full-mass alternative consumes the entire sextuple in the
subgroup of order $e$, so primitivity would give $m=e$,
contradicting $3\mid m$.

For a maximal order $e=3N$, Lemma 7.19 excludes $D=0$.
Apply the even part of Lemma 7.16 to its nonzero $D$.
Equations (7.14a) give the possible top counts and their sum modulo 3.

A $K_7$-line pair has mass twelve, so all six entries are
tops, and $m=3N$. Write $N=7R$, $R>1$ prime to
42. Their other coordinates are $\varepsilon_kwk$,
$k\in U_7$. Zero sum modulo $R$, which is prime to six,
forces three plus and three minus signs: the possible differences
are $-6,-4,-2,0,2,4,6$. Zero sum modulo 7 then gives
$\varepsilon_k=\tau\lambda_7(k)$. If $R=5$, so that $m=105$,
apply (7.7a) at the conductor 7 with $\chi=\lambda_7$. All six entries
have order 105, and the Euler factor is
$(1-\lambda_7(3))(1-\lambda_7(5))=4$, since 3 and 5 are not squares
modulo 7. As $\lambda_7$ is odd, $\lambda_7(\varepsilon_kwk)=\tau\lambda_7(w)$
for every $k$, and the relation reads $24\tau\lambda_7(w)/\varphi(105)=0$,
a contradiction. If $R\ne5$, there is
an even primitive $\psi$ on $R$ with $\psi(3)\ne-1$.
Indeed choose even primitive characters at its prime powers;
at any factor $p^s$ other than $5^1$, that is, with
$p\ne5$ or $s\ge2$, the second and fourth powers of a faithful
character remain primitive and give different values at 3.
The primitive character $\lambda_7\psi$ gives the same
nonzero relation as in Theorem 7.15, a contradiction.
The exceptional even matrix at $N=35$ also has mass twelve;
all six entries are then tops and $m=105$. Each entry is congruent
modulo 3 to its 3-coordinate $\sigma_a$, and by (7.14a)
$\sum_a\sigma_a=\tfrac12\sum_uD(u)=\tfrac12(8-4)(\pm1)=\pm2$. This
contradicts the zero sum of $T$ modulo 3.

Consider a $K_5$-line pair of mass ten, where $5^2\mid N$.
There are exactly five tops, all with the same 3-coordinate
$\sigma$. Zero sum puts the sixth entry in the subgroup of
order $3N$, so primitivity gives $m=3N$.
The remaining entry also has 3-coordinate
$\sigma$, so no entry has order $N$, and the only entries whose
orders are divisible by $N$ are the five tops. Write their other
coordinates as $\varepsilon_jwk_j$, $j=0,\ldots,4$,
on the five-point line. A primitive character at $N$ restricts
nontrivially to $K_5$. Unless all $\varepsilon_j$ agree,
$\sum_j\varepsilon_j\zeta_5^{aj}\ne0$, with $\zeta_5=e^{2\pi i/5}$, for every
$a\not\equiv0\pmod5$, by the minimal polynomial
$1+X+\cdots+X^4$. Choose a primitive odd character $\chi$
of $N$ with $\chi(3)\ne1$. Such a choice exists by
varying the character at the repeated 5-factor between powers
1 and 3, or 2 and 4, of a faithful character, keeping the required
parity; the values differ since $9\ne1$ modulo that factor.
Its conductor-$N$ balance equation is then nonzero, impossible.
All signs therefore agree, giving a full positive 5-progression
and hence a 5-standard sextuple.

The remaining composite possibility is a $K_5$-line pair
of mass eight, with $N=5R$, $R>1$ prime to 30.
It has four or six tops. Six would give their sum
$4\sigma\ne0\pmod3$, so there are four. No distinct
maximal order can coexist: another composite needs at least four
tops; a prime case $3q$, $q\ne5$, needs at least three;
and $15$ already divides this order. Thus $m=3N$, and
there are two other entries. At most one has order $N$: the four
tops contribute $4\sigma$ to the 3-coordinate sum, so the other two
3-coordinates sum to $-\sigma$ modulo 3, and two zero coordinates
are impossible.

Write the four tops as $(\sigma,\varepsilon_kwk)$,
$k\in U_5$, and put
$A(\alpha)=\sum_{k\in U_5}\varepsilon_k\overline{\alpha(k)}$.
For a primitive odd $\chi=\alpha\psi$ at $N$, with
$\alpha\ne1$ modulo 5 and $\psi$ primitive modulo
$R$, its equation is
$$
 (1-\chi(3))\overline{\chi(w)}A(\alpha)
       +2\sum_{\operatorname{ord}(b)=N}\overline{\chi(z_b)}=0.
 \tag{7.15}
$$
Indeed (7.7a) involves only orders $3N,N$. Their denominators
are $2\varphi(N),\varphi(N)$; the former also has the Euler
factor $1-\chi(3)$. Multiply by $2\varphi(N)$, and use
$\overline{\chi(\varepsilon_kwk)}
=\varepsilon_k\overline{\chi(w)}\overline{\alpha(k)}$.
This explains both the factor two and the absence of a 3-coordinate
sign in (7.15).
If there is no order-$N$ entry and the signs are not constant,
choose $\alpha$ with $A(\alpha)\ne0$. Primitive
characters of $R$ of either prescribed parity take at least
two different values at 3: at a prime power factor, the first
and third faithful powers, or the second and fourth, are primitive
and have different values. Choose $\psi$ with
$\psi(3)\ne\alpha(3)^{-1}$. Equation (7.15) fails.

If there is one order-$N$ entry, a two-plus/two-minus sign
pattern has $A(\alpha)=0$ at some nontrivial $\alpha$,
again contradicting (7.15). Indeed, write the two positive positions
as $g^i,g^j$ for a generator of $U_5$. If $i-j$ is odd,
the quadratic character has opposite values there; if $i-j=2$,
either quartic character does. The full nontrivial character sum is
zero, so in both cases $A$ is twice this zero two-term sum.
For a three-against-one pattern with exceptional position $k_0$,
$A(\alpha)=\pm2\overline{\alpha(k_0)}$, and hence
$|A(\alpha)|=2$ for every nontrivial $\alpha$.
Fix an even primitive $\psi$ and use the two quartic
characters modulo 5, whose values at 3 are $i,-i$.
Equation (7.15) would require both
$|1-i\psi(3)|=|1+i\psi(3)|=1$, whereas the sum of their
squares is four. Thus the signs are constant in every possible
case. The four tops are four points of one 5-progression, the case
settled by Lemma 7.18.

Only prime cases $N=q\in\{5,7,11,13\}$ remain. Their
minimal top counts are respectively two, three, five and six.
A single maximal order would give $m=3q$, which has two prime
divisors. Every pair of distinct maximal orders other than 15 and 21
requires at least seven entries. Suppose, finally, that 15 and 21 are
both maximal, so that $m=105$ and every entry order divides 15 or 21.
Let $q\in\{5,7\}$. The row difference $D$ at $3q$ is a nonzero
constant $c_q$, and $N_{3q}\ge\tfrac12(q-1)|c_q|$. If
$|c_q|\ge2$, then $N_{15}+N_{21}\ge7$. Otherwise $D=\pm1$
everywhere, and $W=F(1,\cdot)+F(-1,\cdot)\equiv D\pmod2$ is an odd
function on $U_q$ without zeros. With
$\widehat W(\chi)=\sum_uW(u)\overline{\chi(u)}$, some odd character
$\chi$ modulo $q$ has $\widehat W(\chi)\ne0$, and $\chi(3)\ne1$,
because 3 generates $U_q$ and $\chi(-1)=-1$. The entries whose
orders are divisible by $q$ have orders $3q$ and $q$; the tops
contribute $\tfrac12\widehat W(\chi)$ to the character sum, as
$\chi$ is odd. Multiplying (7.7a) at the conductor $q$ by
$\varphi(3q)=2\varphi(q)$ gives
$$
 \tfrac12(1-\chi(3))\widehat W(\chi)
   +2\sum_{\operatorname{ord}(b)=q}\overline{\chi(z_b)}=0 .
$$
Hence $N_q\ge1$. The strata $\{15,5\}$ and $\{21,7\}$ are
disjoint, so $T$ would have at least $(2+1)+(3+1)=7$ entries, a
contradiction.

Thus the primitive residuals are among the six seeds. Conversely, the
six seeds are pair-free by inspection and balanced. At 33 and 39 their
odd functions are given by (7.2)--(7.3) in Theorem 7.3; substituting
(7.2)--(7.3) into the balance equations and applying (7.4), as in the
proof of Lemma 7.1, makes every correlation vanish. At 45 and 105,
(3.4)--(3.5) express each seed, after adding two inverse pairs, as the
union of a 5-standard tuple and a surface Hodge tuple. The seeds lie outside $\mathrm{Dec}\cup\mathrm{Std}_5$: at 33 and 39 by
Theorem 7.3, at 45 by Theorem 7.11, and at 105 by Lemma 7.18, since
$15x\ne0$ for both progressions and only four entries of either tuple
are congruent modulo 21. The two degree-105 orbits are distinct, since
the first has four unit entries and the second none. Reflection of both
positive predicates shows that every listed seed inflation remains
residual; surjectivity on units preserves the distinction between the
two seeds at each common conductor. $\square$

The proof uses neither a bounded conductor hypothesis nor a premise
that a profile-lowering move preserves positive decomposition. The
four-against-one and three-against-one distinctions are resolved in
the positive tuple itself. The finite enumerations of Section 4 and
Corollary 7.13 are independent checks of the resulting classification.

**Proposition 7.21 (independence from the finite census).** The proof
of Theorem A in Sections 2, 3 and 7 uses no computer enumeration; in
particular it does not use Proposition 4.1.

*Proof.* By content reduction and Lemma 2.1 it suffices to determine
the primitive residual orbits at each odd conductor $m$. We list where
each case is settled.

(a) $\gcd(m,6)=1$: Theorem 7.6. Its proof uses Theorem 7.3 only
at $(p,q)=(5,7)$, whose case $p=5$ is settled by Lemma 7.2 and
the argument following it.

(b) $m$ a prime power: Proposition 7.4, whose prime case is
[1, Lemma 2.3].

(c) $9\mid m$: Theorem 7.11, which identifies the degree-45 seed and
uses Lemma 7.18 for its non-decomposability.

(d) $m=3^a5^b$ with $a=1$: Theorem 7.12, through Theorem 7.3 at 15.

(e) $m=3^aq^b$ with $q\ge7$: Theorem 1.3, through Proposition 7.4,
Theorem 7.8 and Theorem 7.3, which treats the degrees 15, 21, 33, 39
by the potential equations (7.6a).

(f) $5\nmid m$, $3\parallel m$, at least three prime divisors:
Theorem 7.15. Its exits through Theorem 7.3 at $m=3q$,
$q\in\{7,11,13\}$, and through the second degree-39 seed have two
prime divisors, so they do not occur under these hypotheses.

(g) $3\parallel m$, $5\mid m$, at least three prime divisors:
Theorem 7.20. Its exits are a 5-standard tuple; Lemma 7.18, giving
$m\mid45$ or $m\mid105$; Lemma 7.19, giving $\mathrm{Dec}$ or
conductor 39; and the prime cases, which give $m=3q\le39$, at least
seven entries, or, when 15 and 21 are both maximal, a contradiction by
(7.7a). Under these hypotheses the
only conductor reached is $m=105$, where Lemma 7.18 identifies the
two seeds.

The cases (a)--(g) exhaust the odd conductors. From Section 4 the cited
results use only content reduction and the reflection of both
predicates under inflation, which involve no computation; none uses
Proposition 4.1 or another census. $\square$

The proof dependencies in Section 7 are summarized below, where each
arrow points from a proved input to the result whose proof invokes it
and (7.7a) denotes the conductor Fourier relation in Section 7.4:

- Lemmas 7.1 and 7.2 $\to$ Theorem 1.2 and Theorem 7.3 ($m=pq$).
- Lemma 7.5 and Theorem 7.3 (at $m=35$) $\to$ Theorem 7.6 ($\gcd(m,6)=1$).
- Lemma 7.7 and (7.7a) $\to$ Theorem 7.8 ($m=3^a q^b$, $q\ge7$, $\max(a,b)>1$).
- Proposition 7.4 ($m=p^a$), Theorem 7.3 and Theorem 7.8 $\to$ Theorem 1.3.
- Lemma 7.5 and (7.7a) $\to$ Lemmas 7.9 and 7.10 $\to$ Theorem 7.11 ($9\mid m$, using Lemma 7.18 for non-decomposability at $45$).
- Proposition 7.4, Theorem 7.3 (at $m=15$), Theorem 7.11 and (7.7a) $\to$ Theorem 7.12 ($m=3^a 5^b$).
- Lemma 7.5 $\to$ Lemma 7.14; Theorems 7.3, 7.6, 7.11, Lemma 7.14 and (7.7a) $\to$ Theorem 7.15 ($5\nmid m$).
- Lemma 7.5 and Lemma 7.1 $\to$ Lemma 7.16 (short kernels prime to six).
- Lemma 7.10, Lemma 7.16 and (7.7a) $\to$ Lemma 7.17 (odd surface classification).
- Lemma 7.17 and Theorem 7.3 (at $m=21$) $\to$ Lemma 7.18 (four points of a $5$-progression).
- Theorem 7.6, Lemma 7.16 and (7.7a) $\to$ Lemma 7.19 (zero row difference $D=0$).
- Proposition 7.4, Theorems 7.3, 7.6, 7.8, 7.11, 7.12, 7.15, Lemmas 7.16, 7.18, 7.19 and (7.7a) $\to$ Theorem 7.20 and Theorem A (compiled in Proposition 7.21; Theorem 1.4 combines Theorems 7.3, 7.8, 7.11, 7.12, 7.15 and Proposition 7.4).
