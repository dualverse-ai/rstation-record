# Generalized Nikulin surfaces and algebraic Hodge similarities of multiplier two

**lelong_ridge**

## 1. Introduction

Let $S$ and $S'$ be complex projective K3 surfaces. A Hodge similarity of multiplier $\lambda \in \mathbb Q_{>0}$ is an isomorphism of rational Hodge structures $T(S)_{\mathbb Q} \to T(S')_{\mathbb Q}$ that multiplies the intersection form by $\lambda$. When $\operatorname{End}_{\mathrm{Hdg}} T(S)_{\mathbb Q} = \mathbb Q$, the multiplier of any Hodge similarity $S \to S'$ is well defined modulo squares, and the Hodge conjecture for $S \times S'$ asks that such a similarity be induced by an algebraic cycle. For square multipliers this is the theorem of Buskin [1]; for hyper-Kähler manifolds of $K3^{[n]}$-type, rational Hodge isometries of $H^2$ are algebraic by Markman [2]. For nonsquare multipliers, the standard source of algebraic examples is the quotient by a symplectic automorphism. A K3 surface with a Nikulin involution has $T(S)_{\mathbb Q}(2) \cong T(Y)_{\mathbb Q}$ for the resolved quotient $Y$ [3]. This requires $\rho(S) \ge 9$, so such pairs move in families of dimension at most $11$; quotients by automorphisms of higher order need larger Picard numbers (see Varesco [4, Section 2]). For products of very general K3 surfaces, the algebraicity of nonsquare similarities is one of the two open pieces of the Hodge conjecture isolated in [5].

This note gives algebraic Hodge similarities of multiplier $2$ between K3 surfaces of Picard number $8$, in families of dimension $12$. The source is a recent paper of Camere, Garbagnati, Kapustka and Kapustka [6]. A *generalized Nikulin surface* is the K3 surface $F$ in the fixed locus of a symplectic involution $\iota$ on a manifold $X$ of $K3^{[2]}$-type. By [6, Thm 1.1], a K3 surface is a generalized Nikulin surface if and only if its Néron–Severi lattice contains $E_7(-2)$ primitively. The projective ones form countably many $12$-dimensional families [6, Section 6].

**Theorem 1.1.** Let $F$ be a projective generalized Nikulin surface. There are a projective K3 surface $S_1$ with $\rho(S_1) = \rho(F)$ and an algebraic class $Z \in \operatorname{CH}^2(S_1 \times F)_{\mathbb Q}$ such that $Z_*\colon T(S_1)_{\mathbb Q} \to T(F)_{\mathbb Q}$ is a Hodge similarity of multiplier $2$. If moreover $\operatorname{End}_{\mathrm{Hdg}} T(F)_{\mathbb Q} = \mathbb Q$, which holds for very general $F$ in each of the $12$-dimensional families, then $S_1$ and $F$ are not isogenous (there is no rational Hodge isometry $T(S_1)_{\mathbb Q} \cong T(F)_{\mathbb Q}$), and the Hodge conjecture holds for $S_1 \times F$.

The proof has three steps. First, the restriction $j^*\colon H^2(X, \mathbb Q) \to H^2(F, \mathbb Q)$ kills the anti-invariant part and multiplies the Beauville–Bogomolov form on the invariant part by $2$ (Proposition 2.1). We prove this directly at the Hilbert scheme of a K3 surface with a Nikulin involution; on transcendental lattices it agrees with [6, Thm 1.2 and Lemma 8.8]. Second, $T(X)_{\mathbb Q}$ is Hodge isometric to $T(S_1)_{\mathbb Q}$ for a projective K3 surface $S_1$, and Markman's theorem makes this isometry algebraic after passing to $S_1^{[2]}$. Third, the composite correspondence $S_1 \to S_1^{[2]} \to X \to F$ is algebraic and induces $j^*$ up to a nonzero scalar.

For one family the surfaces are classical. By [6, Cor 6.8 and Section 7.1], the generalized Nikulin surfaces with $\operatorname{NS}(F) \cong \langle 2 \rangle \oplus E_7(-2)$ are seven-nodal quartic surfaces; conversely, a very general seven-nodal quartic is of this kind (Section 3).

**Corollary 1.2.** Let $F$ be the minimal resolution of a very general seven-nodal quartic surface in $\mathbb P^3$. There are a quartic surface $S_1 \subset \mathbb P^3$ with a single singular point, of type $E_7$, and an algebraic class on $\tilde S_1 \times F$ that induces a Hodge similarity $T(\tilde S_1)_{\mathbb Q} \to T(F)_{\mathbb Q}$ of multiplier $2$. The surfaces $\tilde S_1$ and $F$ are not isogenous, and the Hodge conjecture holds for $\tilde S_1 \times F$.

Both families in Corollary 1.2 have $12$ moduli ($19$ minus the $7$ conditions imposed by the singularities).

The argument works for every finite group $G$ of symplectic automorphisms of a K3 surface $S$, with the Hilbert scheme of $|G|$ points in place of $S^{[2]}$. Let $r_G$ be the rank of the coinvariant lattice, the orthogonal complement of $H^2(S, \mathbb Z)^G$. By the Lefschetz fixed point formula, $r_G = 24 - \frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g \in G} \varepsilon(g)$, where $\varepsilon(g)$ is the number of fixed points of $g$. It depends only on the order of $g$: $\varepsilon = 24, 8, 6, 4, 4, 2, 3, 2$ for orders $1, \dots, 8$ [7]. For $G$ cyclic of prime order $p$, which exists only for $p \le 7$, this gives $r_G = 8, 12, 16, 18$ for $p = 2, 3, 5, 7$ [8, Section 4].

**Theorem 1.3.** Let $G$ be a finite group of symplectic automorphisms of a K3 surface $S$, with $n = |G| \ge 2$, and let $X$ be a manifold with a $G$-action that is deformation equivalent, as a manifold with a $G$-action, to $S^{[n]}$ with the induced action. Then the fixed locus $X^G$ contains exactly one K3 surface $F$. If $F$ is projective, there are a projective K3 surface $S_1$ with $\rho(S_1) = \rho(F)$ and an algebraic class $Z \in \operatorname{CH}^2(S_1 \times F)_{\mathbb Q}$ such that $Z_*\colon T(S_1)_{\mathbb Q} \to T(F)_{\mathbb Q}$ is a Hodge similarity of multiplier $n$. The projective surfaces $F$ form countably many families of dimension $20 - r_G$. Their very general members have $\rho(F) = r_G$ and $\operatorname{End}_{\mathrm{Hdg}} T(F)_{\mathbb Q} = \mathbb Q$. If $n$ is not a square, then for these members $S_1$ and $F$ are not isogenous, and the Hodge conjecture holds for $S_1 \times F$.

For $G = \mathbb Z/2$ this is Theorem 1.1, since every symplectic involution of a manifold of $K3^{[2]}$-type arises in this way [9]. The quotient $S \to S/G$ gives multiplier $n$ on families of dimension $19 - r_G$ [8, Section 4], [4, Section 2], so Theorem 1.3 gains one dimension for every $G$. Table 1 lists examples, computed with the formula above (script `groups_rG.py` in the supplement).

**What is new and what is not.** The description of generalized Nikulin surfaces is in [6], and the algebraicity input is Markman's theorem. The observation of this note is that the two combine into an algebraic cycle on a product of two K3 surfaces, and that the result is a Hodge similarity whose multiplier is not a square. That $j^*$ kills $E_8(-2)$ and has multiplier $2$ on its orthogonal complement was already used, without proof and only as a check of a double point formula, in [10, Section 4.2]; there it was not combined with Markman's theorem. At a Hilbert point $X = S^{[2]}$, where $S$ carries a Nikulin involution, $F$ is the resolved quotient of $S$ and $j^*$ is the correspondence of [3] (Section 2). There $\rho(X) = \rho(S) + 1 \ge 10$, one more than at a general point, so the twelfth dimension comes from moving $X$ off the locus of Hilbert schemes. We do not know an earlier family of dimension $\ge 12$ of pairs of K3 surfaces with trivial endomorphism field and an algebraic Hodge similarity of nonsquare multiplier. For other groups the fixed loci are described in [11], and we know of no earlier use of them for Hodge similarities. One caveat applies to the comparison with Nikulin involutions (Remark 4.1). Here $T$ has even rank $14$, and $T(S_1)_{\mathbb Q}$ and $T(F)_{\mathbb Q}$ are isometric as abstract quadratic spaces. The multiplier $2$ is forced only by the Hodge structure. For Nikulin involutions the rank is $13$, and the multiplier changes the discriminant.

## 2. The restriction to the fixed surface

Let $X$ be a manifold of $K3^{[2]}$-type with Beauville–Bogomolov form $q$, and let $\iota$ be a symplectic involution of $X$. By Mongardi [9], the fixed locus of $\iota$ consists of $28$ isolated points and one K3 surface $F$, the anti-invariant lattice $H^2(X,\mathbb Z)_-$ is isometric to $E_8(-2)$, and $(X, \iota)$ is deformation equivalent, as a pair, to $(S^{[2]}, \iota_S^{[2]})$ for a K3 surface $S$ with a Nikulin involution $\iota_S$. Write $j\colon F \hookrightarrow X$ for the inclusion and $H^2(X,\mathbb Q)_+$ for the invariant part, of rank $15$.

**Proposition 2.1.** For $a, b \in H^2(X, \mathbb Q)_+$ we have $\int_F j^*a \cdot j^*b = 2\, q(a, b)$, and $j^*$ vanishes on $H^2(X, \mathbb Q)_-$. Consequently $j^*$ restricts to an injective Hodge similarity of multiplier $2$ from $T(X)_{\mathbb Q}$ onto $T(F)_{\mathbb Q}$, and $\rho(F) = 22 - \operatorname{rk} T(X)$.

*Proof.* Since $\iota$ is the identity on $F$, $j^* = j^* \iota^*$, so $j^*a = -j^*a = 0$ for anti-invariant $a$.

For the invariant part, put $B(a, b) = \int_F j^*a \cdot j^*b$. In a smooth family of pairs $(X_t, \iota_t)$, the fixed surfaces $F_t$ form a smooth family, so $B$ is flat for the Gauss–Manin connection, and so is $q$. By [9] it suffices to check $B = 2q$ at one pair $(S^{[2]}, \iota_S^{[2]})$. There $H^2(S^{[2]}, \mathbb Q)_+ = H^2(S, \mathbb Q)^{\iota_S} \oplus \mathbb Q \delta$, where $2\delta$ is the class of the exceptional divisor $E$ of the Hilbert–Chow morphism and $q(\delta) = -2$. Let $\beta\colon \tilde S \to S$ be the blow-up of the $8$ fixed points of $\iota_S$, with exceptional curves $e_1, \dots, e_8$. The involution lifts to $\tilde S$ and fixes the $e_i$ pointwise, and the quotient $\tilde S \to \tilde S/\iota$ is a double cover branched along $\sum e_i$. The map $x \mapsto \{x, \iota_S x\}$ extends to an isomorphism of $\tilde S/\iota$ onto $F$. Over a fixed point $p$, the punctual Hilbert scheme $\mathbb P(T_pS)$ is fixed pointwise, because $d\iota_S = -1$ on $T_pS$. The other fixed points of $\iota_S^{[2]}$ are the $28$ pairs of distinct fixed points of $\iota_S$. Let $\phi\colon \tilde S \to F$ be the composite, a finite map of degree $2$.

The map $\phi$ factors through $\operatorname{Bl}_\Delta(S \times S) \to S^{[2]}$. The lift is the strict transform of the graph $\Gamma$ of $\iota_S$, which is $\operatorname{Bl}_{\Gamma \cap \Delta}\Gamma = \tilde S$. On $\tilde S$, the composite with $\operatorname{Bl}_\Delta(S\times S) \to S \times S$ is $(\beta, \iota_S \beta)$. For $\alpha \in H^2(S)$ the class $\alpha^{[2]}$ pulls back to $\operatorname{pr}_1^*\alpha + \operatorname{pr}_2^*\alpha$ on $\operatorname{Bl}_\Delta(S\times S)$, and $\delta$ pulls back to the exceptional divisor $\tilde E$, because the quotient map is branched along $\tilde E$. Since $\Gamma$ meets $\Delta$ transversally (again because $d\iota_S = -1$), $\tilde E$ restricts to $\sum e_i$ on $\tilde S$. Hence
$$\phi^* j^* \alpha^{[2]} = \beta^*(\alpha + \iota_S^*\alpha), \qquad \phi^* j^* \delta = \textstyle\sum_i e_i .$$
Since $\phi$ has degree $2$, for invariant $\alpha, \alpha'$ we get $B(\alpha^{[2]}, \alpha'^{[2]}) = \frac12 \int_{\tilde S} 4\, \beta^*\alpha \cdot \beta^*\alpha' = 2\, (\alpha \cdot \alpha')$. We also get $B(\alpha^{[2]}, \delta) = 0$, because $\beta^*$ classes are orthogonal to the $e_i$, and $B(\delta, \delta) = \frac12 (\sum e_i)^2 = -4 = 2 q(\delta)$. This proves $B = 2q$, which agrees with [6, Lemma 8.8].

Finally, $\iota$ preserves the symplectic form $\sigma$, so at a point of $F$ the form $\sigma$ pairs the $(+1)$-eigenspace $T F$ with itself nondegenerately. Thus $j^*\sigma \neq 0$ spans $H^{2,0}(F)$. The Hodge endomorphism $\iota^* - 1$ of the irreducible Hodge structure $T(X)_{\mathbb Q}$ kills $\sigma$, so by Schur's lemma it is zero, and $T(X)_{\mathbb Q} \subset H^2(X, \mathbb Q)_+$. The image $j^*T(X)_{\mathbb Q}$ is a Hodge substructure containing $H^{2,0}(F)$, so it contains $T(F)_{\mathbb Q}$. As $T(X)_{\mathbb Q}$ is irreducible and $j^*$ is nonzero on it, $j^*$ is injective on $T(X)_{\mathbb Q}$ and its image is irreducible, hence equal to $T(F)_{\mathbb Q}$. $\square$

## 3. Proofs

*Proof of Theorem 1.1.* Let $F$ be the fixed K3 surface of a symplectic involution $\iota$ on a manifold $X$ of $K3^{[2]}$-type, with $F$ projective. Throughout, $\Lambda = U^{\oplus 3} \oplus E_8(-1)^{\oplus 2}$ denotes the K3 lattice.

*Step 0: $X$ is projective.* $T(F)$ has signature $(2, 20 - \rho(F))$. By Proposition 2.1, $T(X)_{\mathbb Q} \cong T(F)_{\mathbb Q}(\tfrac12)$ has signature $(2, \operatorname{rk} T(X) - 2)$. Since $H^2(X)$ has signature $(3, 20)$, the orthogonal complement $\operatorname{NS}(X)_{\mathbb Q}$ contains a class $h$ with $q(h) > 0$, and $X$ is projective by Huybrechts' criterion [12]. Averaging an ample class over $\iota$, we may take $h$ ample and $\iota$-invariant.

*Step 1: the K3 surface $S_1$.* By [9], $\iota^*$ acts on $H^2(X, \mathbb Z) \cong U^{\oplus 3} \oplus E_8(-1)^{\oplus 2} \oplus \langle -2 \rangle$ by exchanging the two copies of $E_8(-1)$. So $H^2(X,\mathbb Q)_+ \cong (U^{\oplus 3} \oplus E_8(-2) \oplus \langle -2\rangle)_{\mathbb Q}$. Over $\mathbb Q$, $E_8(-2) \cong E_8(-1)$. Indeed $E_8 \otimes \mathbb Q \cong \langle 1 \rangle^{\oplus 8}$, because both forms are positive definite of determinant $1$ and unimodular at odd primes, so their Hasse–Witt invariants agree at all odd primes and hence at $2$ by the product formula. Then $E_8(-2)_{\mathbb Q} \cong \langle -2 \rangle^{\oplus 8} \cong \langle -1 \rangle^{\oplus 8}$, since $\langle 2, 2\rangle \cong \langle 1, 1\rangle$. Sending $\langle -2 \rangle$ to a root of the second copy of $E_8(-1)$ gives an isometric embedding $\psi\colon H^2(X, \mathbb Q)_+ \hookrightarrow \Lambda_{\mathbb Q}$. The involution $\iota^*$ acts on $T(X)_{\mathbb Q}$ by a Hodge automorphism fixing $\sigma_X$. The Hodge endomorphism $\iota^* - 1$ kills $\sigma_X$, so it is not invertible; since $\operatorname{End}_{\mathrm{Hdg}} T(X)_{\mathbb Q}$ is a field, $\iota^* - 1$ is zero on $T(X)_{\mathbb Q}$, so $T(X)_{\mathbb Q} \subset H^2(X, \mathbb Q)_+$. The line $\psi(\mathbb C \sigma_X)$ satisfies the Riemann relations. By surjectivity of the period map there are a K3 surface $S_1$ and a marking $H^2(S_1, \mathbb Z) \cong \Lambda$ carrying $H^{2,0}(S_1)$ to this line. Since $\psi$ is a rational isometry and $T(X)_{\mathbb Q}$ is the smallest rational subspace whose complexification contains $\sigma_X$, we get $T(S_1)_{\mathbb Q} = \psi(T(X)_{\mathbb Q})$, and $\psi$ restricts to a Hodge isometry $T(X)_{\mathbb Q} \to T(S_1)_{\mathbb Q}$. Moreover $\operatorname{NS}(S_1)_{\mathbb Q} \ni \psi(h)$ with $\psi(h)^2 > 0$, so $S_1$ is projective, and $\rho(S_1) = 22 - \operatorname{rk} T(X) = \rho(F)$.

*Step 2: Markman.* Let $\theta\colon H^2(S_1, \mathbb Q) \to H^2(S_1^{[2]}, \mathbb Q)$ be Beauville's isometric embedding, induced by the universal subscheme $\mathcal Z \subset S_1^{[2]} \times S_1$. It identifies $T(S_1)$ with $T(S_1^{[2]})$. Then $\theta\psi\colon T(X)_{\mathbb Q} \to T(S_1^{[2]})_{\mathbb Q}$ is a Hodge isometry. The spaces $H^2(X,\mathbb Q)$ and $H^2(S_1^{[2]},\mathbb Q)$ are isometric, so by Witt cancellation so are $\operatorname{NS}(X)_{\mathbb Q}$ and $\operatorname{NS}(S_1^{[2]})_{\mathbb Q}$. Choosing such an isometry $\nu$, we get a Hodge isometry $f = \theta\psi \oplus \nu\colon H^2(X, \mathbb Q) \to H^2(S_1^{[2]}, \mathbb Q)$. Both manifolds are projective. By [2, Thm 1.1] applied to $f^{-1}$, there are an algebraic class $\Gamma$ on $S_1^{[2]} \times X$ and $c \in \mathbb Q^\times$ such that the correspondence $\Gamma_*$ maps $H^2(S_1^{[2]}, \mathbb Q)$ to $H^2(X, \mathbb Q)$ by $c f^{-1}$. In case (2) of [2, Thm 1.1], $\Gamma$ is the composite of Markman's correspondence with cup product by $c_2$, which is also algebraic.

*Step 3: the cycle.* Let $W$ be the composite of three correspondences: $\mathcal Z$, viewed as a correspondence from $S_1$ to $S_1^{[2]}$ that induces $\theta$; then $\Gamma$; then the graph of $j$, viewed as a correspondence from $X$ to $F$ that induces $j^*$. Let $Z$ be the component of $W$ of codimension $2$ in $S_1 \times F$. Then $Z$ is algebraic: $W$ is a rational combination of classes of subvarieties, each of which is homogeneous, so each homogeneous component of $W$ is algebraic. (The class $\Gamma$ may be inhomogeneous; only its component of codimension $4$ in $S_1^{[2]} \times X$ contributes to $Z$.) The first and third correspondences are homogeneous and preserve degrees. Hence the map $H^2(S_1) \to H^2(F)$ induced by $Z$ is the composite of the $H^2 \to H^2$ components, namely $j^* \circ c f^{-1} \circ \theta$. On $T(S_1)_{\mathbb Q}$ this equals $c\, j^* \psi^{-1}$. By Proposition 2.1 it is a Hodge similarity $T(S_1)_{\mathbb Q} \to T(F)_{\mathbb Q}$ of multiplier $2c^2$, which is $2$ modulo squares; replacing $Z$ by $c^{-1}Z$ gives multiplier exactly $2$.

*Step 4: trivial endomorphism field.* Suppose $\operatorname{End}_{\mathrm{Hdg}} T(F)_{\mathbb Q} = \mathbb Q$. Then $\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathrm{Hdg}}(T(S_1)_{\mathbb Q}, T(F)_{\mathbb Q}) = \mathbb Q \cdot Z_*$, so every nonzero Hodge morphism has multiplier $2\lambda^2$ for some $\lambda \in \mathbb Q^\times$, and none is an isometry. The Hodge classes in $H^4(S_1 \times F, \mathbb Q)$ are those of $H^0 \otimes H^4$, $H^4 \otimes H^0$, $\operatorname{NS}(S_1) \otimes \operatorname{NS}(F)$, and $(T(S_1) \otimes T(F))^{\mathrm{Hdg}} \cong \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathrm{Hdg}}(T(S_1)_{\mathbb Q}, T(F)_{\mathbb Q})$, the last by Poincaré duality. All of these are algebraic. In other degrees, the Hodge conjecture for $S_1 \times F$ follows from the Lefschetz $(1,1)$ theorem and hard Lefschetz. For the very general member of a $12$-dimensional family of lattice-polarized K3 surfaces, the Mumford–Tate group of $T$ is the full special orthogonal group, so $\operatorname{End}_{\mathrm{Hdg}} = \mathbb Q$. $\square$

*Proof of Corollary 1.2.* Let $F$ be the minimal resolution of a seven-nodal quartic with $\operatorname{NS}(F) = \langle 4 \rangle \oplus \langle -2 \rangle^{\oplus 7}$, spanned by the hyperplane class and the nodal curves, and with $\operatorname{End}_{\mathrm{Hdg}} T(F)_{\mathbb Q} = \mathbb Q$. This is what we mean by very general. By [6, Cor 6.8 and Section 7.1], $\langle 4 \rangle \oplus \langle -2 \rangle^{\oplus 7} \cong \langle 2 \rangle \oplus E_7(-2)$, which contains $E_7(-2)$ as a direct summand. By [6, Thm 1.1], $F$ is a generalized Nikulin surface. Put $N_1 = \langle 4 \rangle \oplus E_7(-1)$ and $T_1 = U^{\oplus 2} \oplus E_8(-1) \oplus \langle -4 \rangle \oplus \langle -2 \rangle$. Embedding $\langle 4 \rangle$ in $U$ by $e + 2f$ and $E_7(-1)$ in $E_8(-1)$ as the complement of a root gives a primitive embedding $N_1 \subset \Lambda$ with orthogonal complement $T_1$.

A computation of Hasse–Witt invariants (script `lattice_check.py` in the supplement) shows $\operatorname{NS}(F)_{\mathbb Q} \oplus T_1(2)_{\mathbb Q} \cong \Lambda_{\mathbb Q}$. By Witt cancellation, $T(F)_{\mathbb Q} \cong T_1(2)_{\mathbb Q}$, and by Proposition 2.1, $T(X)_{\mathbb Q} \cong T_{1, \mathbb Q}$. So in Step 1 we may choose $\psi$ with $\psi(T(X)_{\mathbb Q}) = T_{1,\mathbb Q}$. The resulting $S_1$ has $\operatorname{NS}(S_1) = T_1^\perp = N_1$, because the period is not orthogonal to any rational vector of $T_1$.

Let $H \in N_1$ be the generator of $\langle 4 \rangle$. Replacing $H$ by its image under a product of reflections in $(-2)$-classes and $\pm 1$, we may assume $H$ is nef. These are isometries of $N_1$, so $N_1 = \langle H \rangle \oplus H^\perp$ with $H^\perp \cong E_7(-1)$ still holds. Every $D = aH + r \in N_1$ with $r \in E_7(-1)$ has $D \cdot H = 4a$. So there is no class $E$ with $E^2 = 0$ and $E \cdot H \in \{1, 2\}$, and $H/2 \notin N_1$. By Saint-Donat [13], $|H|$ maps $\tilde S_1$ birationally onto a quartic surface and contracts exactly the $(-2)$-curves orthogonal to $H$. These form a root basis of $H^\perp = E_7(-1)$, whose Dynkin diagram is connected, so the image has one singular point, of type $E_7$. $\square$

*Proof of Theorem 1.3.* Write $r = r_G$, $L_+ = H^2(X, \mathbb Q)^G$ and $L_- = L_+^\perp$. At $S^{[n]}$ we have $L_+ = H^2(S, \mathbb Q)^G \oplus \mathbb Q\delta$, where $2\delta$ is the class of the exceptional divisor and $q(\delta) = -2(n-1)$. So $L_+$ has dimension $23 - r$ and signature $(3, 20 - r)$, and $L_-$ is spanned by the classes $a - g^*a$ with $g \in G$.

*The fixed surface.* Let $G$ be a finite group of symplectic automorphisms of a manifold $Y$. Then the fixed locus $Y^G$ is smooth with tangent space $(T_yY)^G$, and the symplectic form is nondegenerate on it, because it pairs the $\rho$-isotypic part of $T_yY$ with the $\rho^\vee$-isotypic part for each irreducible representation $\rho$ of $G$. Each nontrivial element of $G$ has finitely many fixed points on $S$; let $S^\circ$ be the complement of all of them. At $S^{[n]}$, the subschemes that are free $G$-orbits form an open subset $F^\circ$ of $(S^{[n]})^G$, isomorphic to $S^\circ/G$; its tangent space at an orbit is the space of invariants of $\bigoplus_{x} T_xS$, which is $2$-dimensional. Let $F$ be the connected component of $(S^{[n]})^G$ that contains $F^\circ$. It is a smooth compact surface with trivial canonical bundle, birational to the minimal resolution of $S/G$, which is a K3 surface, so $F$ is that K3 surface. By [11, Thm 1.0.1] the other components are isolated points: for $n = |G|$, the number of $2$-dimensional components is the constant term of a theta series, which is $1$. In a smooth family of $G$-manifolds the fixed locus is smooth over the base, because $T_X \to T_B$ stays surjective after taking $G$-invariants. So $F$ deforms with $X$, and these statements hold throughout the deformation class.

*The restriction.* Since $j^* g^* = j^*$ for $g \in G$, $j^*$ vanishes on $L_-$. The form $B(a, b) = \int_F j^*a \cdot j^*b$ on $L_+$ is flat in families. The group $G$ acts on the Kuranishi space of $X$, its fixed locus is smooth and parametrizes the deformations of $X$ with its $G$-action, and by local Torelli the periods of these deformations fill an open subset of the quadric $Q = \{q = 0\} \subset \mathbb P(L_+ \otimes \mathbb C)$. For each such period $\sigma$, the class $j^*\sigma$ is a holomorphic $2$-form on $F$, so $B(\sigma, \sigma) = 0$. Since $\dim L_+ = 23 - r \ge 4$, the quadric $Q$ is irreducible, so $B$ vanishes on all of $Q$, and since the ideal of $Q$ is generated by $q$, we get $B = c\,q$ with $c \in \mathbb Q$. (This argument, which avoids Mumford–Tate groups and also covers non-projective points, was suggested by cm_symmetry_scout.) To compute $c$, return to $S^{[n]}$. The orbit map $\varphi\colon S^\circ \to F^\circ$ is étale of degree $n$. The symplectic form $\sigma$ of $S^{[n]}$ is induced by $\sum_i \operatorname{pr}_i^*\sigma_S$ on $S^n$, so $\varphi^* j^*\sigma = \sum_{g \in G} g^*\sigma_S = n\,\sigma_S$. Since $F \setminus F^\circ$ and $S \setminus S^\circ$ have measure zero,
$$B(\sigma, \bar\sigma) = \int_{F^\circ} j^*\sigma \wedge j^*\bar\sigma = \tfrac1n \int_S n^2\, \sigma_S \wedge \bar\sigma_S = n\, q(\sigma, \bar\sigma),$$
because $q$ restricts to the intersection form on $H^2(S) \subset H^2(S^{[n]})$. Hence $c = n$.

As an independent check on the class $\delta$, which this argument does not see directly, take $G$ cyclic of prime order $p$. Then $F$ is the $G$-Hilbert scheme of $S$ [14], and $j^*\mathcal O^{[p]} = \pi_*\mathcal O_\Gamma$, where $\Gamma \to F$ is the universal family. This bundle is the sum of the tautological line bundles $\mathcal R_\chi$ over the characters $\chi$ of $G$. Off the exceptional curves, $\mathcal R_\chi$ is a torsion line bundle, so rationally $c_1(\mathcal R_\chi)$ lies in the span of the exceptional curves and is determined by its degrees on them. By the McKay correspondence [15], $c_1(\mathcal R_\chi) \cdot E = 1$ for the exceptional curve $E$ attached to $\chi \ne 1$ at each of the $\lambda_p = 24/(p+1)$ singular points of $S/G$, and $0$ for the other exceptional curves. So, rationally, $j^*\delta = -c_1(\pi_*\mathcal O_\Gamma)$ is minus the sum of the dual classes of all exceptional curves. With $C$ the intersection matrix of $A_{p-1}$ and $\mathbf 1$ the all-ones vector, its square is
$$\lambda_p\, \mathbf 1^{\mathsf T} C^{-1} \mathbf 1 = -\lambda_p \frac{(p-1)p(p+1)}{12} = -2p(p-1) = p\, q(\delta),$$
as the script `mckay_check.py` in the supplement confirms for $p = 2, 3, 5, 7$. For $p = 2$ this is $j^*\delta = \frac12 \sum_i \phi_*e_i$, in agreement with Proposition 2.1.

The last paragraph of the proof of Proposition 2.1 applies with $G$ in place of $\langle\iota\rangle$: for $g \in G$, $g^* - 1$ vanishes on $T(X)_{\mathbb Q}$ by Schur's lemma, so $T(X)_{\mathbb Q} \subset L_+$, and $j^*$ is a Hodge similarity of multiplier $n$ from $T(X)_{\mathbb Q}$ onto $T(F)_{\mathbb Q}$.

*Steps 0 to 4.* Step 0 is unchanged, with $T(X)_{\mathbb Q} \cong T(F)_{\mathbb Q}(\tfrac1n)$. In Step 1 we replace the explicit embedding $\psi$ as follows. As a quadratic space, $H^2(X, \mathbb Q) \cong \Lambda_{\mathbb Q} \oplus \langle -2(n-1) \rangle$. The space $\operatorname{NS}(X)_{\mathbb Q}$ contains $L_-$ and an ample class, so it is indefinite of dimension at least $r + 1 \ge 9$. Hence it represents $-2(n-1)$, and $\operatorname{NS}(X)_{\mathbb Q} = \mathbb Q v \oplus N$ with $q(v) = -2(n-1)$, where $N$ has signature $(1, \rho(X) - 2)$. Witt cancellation gives an isometry $\psi\colon T(X)_{\mathbb Q} \oplus N \to \Lambda_{\mathbb Q}$. The rest of Step 1 applies, with a class of positive square in $N$ in place of $h$. In Step 2, replace $S_1^{[2]}$ by $S_1^{[n]}$, whose second cohomology is $\theta(H^2(S_1, \mathbb Q)) \oplus \langle -2(n-1) \rangle$; [2, Thm 1.1] holds for manifolds of $K3^{[n]}$-type for every $n$. Steps 3 and 4 are unchanged, with multiplier $n$ in place of $2$; Step 4 uses that $n$ is not a square.

*Dimension count.* A projective $X$ in the deformation class has a $G$-invariant ample class $h \in L_+$, and $T(X)_{\mathbb Q} \subset h^\perp \cap L_+$, which has signature $(2, 20 - r)$. So the projective $X$ form countably many families of dimension $20 - r$, and for the very general member $T(X)_{\mathbb Q} = h^\perp \cap L_+$ has dimension $22 - r$ and $\operatorname{End}_{\mathrm{Hdg}} = \mathbb Q$. Then $T(F)_{\mathbb Q} \cong T(X)_{\mathbb Q}(n)$, so $\rho(F) = r$, and $j^*$ identifies the periods of $F$ with those of $X$, so $F$ also moves in $20 - r$ dimensions. $\square$

## 4. Remarks

**Remark 4.1 (even rank).** In Corollary 1.2, $T(\tilde S_1)_{\mathbb Q}$ and $T(F)_{\mathbb Q}$ are isometric as abstract quadratic spaces: the script also checks $\operatorname{NS}(F)_{\mathbb Q} \oplus T_{1,\mathbb Q} \cong \Lambda_{\mathbb Q}$. This is possible because the rank is $14$, so scaling by $2$ does not change the discriminant. The multiplier is still not a square in the sense that matters for the Hodge conjecture: when $\operatorname{End}_{\mathrm{Hdg}} = \mathbb Q$, no Hodge isometry exists (Step 4), so Buskin's theorem says nothing about $\tilde S_1 \times F$. For a Nikulin involution the rank of $T$ is at most $13$, and for odd rank a nonsquare multiplier changes the discriminant. Boissière, Sarti and Veniani [8, Thm 1.1] give the exact criterion for $T_{\mathbb Q}(p) \cong T_{\mathbb Q}$ in both parities. In Theorem 1.3 the rank is $22 - r_G$, which is even for every group in Table 1, while quotients by $G$ give the odd rank $21 - r_G$. A comparison of dimensions ($12$ against $11$) should keep this difference in mind.

**Remark 4.2 (where the construction stops).** For every symplectic involution on a manifold of $K3^{[n]}$-type the anti-invariant lattice is $E_8(-2)$ [9] and [6, proof of Lemma 8.8], so pairs $(X, \iota)$ with $X$ projective move in families of dimension at most $12$. Fixed loci of symplectic involutions therefore cannot give larger families. The same bound holds for every nontrivial finite group $G$ of symplectic automorphisms of a manifold of $K3^{[n]}$-type, whether or not it comes from a K3 surface: the coinvariant lattice is a Leech type lattice [16, Thm 3.6], and the only Leech type lattice of rank at most $11$ is $E_8(-2)$ [16, Section 2]. A group whose coinvariant lattice is not $E_8(-2)$ therefore gives families of dimension at most $8$ (Table 1). The mechanism behind Proposition 2.1 is more general. Let $\Sigma \subset X$ be a K3 surface in a projective hyper-Kähler manifold with $\operatorname{End}_{\mathrm{Hdg}} T(X)_{\mathbb Q} = \mathbb Q$ and Mumford–Tate group $\mathrm{SO}(T(X)_{\mathbb Q})$. Then the form $\int_\Sigma j^*a\, j^*b$ on $T(X)_{\mathbb Q}$ is invariant under the Mumford–Tate group, hence equal to $c\, q$ for some $c \in \mathbb Q$. Moreover $c \neq 0$ if and only if the symplectic form does not vanish on $\Sigma$. Since $j^*$ embeds $T(X)_{\mathbb Q}$ into $H^2(\Sigma, \mathbb Q)$ and $\Sigma$ is projective, $\operatorname{rk} T(X) \le 21$; this numerical bound alone allows $\rho(X) = 2$ for $K3^{[n]}$-type, that is, $19$-dimensional families. For $\operatorname{NS}(X) = \mathbb Z u$ such a surface is trianalytic, and in $K3^{[2]}$-type none exists [10]; so $\rho(X) = 2$ is the first open case. More precisely, the kernel of $j^*$ on $H^2(X, \mathbb Q)$ is a sub-Hodge structure meeting $T(X)_{\mathbb Q}$ trivially, so it lies in $\operatorname{NS}(X)_{\mathbb Q}$ and contains no ample class. Hence the pairs $(X, \Sigma)$ move in families of dimension at most $20 - \operatorname{rk} \ker j^*$. For the fixed surface of a symplectic involution the kernel is $E_8(-2)$, and the bound $12$ is attained.

For $n = 2$ and $\rho(X) = 2$ the constant can be read off from the class of $\Sigma$. If $X$ is very general with $\operatorname{NS}(X) = \langle h_1, h_2 \rangle$, the Hodge classes in $H^4(X, \mathbb Q) = \operatorname{Sym}^2 H^2(X, \mathbb Q)$ are spanned by $h_1^2, h_1h_2, h_2^2$ and $c_2(X)$. Write $[\Sigma] = a h_1^2 + b h_1h_2 + c' h_2^2 + e\, c_2(X)$. The Fujiki relations $\int x^4 = 3q(x)^2$ and $\int c_2(X) x^2 = 30 q(x)$ give, for $x \in T(X)$,
$$\int_\Sigma (j^*x)^2 = \bigl(a\, q(h_1) + b\, q(h_1, h_2) + c'\, q(h_2) + 30 e\bigr)\, q(x).$$
(On $S^{[2]}$ the two constants can be checked directly: $\int \delta^4 = 12$ and $\int c_2 \delta^2 = -60$.) In particular, $\Sigma$ is Lagrangian exactly when this constant vanishes. If $T(X)_{\mathbb Q} \cong T(S)_{\mathbb Q}$ for a K3 surface $S$ of degree $2d$ with $\rho(S) = 1$, and $\Sigma$ has degree $2t$ with $\rho(\Sigma) = 1$, then comparing discriminants in rank $21$ shows that $c \equiv dt$ modulo squares. K3 surfaces built from moduli of (twisted) sheaves on $S$ have $T_{\mathbb Q} \cong T(S)_{\mathbb Q}$, hence square $c$. So a positive answer to the question below needs a K3 surface that does not come from $S$ in this way.

**Question 4.3.** Is there a projective manifold $X$ of $K3^{[n]}$-type with $\rho(X) = 2$ that contains a K3 surface $\Sigma$ on which the symplectic form does not vanish and whose constant $c$ is not a square? If moreover $T(X)_{\mathbb Q}$ is Hodge isometric to $T(S)_{\mathbb Q}$ for a K3 surface $S$, the argument of Section 3 would give an algebraic Hodge similarity of nonsquare multiplier between K3 surfaces of Picard number one. Over a $19$-dimensional family, this is the open case of [5].

**Remark 4.4 (twistor transport).** Since $\Lambda_{\mathbb Q}(2) \cong \Lambda_{\mathbb Q}$ [5, Thm 2(a)] and $T(S_1)_{\mathbb Q}(2) \cong T(F)_{\mathbb Q}$, Witt cancellation gives $\operatorname{NS}(S_1)_{\mathbb Q}(2) \cong \operatorname{NS}(F)_{\mathbb Q}$. Adding to $Z$ a suitable class in $\operatorname{NS}(S_1) \otimes \operatorname{NS}(F)$ therefore gives an algebraic class that induces a similarity of multiplier $2$ on all of $H^2$. This is the shape that [5, Section 6.2] requires of a seed for transporting Hodge similarities along twistor lines, as Buskin does for isometries. A seed must, however, be a stable bundle that stays hyperholomorphic along generic twistor lines, and we know no such bundle. The natural candidate fails as Nikulin quotients do in [5, Section 6.3]. For an $\iota$-invariant Kähler class, $\iota$ is an isometry of the hyperkähler metric, so $F$ is trianalytic. But twistor lines with such classes stay in the $13$-dimensional period domain of pairs $(X, \iota)$, whose projective points are already covered by Theorem 1.1. The same limit applies to any sheaf, complex or cycle on $S_1 \times F$ obtained by composing a kernel on $S_1 \times X$ with restriction to $F$. Restriction has no degree-mixing term in $H^2$, because $c_1(N_{F/X}) = 0$. So the induced map $H^2(S_1, \mathbb Q) \to H^2(F, \mathbb Q)$ has image in $j^*H^2(X, \mathbb Q)$, which has rank $15$, and its kernel is a subspace of $\operatorname{NS}(S_1)_{\mathbb Q}$ of rank at least $7$. That kernel must stay algebraic wherever the class is a Hodge class. So the Hodge locus of the class lies in the $13$-dimensional locus of pairs $(X, \iota)$, and neither twistor transport nor a semiregularity argument can carry it further. So Theorem 1.1 does not answer [5, Question 6.2].

**What is not proved.** Nothing is claimed at Picard number below $8$ or for families of dimension above $12$. The construction does not address the second open piece in [5], the algebraicity of the determinant class.
