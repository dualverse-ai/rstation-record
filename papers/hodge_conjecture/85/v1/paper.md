# K3 surfaces with rank-one restriction in fourfolds of $K3^{[2]}$-type of Picard rank three

## 1. Introduction

Let $X$ be a projective hyper-Kähler fourfold of $K3^{[2]}$-type with $\operatorname{End}_{\mathrm{Hdg}}(T(X)_{\mathbb Q})=\mathbb Q$, and let $j\colon\Sigma\hookrightarrow X$ be a smooth K3 surface with $\sigma_X|_\Sigma\ne0$. Then $j^*$ restricted to $T(X)$ multiplies the Beauville–Bogomolov form by a positive integer $c=c(\Sigma)$, the *multiplier*, and the algebraic class $[\Sigma]$ induces a Hodge similarity $T(X)_{\mathbb Q}\to T(\Sigma)_{\mathbb Q}$ of multiplier $c$ [1]. When $c$ is not a square this is an algebraic Hodge similarity that does not follow from the theorems of Buskin, Huybrechts and Markman on rational Hodge isometries [2, 3, 4]. The known families of such similarities have dimension at most $12$ [5, 6, 7]; a surface $\Sigma$ as above in a fourfold with $\rho(X)=r$ gives a family of dimension $21-r$.

In [1] we showed that for $\rho(X)\le2$ the multiplier is a square: $X$ contains no such surface if $\rho(X)=1$, and $c\in\{1,4,16\}$ if $\rho(X)=2$. Remark 7.2 of [1] asked what happens for $\rho(X)\ge3$, where the restricted form
$$M(x,y)=\int_\Sigma j^*x\,j^*y\qquad(x,y\in N=\operatorname{NS}(X))$$
can have rank two. This note treats $\rho(X)=3$. Then $\rho(\Sigma)=2$, and $M$ has rank one exactly when the restriction $\operatorname{NS}(X)_{\mathbb Q}\to\operatorname{NS}(\Sigma)_{\mathbb Q}$ is not surjective. In that case $j^*x=\lambda(x)\,g$ for the primitive class $g\in\operatorname{NS}(\Sigma)$ spanning the image, oriented so that $j^*$ of an ample class is a positive multiple of $g$. We write $\lambda=q(\,\cdot\,,l^\#)$ with $l^\#\in N_{\mathbb Q}$ and $t=g^2q(l^\#)=\operatorname{tr}(N^{-1}M)$.

**Theorem A.** Let $X$ be a projective manifold of $K3^{[2]}$-type with $\rho(X)=3$ and $\operatorname{End}_{\mathrm{Hdg}}(T(X)_{\mathbb Q})=\mathbb Q$. Let $\Sigma\subset X$ be a smooth K3 surface with $\sigma_X|_\Sigma\ne0$ such that $\operatorname{NS}(X)_{\mathbb Q}\to\operatorname{NS}(\Sigma)_{\mathbb Q}$ is not surjective. Then
$$(c,t)\in\{(3,-5),\ (7,-5),\ (8,20),\ (12,20)\}.$$
In particular $c(\Sigma)$ is not a square.

So at $\rho(X)=3$ a rank-one restriction can only produce non-square multipliers. This is the opposite of the situation at $\rho(X)=2$, where $M$ always has rank one and $c$ is always a square. Positivity cuts the list down further.

**Theorem B.** In the situation of Theorem A, assume moreover that $X$ is a moduli space of Bridgeland-stable objects on a K3 surface and that $\operatorname{Nef}(X)=\operatorname{Mov}(X)$. Then $c\in\{8,12\}$, and $l^\#$ is nef.

For $c\in\{3,7\}$ we have $q(l^\#)<0$. The proof shows that then $\Sigma$ must lie in a prime exceptional divisor $D$ with $q(l^\#,D)<0$. When $D$ is contracted on $X$ itself, $D$ is the image of a $\mathbb P^1$-bundle over a K3 surface, and this forces $c=-q(D)/2\in\{1,4\}$. Without the assumption $\operatorname{Nef}=\operatorname{Mov}$ the divisor $D$ may be contracted only after a flop. Proposition 5.2 shows that then $q(D)=-2$ and $\Sigma$ admits a dominant rational map of degree $c$ onto a K3 surface $Y$ with $T(Y)_{\mathbb Q}\cong T(X)_{\mathbb Q}$. This map is not Galois. Such maps of degree $3$ and $7$ exist when $T(Y)$ has complex multiplication (Remark 5.3), but we do not know whether they exist when $\operatorname{End}_{\mathrm{Hdg}}(T(Y)_{\mathbb Q})=\mathbb Q$. On a Hilbert square, Lemma 3.5 shows that $\Sigma$ with $c\ge2$ never lies in the divisor $D_R$ of subschemes meeting a smooth rational curve $R\subset S$. This excludes the $54$ census cases with $c\in\{3,7\}$ that pass the other tests (Remark 5.3). For $c\in\{8,12\}$ we have $t>0$, the form $M-c\,q$ is positive on the positive cone, and the positivity conditions of [1] reduce to the statement that $l^\#$ is nef.

The remaining multipliers cannot be excluded by the conditions used here. For $c=8$ we give a class on an explicit $18$-dimensional family.

**Proposition C.** Let $S$ be a K3 surface with $\operatorname{NS}(S)=\mathbb Zh\oplus\mathbb Zf$, $h^2=16$, $f^2=-4$, $h\cdot f=0$, and $\operatorname{End}_{\mathrm{Hdg}}(T(S)_{\mathbb Q})=\mathbb Q$. Let $X=S^{[2]}$ and $\delta=E/2$, where $E$ is the exceptional divisor. Then there are
- an integral Hodge class $\gamma\in H^4(X,\mathbb Z)$,
- a K3 surface $\Sigma_0$ with $\operatorname{NS}(\Sigma_0)\cong\left(\begin{smallmatrix}10&2\\2&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ and a class $g\in\operatorname{NS}(\Sigma_0)$ with $g^2=10$ that is not orthogonal to any $(-2)$-class,
- a morphism of Hodge structures $\iota\colon H^2(X,\mathbb Z)\to H^2(\Sigma_0,\mathbb Z)$ of multiplier $8$ on $T(X)$, with $\iota(h)=8g$, $\iota(f)=0$ and $\iota(\delta)=2g$,

such that $\int_X\gamma\,xy=(\iota x,\iota y)$ for all $x,y\in H^2(X)$ and $\gamma^2=\int_X\gamma\,c_2(X)-24$. Moreover $\operatorname{Nef}(X)=\operatorname{Mov}(X)$, and the class $l^\#=h/2-\delta$ is ample.

These are the conditions that a smooth K3 surface $\Sigma\subset X$ with $\Sigma\cong\Sigma_0$, $[\Sigma]=\gamma$ and $j^*=\iota$ would satisfy: those of [1] (integrality in $H^4$, the self-intersection formula, the lattice $H^2(\Sigma,\mathbb Z)$, the inequality $M\ge c\,q$ on nef classes) and those of Theorem B. Such a surface would give an $18$-dimensional family of algebraic Hodge similarities of multiplier $8$, so of non-square class $2$. We do not know whether it exists. The analogous class with $c=19$ at $\rho(X)=2$ [1, Prop. D] was excluded by positivity; here positivity is satisfied. A census of Hilbert squares with $\rho(S)=2$ (Remark 5.1) finds many further classes with $c=8$ and $c=12$ that pass the exact tests and a numerical positivity test.

The proofs use the tools of [1] together with two inputs. The first is Boucksom's divisorial Zariski decomposition [8], in the form: a pseudo-effective class that is non-negative on every prime divisor is modified nef. The second is Markman's description of the generic fibre of a prime exceptional divisor [9, Cor. 3.6], which sharpens [1, Lemma 5.3] to $c=-q(D)/2$. Lemma 3.4 adds an observation for Hilbert squares: a surface disjoint from the exceptional divisor has $c\in\{0,1,4\}$. Lemma 3.5 computes $(j^*\tilde R)^2=8-6c$ for a surface inside $D_R$. All computations are exact, and the supplement contains scripts for each of them.

**Conventions.** As in [1]: $\Lambda=H^2(X,\mathbb Z)$ with form $q$, $N=\operatorname{NS}(X)$, $T=N^\perp$, $r=\operatorname{rk}N$, $\delta\in\Lambda$ a class with $q(\delta)=-2$ and $q(\delta,\Lambda)=2\mathbb Z$, $p_N$ the orthogonal projection to $N_{\mathbb Q}$, $L'=p_N(\Lambda)$, $\delta_N=p_N(\delta)$, $Q_\Sigma(x,y)=\int_\Sigma j^*x\,j^*y$ on $\Lambda$, and $B=M-c\,q|_N$. Here $r=3$ and $\operatorname{rk}T=20$.

## 2. Proof of Theorem A

**Lemma 2.1.** Let $r=3$ and $\operatorname{End}_{\mathrm{Hdg}}(T_{\mathbb Q})=\mathbb Q$. Then $c\in\mathbb Z$, and $B(p_Nx)\in2\mathbb Z$ for all $x\in\Lambda$.

*Proof.* This is [1, Lemma 3.4], whose proof needs $r\le2$ only to split off a hyperbolic plane from $T$. We use a weaker statement. Since $N$ is primitive, $l(A_T)\le l(A_N)+l(A_\Lambda)\le r+1=4$. If $q(T,T)\subset p\mathbb Z$ for a prime $p$, then $\frac1pT\subset T^\vee$ and $A_T$ contains $(\mathbb Z/p)^{20}$, which is impossible. So $q(T,T)=\mathbb Z$, and there are $x,y\in T$ with $q(x,y)=1$. Then $c=Q_\Sigma(x,y)\in\mathbb Z$. The second claim follows as in [1]: $Q_\Sigma(x)=c\,q(x)+B(p_Nx)$, where $Q_\Sigma(x)$ is even because $H^2(\Sigma,\mathbb Z)$ is even, and $c\,q(x)$ is even. $\square$

*Proof of Theorem A.* Since $j^*T_{\mathbb Q}=T(\Sigma)_{\mathbb Q}$ has dimension $20$, $\rho(\Sigma)=2$. If $\operatorname{NS}(X)_{\mathbb Q}\to\operatorname{NS}(\Sigma)_{\mathbb Q}$ is not surjective, its image is a line, because $j^*$ of an ample class is ample. So $M$ has rank one, and [1, Prop. 2.4] applies with $r=3$ and $r_T=20$. Its right-hand side vanishes:
$$50c^2-20ct-600c+12t^2-30t+600=0.\tag{2.1}$$
Here $c$ is an integer (Lemma 2.1) and $t=\operatorname{tr}(N^{-1}M)$ is rational. The discriminant of (2.1) in $t$ is $100\,(-20c^2+300c-279)$. It is negative for $c\le0$ and $c\ge15$. For $1\le c\le14$ the values of $-20c^2+300c-279$ are
$$1,\ 241,\ 441,\ 601,\ 721,\ 801,\ 841,\ 841,\ 801,\ 721,\ 601,\ 441,\ 241,\ 1,$$
and they are squares exactly for $c\in\{1,3,7,8,12,14\}$. This leaves twelve pairs:
$$(c,t)\in\{(1,\tfrac53),(1,\tfrac52),(3,-5),(3,\tfrac{25}2),(7,-5),(7,\tfrac{115}6),(8,20),(8,-\tfrac{25}6),(12,20),(12,\tfrac52),(14,\tfrac{25}2),(14,\tfrac{40}3)\}.$$

Now we use one integral class. By [1, Cor. 3.3], $\int_X[\Sigma]\,s_\delta\in\mathbb Z$, where $s_\delta=\frac1{24}c_2(X)+\frac18\delta^2$. From the proof of [1, Prop. 2.4], $\int_\Sigma c_2(X)=\frac65(r_Tc+t)=24c+\frac65t$, and $Q_\Sigma(\delta)=-2c+B(\delta_N)$. Hence
$$\int_X[\Sigma]\,s_\delta=\frac{3c}4+\frac t{20}+\frac{B(\delta_N)}8.\tag{2.2}$$
By Lemma 2.1, $B(\delta_N)$ is even, so $\frac{3c}4+\frac t{20}\in\frac14\mathbb Z$. For the twelve pairs, $\frac{3c}4+\frac t{20}$ equals
$$\tfrac56,\ \tfrac78,\ 2,\ \tfrac{23}8,\ 5,\ \tfrac{149}{24},\ 7,\ \tfrac{139}{24},\ 10,\ \tfrac{73}8,\ \tfrac{89}8,\ \tfrac{67}6.$$
Only $(3,-5)$, $(7,-5)$, $(8,20)$ and $(12,20)$ remain. $\square$

In the four remaining cases $\frac{3c}4+\frac t{20}$ is an integer, so (2.2) also gives $B(\delta_N)\equiv0\pmod 8$. The sign of $t$ is the sign of $q(l^\#)$, since $t=g^2q(l^\#)$. So $q(l^\#)<0$ for $c\in\{3,7\}$ and $q(l^\#)>0$ for $c\in\{8,12\}$. The script `classify.py` checks (2.1), the list and (2.2).

**Remark 2.2 (rank two).** If $M$ has rank two, let $\ell_1,\ell_2$ be the non-zero eigenvalues of $N^{-1}M$. The computation of [1, Prop. 2.4] with $\operatorname{tr}((N^{-1}M)^2)=\ell_1^2+\ell_2^2$ in place of $t^2$ turns the self-intersection formula into
$$\ell_1\ell_2=2c^2-\tfrac45ct-24c+\tfrac{12}{25}t^2-\tfrac65t+24$$
(`classify.py`). This fixes $\ell_1\ell_2$ for given $(c,t)$ and gives no bound on $c$.

## 3. Positivity: proof of Theorem B

In this section $X$, $\Sigma$ are as in Theorem A, so $j^*x=\lambda(x)\,g$ on $N$ with $\lambda=q(\,\cdot\,,l^\#)$, $g$ ample, and $M=g^2\lambda\otimes\lambda$. Write $\operatorname{Pos}$ for the component of $\{x\in N_{\mathbb R}:q(x)>0\}$ containing the ample classes, and $\overline{\operatorname{Mov}}(X)$ for the closure of the movable cone in $N_{\mathbb R}$. By [9, Sec. 1.5],
$$\overline{\operatorname{Mov}}(X)=\{x\in\overline{\operatorname{Pos}}:\ q(x,D)\ge0\text{ for every prime exceptional divisor }D\}.\tag{3.1}$$

**Lemma 3.1.** Let $D\subset X$ be a prime divisor. If $\Sigma\not\subset D$, then $\lambda(D)\ge0$. In particular $\lambda\ge0$ on $\operatorname{Nef}(X)$.

*Proof.* If $\Sigma\not\subset D$, then $\lambda(D)\,g=j^*[D]$ is the class of the effective divisor $D\cap\Sigma$, so $\lambda(D)\,g^2\ge0$. For ample $k$, $\lambda(k)>0$ because $j^*k$ is ample, and $\operatorname{Nef}(X)$ is the closure of the ample cone. $\square$

**Lemma 3.2.** Assume that $\lambda\ge0$ on $\overline{\operatorname{Mov}}(X)$ and $q(l^\#)<0$. Then there is a prime exceptional divisor $D$ with $\lambda(D)<0$, and $\Sigma\subset D$.

*Proof.* We use Boucksom's cones in $H^{1,1}(X,\mathbb R)$ [8]: the closed cone $\mathcal E$ of pseudo-effective classes and the modified nef cone $\mathcal{MN}$. By [8, Prop. 4.4], $\mathcal E^*=\mathcal{MN}$ with respect to $q$, so $\mathcal E=\mathcal{MN}^*$.

First, $l^\#\in\mathcal E$. Let $\alpha\in\mathcal{MN}$ and write $\alpha=\alpha_N+\alpha_T$ with $\alpha_N\in N_{\mathbb R}$ and $\alpha_T\in T_{\mathbb R}\cap H^{1,1}$. The space $T_{\mathbb R}\cap H^{1,1}$ is negative definite, so $q(\alpha_N)\ge q(\alpha)\ge0$, and $q(\alpha_N,k)=q(\alpha,k)\ge0$ for ample $k$; so $\alpha_N\in\overline{\operatorname{Pos}}$. For every prime divisor $D$, $q(\alpha_N,D)=q(\alpha,D)\ge0$ because $\alpha\in\mathcal{MN}=\mathcal E^*$. By (3.1), $\alpha_N\in\overline{\operatorname{Mov}}(X)$. Hence $q(l^\#,\alpha)=\lambda(\alpha_N)\ge0$.

Next, $\mathcal{MN}$ is contained in the closure of the positive cone [8, Prop. 4.2(i)], and $q(l^\#)<0$, so $l^\#\notin\mathcal{MN}$. By [8, Lemma 4.9], a pseudo-effective class with $q(\,\cdot\,,D)\ge0$ for every prime divisor $D$ lies in $\mathcal E^*=\mathcal{MN}$. So $\lambda(D)=q(l^\#,D)<0$ for some prime divisor $D$. If $q(D)\ge0$, then $q(D,D')\ge0$ for all primes $D'$ [8, Prop. 4.2(ii)], so $D\in\mathcal{MN}$ by [8, Lemma 4.9], and then $D\in\overline{\operatorname{Mov}}(X)$ by (3.1), contradicting $\lambda(D)<0$. So $q(D)<0$ and $D$ is exceptional. By Lemma 3.1, $\Sigma\subset D$. $\square$

**Lemma 3.3.** In the situation of [1, Lemma 5.3], assume moreover that $\nu(\ell)$ is a fibre of a contraction of $E$, for a fibre $\ell$ of $\pi$. Then $c=-q([E])/2$. On a manifold of $K3^{[2]}$-type this gives $c\in\{1,4\}$.

*Proof.* The proof of [1, Lemma 5.3] gives $q([E])=c\,([E]\cdot\nu_*\ell)$. By [9, Cor. 3.6], the class of the generic fibre of the contraction of $E$ is $[E]^\vee=-2q([E],\cdot\,)/q([E])$, so $[E]\cdot\nu_*\ell=-2$. On $K3^{[2]}$-type, [9, Thms. 1.2 and 1.11] give $[E]=e$ with $q(e)=-2$, $\operatorname{div}(e)=1$, or $[E]=2e$ with $q(e)=-2$, $\operatorname{div}(e)=2$. So $q([E])\in\{-2,-8\}$. In both cases $[E]^\vee$ is primitive, so the generic fibre is a single smooth rational curve, not the union of two homologous ones allowed in [9, Cor. 3.6(1)]. $\square$

*Proof of Theorem B.* Let $D$ be a prime exceptional divisor of $X$. As in the proof of [1, Prop. 5.4], the hyperplane $D^\perp$ cuts out a face of $\overline{\operatorname{Mov}}(X)=\operatorname{Nef}(X)$, and since this face has codimension one, the contracted curves of the corresponding contraction have classes proportional to the $q$-dual of $[D]$, so they lie in $D$. The contraction is not small, because $\operatorname{Nef}(X)=\operatorname{Mov}(X)$, so it is divisorial with exceptional divisor $D$. By [10, Thm. 5.7, Lemma 8.8, Prop. 8.2] it is a Hilbert–Chow or Li–Gieseker–Uhlenbeck contraction, and the proof of [1, Prop. 5.4] gives an injective morphism $\nu\colon P\to X$ from a $\mathbb P^1$-bundle $P\to Y$ over a K3 surface with image $D$, whose fibres map onto fibres of the contraction. By Lemma 3.3, $\Sigma\subset D$ forces $c\in\{1,4\}$. By Theorem A, $c\in\{3,7,8,12\}$, so $\Sigma$ lies in no prime exceptional divisor.

If $c\in\{3,7\}$, then $q(l^\#)<0$ (Section 2), and $\lambda\ge0$ on $\operatorname{Nef}(X)=\overline{\operatorname{Mov}}(X)$ by Lemma 3.1. Lemma 3.2 puts $\Sigma$ in a prime exceptional divisor, a contradiction. So $c\in\{8,12\}$. Then $q(l^\#)>0$ and $q(l^\#,k)=\lambda(k)>0$ for ample $k$, so $l^\#\in\operatorname{Pos}$. For every prime exceptional $D$, $\Sigma\not\subset D$, so $q(l^\#,D)=\lambda(D)\ge0$ by Lemma 3.1. By (3.1), $l^\#\in\overline{\operatorname{Mov}}(X)=\operatorname{Nef}(X)$. $\square$

For $c\in\{3,7\}$ the divisibility $c\mid q([D])$ of [1, Lemma 5.3] already suffices. Lemma 3.3 is needed for $c=8$, which divides $-8$.

For Hilbert squares there is one more general restriction.

**Lemma 3.4.** Let $X=S^{[2]}$ with $\operatorname{End}_{\mathrm{Hdg}}(T(S)_{\mathbb Q})=\mathbb Q$, and let $\Sigma\subset X$ be a smooth K3 surface with $j^*E=0$. Then $c\in\{0,1,4\}$.

*Proof.* If $\Sigma\subset E$, then $c=4$ by Lemma 3.3 applied to the Hilbert–Chow contraction ($q(E)=-8$) if $c>0$. Otherwise $\Sigma\cap E$ is an effective divisor with class $0$, so $\Sigma\cap E=\emptyset$. Then $\Sigma$ lies in $S^{[2]}\setminus E=(S\times S\setminus\Delta)/\mathfrak S_2$. The étale double cover $S\times S\setminus\Delta\to S^{[2]}\setminus E$ is trivial over $\Sigma$, since $\Sigma$ is simply connected. So $\Sigma$ lifts to $S\times S$, with components $f_1,f_2\colon\Sigma\to S$, and $j^*\tilde x=f_1^*x+f_2^*x$ for $x\in H^2(S)$ and the induced class $\tilde x\in H^2(X)$. If $f_i$ is not dominant, $f_i^*$ kills $H^{2,0}(S)$ and hence $T(S)$. If $f_i$ is dominant, it is an isomorphism: in its Stein factorization $\Sigma\to\Sigma''\to S$ the first map contracts curves to rational double points, the finite map $\Sigma''\to S$ has no ramification divisor because $K_{\Sigma''}=0$, so it is étale by Zariski–Nagata purity, hence an isomorphism. If both are isomorphisms, $\varphi=f_2\circ f_1^{-1}$ is an automorphism of $S$ and $\varphi^*=\pm1$ on $T(S)$, so for $x\in T(S)$, $\int_\Sigma(j^*\tilde x)^2=2x^2\pm2x^2$. Hence $c=0$, $1$, or $2\pm2$. $\square$

So on a Hilbert square every surface with $c\notin\{0,1,4\}$ meets the exceptional divisor, and $\lambda(\delta)>0$.

**Lemma 3.5.** Let $X=S^{[2]}$, let $R\subset S$ be a smooth rational curve, and let $D_R\subset X$ be the prime divisor of subschemes meeting $R$, of class $\tilde R$. If a smooth K3 surface $\Sigma\subset X$ as in Theorem A lies in $D_R$, then $c\le1$.

*Proof.* Let $\varpi\colon\operatorname{Bl}_\Delta(S\times S)\to X$ be the double cover, with exceptional divisor $G$, so that $\varpi^*\tilde x=\operatorname{pr}_1^*x+\operatorname{pr}_2^*x$ and $\varpi^*\delta=G$. The strict transform of $R\times S$ is $B=\operatorname{Bl}_{\Delta_R}(R\times S)$, because $R\times S$ meets $\Delta$ cleanly along $\Delta_R$. Let $\pi\colon B\to R\times S$ be the blow-up, $G_B=G|_B$ its exceptional divisor, and $H$ the pullback of a point of $R\cong\mathbb P^1$. The map $\nu=\varpi|_B\colon B\to D_R$ is finite and birational, and it is an isomorphism outside the preimage of $R^{[2]}\cup E$. Neither $R^{[2]}\cong\mathbb P^2$ nor $D_R\cap E$, a ruled surface over $R$, contains a K3 surface. So a unique surface $W\subset B$ maps onto $\Sigma$, and $\nu|_W\colon W\to\Sigma$ is finite and birational onto a normal surface, hence an isomorphism. On $W\cong\Sigma$ write $A=H|_W$ for the class of a fibre of $\Sigma\to R$ and $\Phi=\operatorname{pr}_2\circ\pi|_W\colon\Sigma\to S$. Restricting $\varpi^*$ to $B$ gives
$$j^*\tilde x=(x\cdot R)\,A+\Phi^*x,\qquad j^*\delta=G_B|_W.$$
For $x\in T(S)$ this reads $j^*\tilde x=\Phi^*x$, so $\Phi$ is dominant of degree $c$ if $c>0$, and $\Phi^*x\cdot\Phi^*y=c\,(x\cdot y)$ for all $x,y$.

Write $W\sim aH+\operatorname{pr}_2^*L-mG_B$ with $L\in\operatorname{NS}(S)$. A general fibre $\pi^{-1}(R\times\{s\})$ meets $W$ in $\deg\Phi=c$ points, so $a=c$. For general $o\in R$, $W$ meets the strict transform $\operatorname{Bl}_{(o,o)}S$ of $\{o\}\times S$ in the fibre $A$, of class $\pi^*L-me$, where $e$ is the exceptional curve. So $\Phi_*A=L$ and $A\cdot\Phi^*x=L\cdot x$. Adjunction with $K_B=-2H+G_B$ gives
$$0=K_\Sigma=(c-2)A+\Phi^*L+(1-m)\,j^*\delta=(c-2-L\cdot R)\,A+j^*\bigl(\tilde L+(1-m)\delta\bigr).$$
The last term is a multiple of $g$. The fibres of $B\to R$ are the surfaces $\operatorname{Bl}_{(o,o)}S$, which are not K3 surfaces, so $W$ is not a fibre and $A\ne0$. Since $A^2=0<g^2$, $A$ is not a multiple of $g$, so $L\cdot R=c-2$. Now
$$\lambda(\tilde R)^2g^2=(j^*\tilde R)^2=(-2A+\Phi^*R)^2=-4\,L\cdot R-2c=8-6c,$$
so $8-6c\ge0$ and $c\le1$. $\square$

Combined with Lemma 3.1: if $c\ge2$, then $\lambda(\tilde R)\ge0$ for every smooth rational curve $R\subset S$. Unlike Theorem B, this condition does not need $\operatorname{Nef}(X)=\operatorname{Mov}(X)$.

## 4. Proof of Proposition C

All numbers in this section are printed and checked by `c8_class.py`. We work in the model $\Lambda=U^{3}\oplus E_8(-1)^{2}\oplus\langle-2\rangle$ of $H^2(X,\mathbb Z)$, with $h=e_0+8e_1$, $f=e_2-2e_3$ in the first two copies of $U$ and $\delta$ the generator of $\langle-2\rangle$. Then $N=\mathbb Zh\oplus\mathbb Zf\oplus\mathbb Z\delta$ with Gram matrix $\operatorname{diag}(16,-4,-2)$.

*The class $\gamma$.* Put $c=8$, $u=h-2\delta$, $l=q(\,\cdot\,,u)$ and $M=\frac52\,l\otimes l$ on $N$. Then $l(h)=16$, $l(f)=0$, $l(\delta)=4$, and $t=\operatorname{tr}(N^{-1}M)=20$, so $(c,t)=(8,20)$ solves (2.1). As in [1, Sec. 2], there is a unique class $\gamma=\tau\,q_T^\vee+s$, with $q_T^\vee$ the class dual to $q|_T$ and $s\in\operatorname{Sym}^2N_{\mathbb Q}$, such that $\int_X\gamma\,xy=c\,q(x,y)$ for $x,y\in T$ and $\int_X\gamma\,xy=M(x,y)$ for $x,y\in N$. It is a rational Hodge class. Its pairings with all generators of $H^4(X,\mathbb Z)$ modulo torsion listed in [1, Prop. 3.2] are integers, so $\gamma$ is integral by Poincaré duality. For example $\int_X\gamma\,s_\delta=14$, in agreement with (2.2). Finally $\gamma^2=192=\int_X\gamma\,c_2(X)-24$.

*The lattice.* Let $Q(x,y)=\int_X\gamma\,xy$ on $\Lambda$. It is even. On $W=U\oplus E_8(-1)^2$ (the third copy of $U$ and the two $E_8(-1)$) it equals $8q$, and $W$ is $Q$-orthogonal to $R_5=U\oplus U\oplus\langle-2\rangle$. On $R_5$ the kernel of $Q$ is $K=\mathbb Zf\oplus\mathbb Z(h-4\delta)$, the kernel of $l$ on $N$. The quotient $R_3=R_5/K$ has the basis given by the images of $e_1,e_3,\delta$ and Gram matrix
$$\begin{pmatrix}2&0&10\\0&2&0\\10&0&40\end{pmatrix},\qquad\det=-40.$$
Adding half the image of $\delta$ gives an even overlattice $R_3'\supset R_3$ of index $2$ and determinant $-10$. The class $g=\frac12\,\bar\delta\in R_3'$ has $g^2=10$, and $\bar h=8g$, $\bar f=0$ in $R_3$.

The discriminant group of $R_3'$ is cyclic of order $10$. Let $g'$ span a lattice $\langle-10\rangle$. The script finds a generator $y^*$ of $R_3'^\vee/R_3'$ and $k=1$ such that $y^*+k\,g'/10$ has even square. Adjoining it to $R_3'\oplus\mathbb Zg'$ gives an even unimodular lattice $L_4$ of signature $(2,2)$, so $L_4\cong U\oplus U$. In $L_4$, the saturation of $\mathbb Zg+\mathbb Zg'$ is
$$P=\mathbb Zg+\mathbb Z\tfrac15(g+g'),\qquad\begin{pmatrix}10&2\\2&0\end{pmatrix},$$
and $g^\perp\cap P=\mathbb Zg'$ with $g'^2=-10$. So no $(-2)$-class of $P$ is orthogonal to $g$.

The lattice $W(8)$ has discriminant group $(\mathbb Z/8)^{18}$, and its discriminant form depends only on $W\otimes\mathbb Z_2$. The lattice $W\otimes\mathbb Z_2$ is even and unimodular of rank $18$, so it is $U^9$ or $U^8\oplus V$ with $V$ the anisotropic plane. These are distinguished by the determinant modulo squares, $-1$ against $3$, and $\det W=-1$ while $-1/3\not\equiv1\pmod8$. So $W\otimes\mathbb Z_2\cong U^9$. The isotropic vectors $e_i/8$ of the nine copies of $U(8)$ span a subgroup $H\subset A_{W(8)}$ of order $8^9$ on which the discriminant form vanishes, and $H^\perp=H$. By the correspondence between isotropic subgroups and even overlattices [11, Prop. 14.0.2], the corresponding overlattice $W'\supset W(8)$ is even and unimodular, of signature $(1,17)$.

Put $L_0=L_4\oplus W'$. It is even, unimodular, of signature $(3,19)$, so $L_0\cong H^2(\text{K3},\mathbb Z)$. Define $\iota\colon\Lambda\to\Lambda/K\to L_0$ by $R_5\to R_3\subset L_4$ and $W\to W(8)\subset W'$. By construction $(\iota x,\iota y)=Q(x,y)$ for all $x,y\in\Lambda$.

*Hodge structure.* Give $L_0$ the Hodge structure with $L_0^{2,0}=\iota(H^{2,0}(X))$. This is a K3 period: $Q(\sigma,\sigma)=c\,q(\sigma,\sigma)=0$ and $Q(\sigma,\bar\sigma)=c\,q(\sigma,\bar\sigma)>0$, since $\sigma\in T_{\mathbb C}$. By the surjectivity of the period map there is a K3 surface $\Sigma_0$ with $H^2(\Sigma_0,\mathbb Z)\cong L_0$ as Hodge structures. The map $\iota$ is a morphism of Hodge structures: on $T$ it is injective with $Q=c\,q$, and $\iota(N)\subset\iota(T)^\perp$ because $Q(N,T)=0$. Since $\operatorname{End}_{\mathrm{Hdg}}(T_{\mathbb Q})=\mathbb Q$, $T_{\mathbb Q}$ is irreducible, so $T(\Sigma_0)_{\mathbb Q}=\iota(T)_{\mathbb Q}$ has rank $20$. Its orthogonal complement in $L_0$ is $P$, so $\operatorname{NS}(\Sigma_0)=P$. Composing $\iota$ with $-1$ and with reflections in $(-2)$-classes of $\operatorname{NS}(\Sigma_0)$, which are Hodge isometries of $L_0$ and do not change $Q$, we may take $g$ in the closure of the ample cone. Then $g$ is ample, because no $(-2)$-class is orthogonal to it.

*Positivity.* $X=S^{[2]}$ is the moduli space of sheaves with Mukai vector $v=(1,0,-1)$. By [10, Thm. 12.1] the walls of $\operatorname{Nef}(X)$ come from classes $a\in H^*_{\mathrm{alg}}(S,\mathbb Z)$ with $a^2\ge-2$ and $|(v,a)|\le1$. Those with $(v,a)=0$, $a^2=-2$, and those with $(v,a)=1$, $a^2=0$, give walls of $\operatorname{Mov}(X)$ [10, Thm. 12.3]. Those with $a^2\ge0$, $(v,a)=0$, or $a^2\ge2$, $(v,a)=1$, give hyperplanes $x^\perp$ with $q(x)\ge0$ ($x=a$ or $x=2a-v$), which miss the open positive cone because $N$ has signature $(1,2)$. So $\operatorname{Nef}(X)\ne\operatorname{Mov}(X)$ only if there is $a$ with $a^2=-2$ and $(v,a)=1$, and then $x=2a-v\in N$ has $q(x)=-10$ and $q(x,\Lambda)=2\mathbb Z$. Such a class has the form $x=2\alpha+k\delta$ with $\alpha\in\operatorname{NS}(S)$, so $4\alpha^2-2k^2=-10$. Since $\alpha^2\in4\mathbb Z$, this gives $k^2\equiv5\pmod8$, which is impossible. So $\operatorname{Nef}(X)=\operatorname{Mov}(X)$, and the ample cone is a union of chambers, one of which contains the ample class $h-\varepsilon\delta$ ($0<\varepsilon\ll1$), of the positive cone cut out by the hyperplanes $E^\perp$, $q(E)=-2$.

Here $l^\#$ is determined by $g^2\,q(l^\#)=t$ and $\lambda(h)=8$, so $l^\#=u/2=h/2-\delta$, with $q(l^\#)=2$. Let $E=xh+yf+z\delta$ with $q(E)=16x^2-4y^2-2z^2=-2$. Then $q(l^\#,E)=8x+2z$ and $q(h-\varepsilon\delta,E)=16x+2\varepsilon z$. If $E=\pm\delta$, both are $\pm2$ up to a positive factor. Otherwise $16x^2=4y^2+2z^2-2>z^2$, so $4|x|>|z|$ and both have the sign of $x$. Hence $l^\#$ lies in the chamber of $h-\varepsilon\delta$, so it is ample. Finally $q(l^\#)>0$ and $N$ has signature $(1,2)$, so reverse Cauchy–Schwarz gives $\lambda(x)^2\ge q(x)\,q(l^\#)$ for $x\in\operatorname{Pos}$. Hence $M(x)\ge t\,q(x)=20\,q(x)$ and $B(x)\ge12\,q(x)>0$ on $\operatorname{Pos}$, and $B(l^\#)=M(l^\#)-8\cdot2=40-16=24$. This proves Proposition C. $\square$

*Remark 4.1.* A surface $\Sigma$ realizing Proposition C would have $\lambda(\delta)=2>0$, as Lemma 3.4 requires, so it would meet the exceptional divisor along a curve in $|2\,\iota(\delta)|=|4g|$. Its preimage in $\operatorname{Bl}_\Delta(S\times S)$ is a double cover $\Gamma\to\Sigma$ branched along this curve, with projections $f_1,f_2\colon\Gamma\to S$ exchanged by the involution. Since $[S_p]=s_\delta$ [1, Lemma 3.1], each $f_i$ has degree $\int_X\gamma\,s_\delta=14$. For $x\in T(S)$ the class $f_1^*x+f_2^*x$ is the pullback of $j^*\tilde x$, so $\int_\Gamma(f_1^*x+f_2^*x)^2=2c\,x^2=16x^2$, and hence $\int_\Gamma f_1^*x\,f_2^*x=-6x^2$. As $\operatorname{End}_{\mathrm{Hdg}}(T(S)_{\mathbb Q})=\mathbb Q$, the correspondence $\Gamma\subset S\times S$ would act on $T(S)$ as $-6$. On $\operatorname{NS}(S)$, the relation $\int_\Gamma(f_1^*a+f_2^*a)(f_1^*b+f_2^*b)=2M(a,b)$ gives $\Gamma_*h=26h$ and $\Gamma_*f=-14f$. So the image $\bar\Gamma\subset S\times S$ would have class $-6[\Delta]+20([\mathrm{pt}\times S]+[S\times\mathrm{pt}])+2\,h\times h+2\,f\times f$, with $\bar\Gamma^2=1984$. Suppose moreover that $\Sigma$ meets $E$ transversally along a smooth curve $C$. Then $C$ has genus $81$, $\chi(\mathcal O_\Gamma)=24$, $K_\Gamma^2=80$, and the image of $C$ in $S$ has class $10h$, because $h|_E$ is twice the pullback of $h$. If $\Gamma\to S\times S$ is an immersion with transverse double points, the double point formula gives $720$ of them. They lie on the diagonal, since $\Gamma\to\bar\Gamma$ is an isomorphism off it. If $C$ maps birationally onto a nodal curve, its nodes give exactly such double points, and their number is $p_a-g(C)=801-81=720$. So these invariants are consistent with each other and give no obstruction (`gamma_corr.py`). The class $\gamma$ is a target for a construction; we have not found one.

## 5. Remarks

*Remark 5.1 (census).* The scripts `rho3_census.py`, `rho3_g3.py` and `rho3_g1x.py` search Hilbert squares $X=S^{[2]}$ with $\rho(S)=2$. They take even binary forms $\operatorname{NS}(S)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2a&b\\b&2a'\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ with $1\le a\le20$, $|b|\le a$, and determinant $D<0$ such that $c^2(c-t)/D\in\mathbb Z$ (by the determinant identity of [1, Lemma 4.1(e)] in rank three, $\det B|_{L'}=2c^2(c-t)/D$, and this lies in $2\mathbb Z$ because $B|_{L'}$ is even), and primitive forms $l$ on $L'$ with coefficients of absolute value at most $8$. For each $(c,t)$ of Theorem A they keep the pairs $(\operatorname{NS}(S),l)$ that pass three tests in turn.
- (i) The integrality conditions of [1, Lemma 4.1(a)–(c)] for $B$ on $L'$, and (2.2). This leaves $1868$, $1584$, $1888$, $1858$ hits (counted with repetition: the search does not reduce modulo $\mathrm O(N)$) for $c=3,7,8,12$; integrality removes no multiplier.
- (ii) Positivity. First $B\ge0$ on $\operatorname{Nef}(S)\subset\operatorname{Nef}(X)$, then $B\ge0$ on $\operatorname{Nef}(X)$, $\lambda\ge0$ on $\operatorname{Mov}(X)$, and $\lambda(D)\ge0$ for every prime exceptional $D$ whose wall is a face of $\operatorname{Nef}(X)$ (Lemma 3.3). The cones come from [10, Thms. 12.1, 12.3]. This step uses floating point arithmetic with a safety margin, so it is a heuristic filter, not a proof. No case with $c\in\{3,7\}$ survives; $503$ cases with $c=8$ and $515$ with $c=12$ do. They give $241$ and $228$ distinct pairs $(\operatorname{NS}(S),\vartheta)$.
- (iii) The lattice $H^2(\Sigma,\mathbb Z)$, as in Section 4: an even unimodular $L_0\supset\Lambda/K$ of signature $(3,19)$ in which $g^\perp\cap\operatorname{NS}(\Sigma)$ contains no $(-2)$-class. The test is exact. It uses the discriminant forms of $R_3$ and $W(c)$ and asks for an isotropic subgroup $H$ with $H^\perp/H\cong\langle1/2m\rangle$ (the discriminant form of $\langle-2m\rangle$ with sign reversed), $m\ge2$. Run on one survivor of (ii) for each pair $(\operatorname{NS}(S),\vartheta)$, it passes for $127$ of the $241$ pairs with $c=8$ and $73$ of the $228$ with $c=12$.

The full $H^4(X,\mathbb Z)$ pairing test was run on samples and agrees with (i). Proposition C is one of the $c=8$ survivors, re-done exactly. An example for $c=12$ is $\operatorname{NS}(S)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}10&2\\2&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ with $\vartheta=10$. There $\det R_3=-1440$ and (iii) passes with $2m=10$, but $N$ contains classes with $q=-10$ and divisibility $2$, so $\operatorname{Nef}(X)\ne\operatorname{Mov}(X)$ and the positivity for this example rests only on (ii).

**Proposition 5.2 (the flop case).** In the situation of Theorem A, assume that $X$ is a moduli space of Bridgeland-stable objects on a K3 surface and that $c\in\{3,7\}$. Then $\Sigma$ lies in a prime exceptional divisor $D$ with $q(D)=-2$, and there is a dominant rational map $\Sigma\dashrightarrow Y$ of degree $c$ onto a K3 surface $Y$ with $T(Y)_{\mathbb Q}\cong T(X)_{\mathbb Q}$.

*Proof.* First, $\lambda\ge0$ on $\overline{\operatorname{Mov}}(X)$. A big movable class is nef, hence semiample, on some birational model $X'$ of $X$ that is again a moduli space [10, Thm. 1.2], and the base locus of its multiples on $X$ lies in the indeterminacy locus $I$ of $X\dashrightarrow X'$. The locus $I$ is covered by rational curves. Indeed, $X\dashrightarrow X'$ and its inverse are isomorphisms in codimension one between smooth varieties, so on its domain of definition each of them contracts no curve (by purity of the exceptional locus) and is an open immersion. Hence $I$ is the union of the total transforms in $X$ of the points where $X'\dashrightarrow X$ is not defined, and each of these is covered by rational curves [12, Cor. 1.7]. Since $I$ has codimension at least two, $\Sigma\subset I$ would make $\Sigma$ an irreducible component of $I$ covered by rational curves. So $\Sigma\not\subset I$. Hence a general member of a multiple of the linear system does not contain $\Sigma$, its restriction to $\Sigma$ is effective, and $\lambda\ge0$ on the class; by continuity $\lambda\ge0$ on $\overline{\operatorname{Mov}}(X)$. Since $q(l^\#)<0$, Lemma 3.2 gives a prime exceptional divisor $D\supset\Sigma$. The hyperplane $D^\perp$ meets $\overline{\operatorname{Mov}}(X)$ in a face of codimension one [9, Sec. 1.5]. Its general points are big, so by [10, Thm. 1.2] one of them lies on a codimension-one face $D^\perp\cap\operatorname{Nef}(X')$ for a birational model $X'$ that is again a moduli space. This face lies on the boundary of $\overline{\operatorname{Mov}}(X')=\overline{\operatorname{Mov}}(X)$, so the corresponding contraction is not small; it is divisorial, and its exceptional divisor $E'$ is prime exceptional with $[E']$ proportional to $[D']$, as in the proof of Theorem B. If $E'\ne D'$, then $q(E',D')\ge0$ for these distinct prime divisors [8, Prop. 4.2(ii)], while proportionality and $q(D')<0$ give $q(E',D')<0$. So $E'=D'$. As in the proof of Theorem B, there is an injective morphism $\nu\colon P\to X'$ with image $D'$, where $\pi\colon P\to Y$ is a $\mathbb P^1$-bundle over a K3 surface and the fibres of $\pi$ map onto fibres of the contraction.

Let $a\colon Z\to X$, $b\colon Z\to X'$ be a resolution of the closure of the graph. The isometry $H^2(X,\mathbb Z)\to H^2(X',\mathbb Z)$, $x\mapsto x'=b_*a^*x$, is a Hodge isometry for $q$ [9, Sec. 3]. For $x\in T$, $a^*x-b^*x'$ lies in $\ker b_*\subset H^2(Z,\mathbb Q)$, which is spanned by classes of $b$-exceptional divisors. So $x\mapsto a^*x-b^*x'$ is a morphism of Hodge structures from $T_{\mathbb Q}$ to a space of type $(1,1)$. Its kernel contains $H^{2,0}$, so it is all of $T_{\mathbb Q}$, and $a^*x=b^*x'$.

As above, $\Sigma$ is not contained in the indeterminacy locus of $X\dashrightarrow X'$, since that locus is covered by rational curves. Let $\rho\colon\hat\Sigma\to Z$ be a resolution of the strict transform of $\Sigma$. Then $a\circ\rho\colon\hat\Sigma\to\Sigma$ is birational, $b\circ\rho$ maps $\hat\Sigma$ birationally onto a surface in $D'$, and it lifts to $\psi\colon\hat\Sigma\to P$ with $\nu\circ\psi=b\circ\rho$: since $\nu$ is finite and injective, a component of the preimage of $b(\rho(\hat\Sigma))$ maps birationally onto it, and $\hat\Sigma$ maps to the normalization of both. The map $\pi\circ\psi$ is dominant, since otherwise $\hat\Sigma$ would be birational to a ruled surface. Let $d$ be its degree. As in [1, Lemma 5.3], $\nu^*x'=\pi^*\varphi(x)$ for a morphism of Hodge structures $\varphi\colon T_{\mathbb Q}\to H^2(Y,\mathbb Q)$, and $q(D)=q(D')=\big(\int_Y\varphi(x)^2/q(x)\big)\,([D']\cdot\nu_*\ell)$ with $[D']\cdot\nu_*\ell=-2$ (Lemma 3.3). Hence
$$c\,q(x)=\int_{\hat\Sigma}(\rho^*a^*x)^2=\int_{\hat\Sigma}(\psi^*\pi^*\varphi(x))^2=d\int_Y\varphi(x)^2=d\cdot\tfrac{-q(D)}2\,q(x).$$
Since $q(D)\in\{-2,-8\}$ and $4\nmid c$, we get $q(D)=-2$ and $d=c$. Then $\varphi$ is an isometric embedding, and its image is a rational Hodge structure of rank $20$ containing $H^{2,0}(Y)$. It contains $T(Y)_{\mathbb Q}$ and is irreducible, because $T_{\mathbb Q}$ is, so it equals $T(Y)_{\mathbb Q}$. $\square$

*Remark 5.3.* A Galois map $\Sigma\dashrightarrow Y$ of prime degree $c$ is a quotient by a symplectic automorphism of order $c$, which needs $\rho(\Sigma)\ge9$ [13]. So the map of Proposition 5.2 is not Galois. Non-Galois dominant maps between K3 surfaces of Picard rank $2$ exist: a general $(2,3)$-divisor in $\mathbb P^1\times\mathbb P^2$ has a rational self-map of degree $16$ that is multiplication by $4$ on the elliptic fibres [14, Ex. 1.5]. That degree is a square. Degrees $3$ and $7$ occur when $T$ has complex multiplication. The general K3 surface $y^2=x^3+p_{12}(t)$ has $\operatorname{NS}=U$ [15, Prop. 4.2, Thm. 3.3], so the Mordell–Weil group of its generic fibre $E/\mathbb C(t)$ is trivial [11, Prop. 11.3.2]. With $\zeta_3$ acting by $x\mapsto\zeta_3x$, each $\alpha\in\mathbb Z[\zeta_3]$ is an endomorphism of $E$ of degree $|\alpha|^2$, which is $3$ for $\alpha=1-\zeta_3$ and $7$ for $\alpha=3+\zeta_3$. A deck transformation of $\alpha$ fixes $t$, so it is $x\mapsto\beta x+P$ with $\beta\in\mu_6$ and $P\in E(\mathbb C(t))=0$, and $\alpha\beta=\alpha$ forces $\beta=1$. So these self-maps are not Galois. Here $\operatorname{End}_{\mathrm{Hdg}}(T_{\mathbb Q})\supseteq\mathbb Q(\zeta_3)$, so they are not of the kind Proposition 5.2 produces. We do not know whether non-Galois maps of degree $3$ or $7$ exist between K3 surfaces of Picard rank $2$ with $\operatorname{End}_{\mathrm{Hdg}}(T_{\mathbb Q})=\mathbb Q$; if not, the conclusion $c\in\{8,12\}$ of Theorem B holds without the assumption $\operatorname{Nef}(X)=\operatorname{Mov}(X)$. In the census, $710$ of the $750$ cases with $c=3$ and $526$ of the $540$ with $c=7$ fail one of the tests that are proofs once the cones are known: $B\ge0$ on $\operatorname{Nef}(X)$ (Lemma 5.1 of [1]), or $\lambda(D)\ge0$ for a divisor $D$ whose wall bounds $\operatorname{Nef}(X)$ (Lemmas 3.1 and 3.3). In the remaining $54$ cases, $\operatorname{NS}(S)\cong U$, and every divisor $D$ the census finds with $\lambda(D)<0$ on a wall of $\operatorname{Mov}(X)$ has $q(D)=-2$ (a floating-point computation, `rho3_g3_div.py`). Lemma 3.5 excludes all $54$. The roots of $U$ are $\pm R$, where $R$ is the class of the unique smooth rational curve (an effective $(-2)$-class contains a $(-2)$-curve, whose class must be $R$), and in each of the $54$ cases $\lambda(\tilde R)<0$ in exact integer arithmetic (`rho3_root.py`). Lemma 3.1 then puts $\Sigma$ in $D_R$, and Lemma 3.5 gives $c\le1$. The same exact test excludes $244$ of the $1290$ census cases with $c\in\{3,7\}$ directly. Only the selection of the $54$ uses floating point.

*Bearing on the Hodge conjecture.* For $X$ as in Theorem A every Hodge class in $H^4(X,\mathbb Q)=\operatorname{Sym}^2H^2(X,\mathbb Q)$ lies in the span of $\operatorname{Sym}^2\operatorname{NS}(X)$ and $c_2(X)$, because $\operatorname{End}_{\mathrm{Hdg}}(T_{\mathbb Q})=\mathbb Q$. So it is algebraic, and $\gamma$ in Proposition C is algebraic. The open question is about $S\times\Sigma_0$. For $X=S^{[2]}$ we have $T(X)\cong T(S)$, and $\iota$ gives a Hodge similarity $T(S)_{\mathbb Q}\to T(\Sigma_0)_{\mathbb Q}$ of multiplier $8$. Its class in $H^4(S\times\Sigma_0,\mathbb Q)$ is a Hodge class, and the Hodge conjecture predicts that it is algebraic. Results on rational Hodge isometries [2, 3, 4] do not apply, because $8$ is not a square. A smooth surface $\Sigma\subset X$ in the class $\gamma$ would prove algebraicity on an $18$-dimensional family, larger than the families of dimension at most $12$ known so far [5, 6, 7]. This note does not construct $\Sigma$. Its results are the reduction to $c\in\{3,7,8,12\}$ (Theorem A), the exclusion of $3$ and $7$ under $\operatorname{Nef}=\operatorname{Mov}$ (Theorem B), the reduction of the general case for $3$ and $7$ to non-Galois maps of degree $c$ between K3 surfaces (Proposition 5.2), and an explicit candidate class that passes all the tests we know (Proposition C).
