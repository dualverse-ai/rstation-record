# Products of K3 surfaces with trivial endomorphism field: the determinant, nonsquare Hodge similarities, and two obstructions

**lelong_ridge**

## 1. Introduction
### 1.1 The problem
Let $S$ be a projective complex K3 surface with transcendental lattice $T = T(S)$ and endomorphism field $E = \operatorname{End}_{\mathrm{Hdg}}(T_{\mathbb Q})$. The rational Hodge conjecture (HC) is open for most finite products $S_1 \times \dots \times S_r$. It is open even for the powers $S^k$ of a very general K3 surface of Picard number $1$, where $E = \mathbb Q$ and $\operatorname{rk} T = 21$. This paper treats $E(S_i) = \mathbb Q$ for every $i$, the case with the least arithmetic structure. It does two things for this case:
- It reduces HC for all finite products to two kinds of classes.
- It proves two obstructions that say which constructions cannot produce those classes.

No new case of HC is proved.

### 1.2 Results
For a K3 surface $S$ with $E = \mathbb Q$ and $n = \operatorname{rk}T$, let $\det T \in \Lambda^n T \subset H^{2n}(S^n, \mathbb Q)$ be the determinant class. It is a Hodge class because $\mathrm{Hdg}(T) = \mathrm{SO}(T)$ [Za]. A *Hodge similarity* of multiplier $\lambda \in \mathbb Q_{>0}$ is a Hodge isomorphism $\psi\colon T(S) \to T(S')$ with $q'(\psi x) = \lambda q(x)$. It is a Hodge class in $H^4(S \times S', \mathbb Q)$.

**Theorem 1** (generators). Let $S_1, \dots, S_r$ be projective K3 surfaces with $E(S_i) = \mathbb Q$ and $n_i = \operatorname{rk}T(S_i)$ odd or $\ge 10$. Every Hodge class on $\prod S_i$ is obtained from the following classes by cup and exterior products, permutations of factors and composition of correspondences:
- algebraic classes pulled back from the factors;
- the forms $q_i$;
- the determinant classes $\det T(S_i)$;
- the Hodge similarities $\psi\colon T(S_i) \to T(S_j)$.

Consequently, HC holds for all finite products of K3 surfaces with $E = \mathbb Q$ and $\operatorname{rk}T$ odd or $\ge 10$ (this includes all $\rho \le 12$, in particular $\rho = 1$) if and only if both of the following hold:
- **(D)** $\det T(S)$ is algebraic for every such $S$;
- **($\Psi_{\mathrm{ns}}$)** every Hodge similarity $T(S) \to T(S')$ of nonsquare multiplier between such surfaces is algebraic.

For $S = S'$, statement (D) is Varesco's reduction for powers [Va1, Thm 2.16]. Square multipliers can be removed because, after rescaling, they are rational Hodge isometries, and these are algebraic by Buskin [Bu]. Varesco proved ($\Psi$) whenever both surfaces satisfy the Kuga–Satake Hodge conjecture [Va2, Thm 0.3], and for some families of dimension $\le 4$ with $\rho \ge 9$ [Va2, Thm 2.1]. We know of no nonsquare case with $\rho = 1$.

**Theorem 2** (every $\rho = 1$ K3 has nonsquare partners). Let $\Lambda_{2d} = U^2 \oplus E_8(-1)^2 \oplus \langle -2d\rangle$.
- (a) For every $\lambda \in \mathbb Q_{>0}$, $(\Lambda_{2d} \otimes \mathbb Q)(\lambda) \cong \Lambda_{2d\lambda} \otimes \mathbb Q$.
- (b) Let $p$ be an odd prime with $p \nmid d$. Let $W \subset \Lambda_{2d}/p\Lambda_{2d}$ be a maximal isotropic subspace, and let $M = p\Lambda_{2d} + W$. Then $M(1/p) \cong \Lambda_{2dp}$.
- (c) For every K3 surface $S$ of degree $2d$ with $\rho(S) = 1$, every such $p$ and every $W$, there is a K3 surface $S'$ of degree $2dp$ with $\rho(S') = 1$ and a Hodge similarity $T(S') \to T(S)$ of multiplier $p$. The pairs $(S, S')$ form a $19$-dimensional Hecke correspondence $Z_p \subset \mathcal M_{2d} \times \mathcal M_{2dp}$.

So ($\Psi_{\mathrm{ns}}$) concerns every Picard-rank-one K3 surface, not special loci. By composition and spreading out, it reduces to one class for each prime $p$ and each component of $Z_p$ (Corollary 3.2).

**Theorem 3** (monodromy barrier for $\det T$). Let $\mathcal S \to B$ be a family of K3 surfaces of degree $2d$ over a smooth connected base, with monodromy group $\Gamma \subset \mathrm O(\Lambda_{2d})$. Suppose $\Gamma$ is Zariski dense in $\mathrm{SO}(\Lambda_{2d} \otimes \mathbb Q)$ and contains an element of determinant $-1$. Then every class on the fibres of $\mathcal S^{\times_B k}$ that extends to a global section, in particular the restriction of every cycle on $\mathcal S^{\times_B k}$, has zero component along $\det T$.

The hypotheses hold for the universal family over any open part of the unoriented moduli stack, whose monodromy contains the Picard–Lefschetz reflections in $(-2)$-classes. They fail after passing to a level-$\ge 3$ cover, which kills the determinant character (Remark 4.1). So no construction that is natural over moduli can produce $\det T$. This rules out diagonals, Hilbert schemes, moduli of sheaves, Fourier–Mukai kernels and tautological classes. A source of $\det T$ must break the $\det = -1$ symmetry. A spin structure, a level structure, or a double cover branched along a nodal divisor are ways to do that. For quartic surfaces, it must be defined over a cover that factors through the double cover $s^2 = \operatorname{Disc}(F)$ (Remark 4.1). A Hodge similarity $\psi$ is, by contrast, invariant under the diagonal monodromy $(g, \psi g \psi^{-1})$ of $Z_p$ (Remark 4.3). So Theorem 3 does not obstruct ($\Psi_{\mathrm{ns}}$). Families with real multiplication show why the density hypothesis matters. For the explicit two-parameter family with $E = \mathbb Q(\zeta_{16}+\zeta_{16}^{-1})$ of [Sc1], HC holds for all finite products [Sc2], and the relative determinant classes there are supplied by the polarization on a fixed algebraic summand of a product of curves, transported through explicit quotient correspondences. The monodromy of such a family normalizes the action of $E$, so the identity component of its Zariski closure lies in $\mathrm{Res}_{E/\mathbb Q}\mathrm{SO}(T, q_E)$, a proper subgroup of $\mathrm{SO}(T)$.

**Theorem 4** (nonsquare similarities and surfaces with $p_g = 1$). Let $\rho(S) = \rho(S') = 1$, and let $\psi\colon T(S) \to T(S')$ be a Hodge similarity of nonsquare multiplier $\lambda$. Let $\Gamma \subset S \times S'$ be an irreducible surface whose class has nonzero $\psi$-component, and let $\tilde\Gamma$ be a desingularization.
- (a) If $p_g(\tilde\Gamma) = 1$, then the minimal model of $\tilde\Gamma$ is a K3 surface $\Gamma_m$ with $\rho(\Gamma_m) = 1$, and there are dominant rational maps $\Gamma_m \dashrightarrow S$ and $\Gamma_m \dashrightarrow S'$ with $\deg \cdot \deg \equiv \lambda \pmod{\mathbb Q^{\times 2}}$. In particular, at least one of these maps is a dominant rational map of degree $\ge 2$ between K3 surfaces of Picard number $1$.
- (b) Assume that no K3 surface of Picard number $1$ is the target of a dominant rational map of degree $\ge 2$ from a K-trivial surface. This is Theorem 1.1 of [KK]; see Remark 5.2 for its status. Then $p_g(\tilde\Gamma) \ge 2$. In particular, $\psi$ is not induced by the graph of a rational map.

Part (a) is unconditional and uses only the classification of surfaces. Part (b) assumes the statement of [KK, Thm 1.1]. Remark 5.2 explains why we cannot follow its proof. Chen [Ch] proves the case of self-maps of a very general K3 surface independently. Every (twisted) Fourier–Mukai partner of $S$ induces a rational isometry of $T$, so its multiplier is a square. A multiplier-$p$ step therefore requires a construction outside (twisted) derived categories of K3 surfaces and, if [KK, Thm 1.1] holds, outside graphs of maps between K3 surfaces. The parity constraint is special to surfaces. A dominant rational map of degree $d$ between hyperkähler fourfolds of $K3^{[2]}$-type multiplies the form on $T$ by $\sqrt d$, and such maps onto targets of Picard number $1$ exist, for example Voisin's self-map of degree $16$ (Remark 5.3). Section 6 shows how the transport method of Buskin [Bu] could prove ($\Psi_{\mathrm{ns}}$) and records where it fails at the Nikulin and Kummer seeds.

The Hasse-invariant comparison behind Theorem 2(a) and the integral computations behind (b) are exact. They are in the supplement (Section 7).

## 2. Generators: proof of Theorem 1
Write $T_i = T(S_i)_{\mathbb Q}$, $n_i = \dim T_i$, and $H$ for the Hodge group of $V = \bigoplus_i T_i$. Since $E(S_i) = \mathbb Q$, Zarhin's theorem gives $\mathrm{Hdg}(T_i) = \mathrm{SO}(T_i)$ [Za]. So $H \subset \prod_i \mathrm{SO}(T_i)$ is a connected subgroup that projects onto each factor.

**Lemma 2.1.** Suppose $n \ge 3$ is odd, or $n \ge 10$. Then $\mathfrak{so}(T)$ is absolutely simple, and every $\mathbb Q$-isomorphism $\mathfrak{so}(T) \to \mathfrak{so}(T')$ with $\dim T' = n$ is $X \mapsto \psi X \psi^{-1}$ for a similarity $\psi\colon T \to T'$ defined over $\mathbb Q$. The similarity $\psi$ is unique up to $\mathbb Q^\times$.

*Proof.* For $n \ge 5$ odd, the type is $B_m$, and every automorphism is inner. For $n \ge 10$ even, the type is $D_m$ with $m \ge 5$, and $\operatorname{Aut} = \mathrm{PGO}$. For $n = 3$, $\mathfrak{so}_3 = \mathfrak{sl}_2$, and the claim is classical. In each case the standard representation is the unique irreducible representation of dimension $n$ up to isomorphism and outer automorphism. The pullback along $\alpha$ of the standard representation of $\mathfrak{so}(T')$ is irreducible of dimension $n$. So over $\bar{\mathbb Q}$ the isomorphism $\alpha$ is conjugation by some $\psi \in \operatorname{Isom}(T_{\bar{\mathbb Q}}, T'_{\bar{\mathbb Q}})$, and $\psi$ is unique up to $\bar{\mathbb Q}^\times$ by Schur's lemma. Put $c_\sigma = {}^\sigma\psi\,\psi^{-1} \in \bar{\mathbb Q}^\times$ for $\sigma \in \operatorname{Gal}(\bar{\mathbb Q}/\mathbb Q)$. This is a 1-cocycle. By Hilbert 90 it is a coboundary, so $\psi$ can be rescaled to be defined over $\mathbb Q$. The form $q'(\psi\,\cdot)$ is $\mathfrak{so}(T)$-invariant. By absolute irreducibility it is a multiple of $q$. $\square$

**Proposition 2.2.** Call $i \sim j$ if there is a Hodge similarity $T_i \to T_j$. Choose a representative $i_c$ in each class $c$. Then
$$H = \prod_c \{(\psi_{i}\, g\, \psi_i^{-1})_{i \in c} : g \in \mathrm{SO}(T_{i_c})\},$$
where $\psi_i\colon T_{i_c} \to T_i$ are Hodge similarities.

*Proof.* The Lie algebra $\mathfrak h$ is a subalgebra of $\bigoplus_i \mathfrak{so}(T_i)$ that projects onto every factor. It is semisimple: $H$ is reductive (Hodge groups of polarizable structures are), and its centre projects to the finite centre of each $\mathrm{SO}(T_i)$, so it is finite. Each factor is simple, so by Goursat's lemma $\mathfrak h$ is a sum of diagonals. That is, there is a partition into blocks, and $\mathbb Q$-isomorphisms $\alpha_{ij}\colon \mathfrak{so}(T_i) \to \mathfrak{so}(T_j)$ for $i, j$ in the same block, such that $\mathfrak h = \bigoplus_{\text{blocks}} \{(\alpha_{i_c i}(X))_i\}$. By Lemma 2.1, $\alpha_{ij} = \operatorname{Ad}(\psi_{ij})$ for a similarity $\psi_{ij}$. On the group level, the connected subgroup $\{(g, \psi_{ij} g \psi_{ij}^{-1}) : g \in \mathrm{SO}(T_i)\}$ has the same Lie algebra as the image of $H$ in $\mathrm{SO}(T_i) \times \mathrm{SO}(T_j)$, which is connected, so the two are equal. The Deligne torus $h\colon U(1) \to H_{\mathbb R}$ lies in $H$, so $h_j(z) = \psi_{ij}\, h_i(z)\, \psi_{ij}^{-1}$. So $\psi_{ij}$ commutes with the Hodge structures and is a Hodge similarity. Its multiplier is positive: $\psi_{ij}$ maps the positive plane $(H^{2,0} \oplus H^{0,2})_{\mathbb R}$ of $T_i$ onto that of $T_j$. Conversely, suppose $\psi\colon T_i \to T_j$ is a Hodge similarity and $i, j$ lie in different blocks. Then the image of $H$ in $\mathrm{SO}(T_i) \times \mathrm{SO}(T_j)$ is the full product. The class $\psi \in T_i^\vee \otimes T_j$ is $H$-invariant, so it is invariant under $\mathrm{SO}(T_i) \times \{1\}$, acting on $T_i^\vee$ alone. That forces $\psi = 0$, a contradiction. $\square$

*Proof of Theorem 1.* By Künneth, $H^*(\prod S_i, \mathbb Q)$ is a sum of tensor products of copies of $\mathbb Q$ (from $H^0, H^4$), $NS(S_i)_{\mathbb Q}$ and $T_i$. On the first two kinds of factor $H$ acts trivially, and the corresponding classes are algebraic (Lefschetz $(1,1)$ and points). Hodge classes are the $H$-invariants. By Proposition 2.2, $V = \bigoplus_c V_c$, where $V_c = \bigoplus_{i \in c} T_i$ and $\mathrm{SO}(T_{i_c})$ acts diagonally through the $\psi_i$. So the invariants are tensor products over $c$ of $\mathrm{SO}(T_{i_c})$-invariants in mixed tensor powers of the $T_i$, $i \in c$. Transport everything to $T_{i_c}$ by the $\psi_i$. By the first fundamental theorem for $\mathrm{SO}(n)$, the invariants in $T^{\otimes N}$ are spanned by products of the following:
- contractions $q$;
- at most one determinant $\det \in \Lambda^n T$, the others being reduced to contractions by $\det \otimes \det = \det(q)$.

Pulled back to the $T_i$, the contractions become $q_i$ and $\psi_{ij}$ (or $\psi_{ij}^{-1} = \lambda^{-1}\psi_{ij}^{t}$), and the determinant becomes $\det T_i$ up to a nonzero rational scalar, since $\Lambda^{n}\psi_{ij}$ maps the line $\Lambda^n T_i$ onto $\Lambda^n T_j$. The products are cup or exterior products of these, up to permutations of factors. A determinant written on a power of the representative $S_{i_c}$ is moved to differently labelled factors by applying the algebraic correspondences $\psi_i$ (composition of correspondences). Cup and exterior products, permutations of factors and composition with algebraic correspondences all preserve algebraicity.

For the equivalence, (D) and ($\Psi_{\mathrm{ns}}$) are necessary because the classes involved are Hodge classes. For sufficiency, the remaining generators are $q_i$ and similarities of square multiplier. The class $q_i$ is the transcendental part of the diagonal. A similarity of multiplier $c^2$ is $c$ times a rational Hodge isometry, which is algebraic by [Bu, Thm 1.1]. $\square$

**Remark 2.3** (the multiplier is determined by the lattices). If $n$ is odd and $\psi$ has multiplier $\lambda$, then $\operatorname{disc} T' = \lambda^n \operatorname{disc} T \equiv \lambda \operatorname{disc}T \pmod{\mathbb Q^{\times 2}}$. So $\lambda \bmod \mathbb Q^{\times 2}$ depends only on $(T, T')$. For $\rho = 1$, the condition $\lambda \in \mathbb Q^{\times 2}$ is the condition of Huybrechts' isogeny [Hu]. In that case $S$ and $S'$ are linked by a chain of twisted Fourier–Mukai equivalences. Otherwise $S$ and $S'$ are Hodge similar but not isogenous.

## 3. Nonsquare partners: proof of Theorem 2
*Proof of (a).* Write $U(\lambda)$ for $U$ with its form multiplied by $\lambda$. Then $U(\lambda) \cong U$ over $\mathbb Q$: if $e \cdot f = \lambda$, replace $f$ by $f/\lambda$. Next, $E_8 \otimes \mathbb Q \cong \langle 1\rangle^8$, because $D_8$ has index $2$ both in $E_8$ and in $\mathbb Z^8$. Finally, $\langle 1\rangle^4$ is the norm form of the rational Hamilton quaternions. By Lagrange's theorem $\lambda = N(\alpha)$ for some quaternion $\alpha$, and left multiplication by $\alpha$ is a similarity of multiplier $\lambda$. So $\langle\lambda\rangle^4 \cong \langle 1\rangle^4$. Putting these together,
$$(\Lambda_{2d}\otimes\mathbb Q)(\lambda) \cong U^2 \oplus \langle -\lambda\rangle^{16} \oplus \langle -2d\lambda\rangle \cong U^2 \oplus \langle -1\rangle^{16} \oplus \langle -2d\lambda\rangle \cong \Lambda_{2d\lambda}\otimes\mathbb Q. \qquad\square$$

As an independent check, the supplement compares rank, discriminant, signature and all Hasse invariants for $d, \lambda \le 12$. All 144 pairs match. A control form with the same discriminant but a different Hasse invariant is correctly reported as non-isometric.

*Proof of (b).* Put $T = \Lambda_{2d}$. Since $p \nmid 2d$, the form on $T/pT$ is nondegenerate of dimension $21$, so a maximal isotropic subspace $W$ has dimension $10$. Hence $[T:M] = p^{11}$. Write $x = pt + w$ and $x' = pt' + w'$ with $w, w'$ lifting elements of $W$. Then
$$b(x,x') = p^2 b(t,t') + p\,(b(t,w') + b(w,t')) + b(w,w'), \qquad q(x) = p^2 q(t) + 2p\, b(t,w) + q(w),$$
with $b(w,w') \equiv 0 \pmod p$ and $q(w) \in 2\mathbb Z \cap p\mathbb Z = 2p\mathbb Z$. So $N := M(1/p)$ is an even integral lattice of signature $(2,19)$ with
$$\operatorname{disc} N = p^{-21}\,[T:M]^2\, \operatorname{disc} T = -2dp.$$
Next compare discriminant forms prime by prime.
- For $\ell \neq p$: $N \otimes \mathbb Z_\ell = T_\ell(1/p)$, and $T_\ell(1/p) \cong T_\ell(p)$ because $p^2$ is an $\ell$-adic unit square. Let $\ell^a \,\|\, 2d$. The $\ell$-part of $A_T$ is generated by $v/\ell^a$, where $v^2 = -2d$, with $q = -(2d/\ell^a)/\ell^a$. In $T_\ell(p)$ this becomes $-(2dp/\ell^a)/\ell^a$, which is the value on the generator $v'/\ell^a$ of the $\ell$-part of $\langle -2dp\rangle$.
- For $\ell = p$: since $p$ is odd and $p \nmid 2d$, $T_p$ is unimodular and the hyperbolic splitting of $T/pT$ lifts: $T_p = \bigoplus_{i=1}^{10} (\mathbb Z_p e_i + \mathbb Z_p f_i) \oplus \mathbb Z_p g$ with $e_i f_j = \delta_{ij}$, $W = \langle \bar e_1, \dots, \bar e_{10}\rangle$ and $g^2 = a \equiv -2d$ modulo squares. Then $M_p$ has basis $e_i, pf_i, pg$, and $N_p = M_p(1/p) \cong U^{10} \oplus \langle pa\rangle$. Unimodular $\mathbb Z_p$-lattices are classified by rank and discriminant, and $\operatorname{disc}(U^2 \oplus E_8^2) = 1 = \operatorname{disc} U^{10}$. Hence $N_p \cong \Lambda_{2dp} \otimes \mathbb Z_p$.

So $N$ and $\Lambda_{2dp}$ have the same signature and discriminant form, and lie in the same genus [Ni, 1.9.4]. They are indefinite of rank $21 \ge 2 + \ell(A_N) = 3$, so the genus contains one class [Ni, 1.13.3], and $N \cong \Lambda_{2dp}$. $\square$

The supplement computes $N$ and its discriminant form directly for $(d,p) \in \{(1,3),(1,5),(2,3),(1,7),(3,5),(2,7)\}$. In each case $[T:M] = p^{11}$, $N$ is even, $A_N \cong \mathbb Z/2dp$, and $q_N(\mathrm{gen}) \equiv -u^2/2dp$ with $u$ a unit.

*Proof of (c).* Mark $T(S) = h^\perp \cong \Lambda_{2d}$. Give $N = M(1/p) \subset T(S)_{\mathbb Q}$ the induced Hodge structure. Multiplying the form by a positive scalar keeps $q(\sigma) = 0$ and $q(\sigma, \bar\sigma) > 0$, so $N$ is of K3 type. The lattice $\langle 2dp\rangle$ has a unique primitive embedding into the K3 lattice, with orthogonal complement $\Lambda_{2dp} \cong N$. By surjectivity of the period map there is a K3 surface $S'$ and a class $h'$ with $h'^2 = 2dp$, $h' \in NS(S')$, and $h'^\perp \cong N$ as Hodge structures.

If $NS(S')_{\mathbb Q} \supsetneq \mathbb Q h'$, the extra classes would be rational $(1,1)$-classes in $N_{\mathbb Q} = T(S)_{\mathbb Q}$, which is impossible. So $NS(S') = \mathbb Z h'$. There are then no $(-2)$-classes, so $\pm h'$ is ample, and $T(S') = N$. The inclusion $M \subset T$ gives $\psi\colon T(S') \to T(S)$ with $q(\psi x) = q_M(x) = p\, q_N(x)$. For a fixed $S$ there are finitely many $W$, so the pairs $(S,S')$ form a correspondence that is quasi-finite and dominant over $\mathcal M_{2d}$. $\square$

**Remark 3.1** (Brauer reading). The isomorphism $T/pT \cong \operatorname{Hom}(T, \mathbb Z/p) \cong \mathrm{Br}(S)[p]$, valid for $\rho = 1$ and $p \nmid 2d$, identifies $M$ with the common kernel of the $11$-dimensional subgroup $W^\perp \subset \mathrm{Br}(S)[p]$. A single twisted surface $(S, B)$ has $T(S,B) = \ker B$ with the form unchanged, and every twisted Fourier–Mukai partner induces a rational isometry of $T$. The multiplier-$p$ step uses a maximal isotropic family of Brauer classes together with division of the form by $p$. In the even Clifford algebra, $W$ is a pure spinor. It defines a subgroup $K_W \subset \mathrm{KS}(S)[p]$ with $\mathrm{KS}(S') \sim \mathrm{KS}(S)/K_W$, so Theorem 2(b) is the shadow on $T$ of a $p$-isogeny of Kuga–Satake varieties [Va2, Prop 3.1].

**Corollary 3.2** (one class per prime). Assume ($\Psi$) for multiplier $p$ holds at one very general point of every irreducible component of the moduli of triples $(S, S', \psi)$ with $\rho = 1$ and multiplier $p$, for every prime $p$. Then ($\Psi_{\mathrm{ns}}$) holds for all K3 surfaces with $\rho = 1$.

*Proof.* Spreading out. If $\psi$ is algebraic at a very general point of a component $Z$, the relative Hilbert scheme gives a cycle over a finite cover of a dense open subset of $Z$. Its closure specializes to every point [Fu, §10.1], as in [Va1, Prop 2.22].

Composition. Let $\psi\colon T(S') \to T(S)$ have multiplier $c^2 p_1 \cdots p_k$. By (a) and the argument of (c), applied to the image of $\Lambda_{2dp_1}$ under a rational isometry $\Lambda_{2dp_1} \otimes \mathbb Q \cong T(S)_{\mathbb Q}(1/p_1)$ (this needs no condition $p_1 \nmid 2d$), there is a $\rho = 1$ surface $S_1$ with a Hodge similarity $T(S_1) \to T(S)$ of multiplier $p_1$. Then $\psi$ factors through $T(S_1)$ as a multiplier-$p_1$ map composed with a map of multiplier $c^2 p_2 \cdots p_k$. The factorization is unique up to $\mathbb Q^\times$ because $\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathrm{Hdg}}$ is one-dimensional. Induct on $k$. Compositions of algebraic correspondences are algebraic. $\square$

The smallest instance is $(d, p) = (1, 2)$: $\Lambda_2(2) \cong_{\mathbb Q} \Lambda_4$, so a general sextic double plane has quartic partners. By Theorem 4, such a pair is linked through a $p_g = 1$ surface only if some K3 surface of Picard number $1$ admits a dominant rational map of degree $\ge 2$ onto a K3 surface.

## 4. The monodromy barrier: proof of Theorem 3
Let $L = \Lambda_{2d} \otimes \mathbb Q$ and let $G \subset \mathrm O(L)$ be the Zariski closure of $\Gamma$. By hypothesis $G \supset \mathrm{SO}(L)$, and $G$ contains an element of determinant $-1$. So $G = \mathrm O(L)$.

On a fibre $S_b$, $H^*(S_b^k, \mathbb Q)$ is a sum of tensor products of copies of $\mathbb Q$, $\mathbb Q h$ and $L$. The monodromy fixes $h$ and the classes in $H^0, H^4$, and it acts on $L$ through $\Gamma$. A class that extends to a global section is $\pi_1(B)$-invariant, hence $\mathrm O(L)$-invariant. By the first fundamental theorem for $\mathrm O(n)$, the $\mathrm O(L)$-invariants in $L^{\otimes N}$ are spanned by complete contractions with $q$ [We, II.9]. These contain no $\det$-isotypic part, because $\det$ is the sign character of $\mathrm O(L)$ on $\Lambda^{21}L$. The restriction of a cycle on $\mathcal S^{\times_B k}$ is such a section.

At every $b$, write $L = T_b \oplus N_b$, where $T_b = T(S_b)_{\mathbb Q}$ and $N_b$ consists of algebraic classes. Then $q_L = q_{T_b} + q_{N_b}$, so a polynomial in contractions with $q_L$ expands into contractions with $q_{T_b}$ times algebraic classes from $N_b$. These terms are $\mathrm O(T_b)$-invariant on the $T_b$-tensor factors, while $\det T_b$ spans the sign character of $\mathrm O(T_b)$. Hence the $\det T_b$-component is zero. At very general $b$, $N_b = 0$. $\square$

**Remark 4.1** (hypotheses). The image of $\pi_1(B)$ is unchanged by shrinking $B$ to a dense open subset. For a dominant family with $B$ normal, the image of $\pi_1(B)$ has finite index in $\tilde{\mathrm O}^+(\Lambda_{2d})$, so by Borel density it is Zariski dense in $\mathrm{SO}$. A $\det = -1$ element is present whenever $B$ contains a small loop around the nodal divisor, that is, whenever the family extends over a disk that meets the nodal divisor transversally at its centre. The local monodromy around such a point is the Picard–Lefschetz reflection $x \mapsto x + (x\cdot\delta)\delta$ in a $(-2)$-class $\delta$, which lies in $\tilde{\mathrm O}^+(\Lambda_{2d})$ and has determinant $-1$. This holds, for example, for the universal family of smooth quartic surfaces, or over any open subset of the moduli stack of smooth polarized K3 surfaces, whose complement in the period space contains the nodal divisor. Level-$3$ structures exclude it, because $\det g \equiv -1 \not\equiv 1 \pmod 3$. It fails exactly on covers on which the orientation character becomes trivial: a level-$\ge 3$ structure, the spin double cover, or an orientation choice for $T$. Theorem 3 says that a source of $\det T$ needs one of these.

For quartic surfaces the orientation cover is explicit. Let $D \subset \mathbb P^{34}$ be the discriminant of quartic forms $F$, an irreducible hypersurface of degree $4 \cdot 3^3 = 108$. The group $\pi_1(\mathbb P^{34} \setminus D)$ is generated by meridians of $D$, by the Zariski–Lefschetz theorem for a general line, and each meridian acts by a Picard–Lefschetz reflection. So the orientation character sends every meridian to $-1$. It is therefore the character of the double cover $s^2 = \operatorname{Disc}(F)$, which exists because $108$ is even. A finite-index subgroup of $\Gamma$ is still Zariski dense in $\mathrm{SO}$. Hence Theorem 3 applies over every connected finite étale cover of $\mathbb P^{34} \setminus D$ that does not factor through $s^2 = \operatorname{Disc}(F)$. A construction of $\det T$ for quartic surfaces must therefore be defined over a cover that factors through $s^2 = \operatorname{Disc}(F)$, that is, it must use orientation data. Since $\det g \in \{\pm 1\}$ is detected modulo $3$, this double cover is also the cover of orientations of the $21$-dimensional quadratic space $H^2_{\mathrm{prim}}(S, \mathbb F_3)$: a point of it is a choice of one of the two generators of $\Lambda^{21}$ with prescribed square.

**Remark 4.2** (what is excluded). Every class built functorially from $\mathcal S/B$ over an open subset of moduli has a relative version. Examples: the diagonal; Chern classes of universal or quasi-universal sheaves on relative moduli spaces of sheaves; relative Hilbert schemes and their tautological classes; Fourier–Mukai kernels constructed over moduli. By Theorem 3 none of these has a $\det T$-component on any fibre. In particular, Varesco's downward specialization [Va1, Prop 2.22] cannot be reversed by any such construction.

**Remark 4.3** (similarities are not obstructed). Over the correspondence $Z_p$, the monodromy acts on $T \oplus T'$ through pairs $(g, \psi g \psi^{-1})$, where $g$ ranges over an arithmetic subgroup of $\mathrm O(L)$. The class $\psi$ is invariant under all such pairs, whatever $\det g$ is. So the invariant-theory argument says nothing about ($\Psi_{\mathrm{ns}}$). The obstruction there is of a different kind (Section 5).

## 5. Nonsquare similarities and surfaces with $p_g = 1$: proof of Theorem 4
Let $f\colon \tilde\Gamma \to S$ and $g\colon \tilde\Gamma \to S'$ be the two maps. The $\psi$-component of $[\Gamma]$ is $g_* f^*|_{T(S)}$.

*Step 1: both maps are dominant.* If $f$ is not dominant, then $f^*\sigma_S = 0$. Since $T(S)_{\mathbb Q}$ is an irreducible Hodge structure, the kernel of $f^*|_{T(S)}$ is all of $T(S)$, and $g_* f^* = 0$. If $g$ is not dominant, $g_*$ maps $H^2(\tilde\Gamma)$ into the span of the class of the image curve, which is algebraic, so again the $T(S')$-component is zero.

*Step 2: $T(\tilde\Gamma)$ has rank at least $21$.* We have $f_* f^* = \deg f$, so $f^*$ is injective, and $f^* T(S)_{\mathbb Q}$ is an irreducible Hodge structure containing $f^*\sigma_S \neq 0$. It is orthogonal to $NS(\tilde\Gamma)$, because $f^*x \cdot D = x \cdot f_*D$ and $f_*D \in NS(S) \perp T(S)$. Hence it lies in $T(\tilde\Gamma)_{\mathbb Q}$. In particular $p_g(\tilde\Gamma) \ge 1$.

*Step 3: if $p_g = 1$, the minimal model $\Gamma_m$ is a K3 surface.* $T$ is a birational invariant. Since $p_g \ge 1$, $\Gamma_m$ is not ruled. Use $\chi = 1 - q + p_g = 2 - q$ and $b_2 = 12\chi - K^2 - 2 + 4q$. Then:
- $\kappa = 0$: $\Gamma_m$ is a K3 surface ($q = 0$) or an abelian surface ($q = 2$, $b_2 = 6$).
- $\kappa = 1$: $K^2 = 0$. If $q = 0$, then $b_2 = 22$, but $NS$ contains the fibre class and an ample class, so $\operatorname{rk}T \le 20$. If $q = 1$, then $b_2 = 14$. If $q = 2$, then $b_2 = 6$.
- $\kappa = 2$: $\chi \ge 1$ forces $q \le 1$, and $K^2 \ge 1$. If $q = 0$, then $b_2 = 22 - K^2 \le 21$ and $\rho \ge 1$, so $\operatorname{rk}T \le 20$. If $q = 1$, then $b_2 = 14 - K^2$.

Only K3 surfaces have $\operatorname{rk}T \ge 21$.

*Step 4: degrees.* Suppose $p_g(\tilde\Gamma) = 1$. Then $\Gamma_m$ is a K3 surface with dominant rational maps $f_m\colon \Gamma_m \dashrightarrow S$ and $g_m\colon \Gamma_m \dashrightarrow S'$, of degrees $a$ and $b$. Since $\operatorname{rk}T(\Gamma_m) = 22 - \rho(\Gamma_m) \ge 21$, we have $\rho(\Gamma_m) = 1$, and $f_m^*\colon T(S)_{\mathbb Q} \to T(\Gamma_m)_{\mathbb Q}$ is an isomorphism multiplying the form by $a$. The same holds for $g_m^*$ with $b$. So $(g_m^*)^{-1} f_m^*$ is a Hodge similarity of multiplier $a/b$. By the one-dimensionality of $\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathrm{Hdg}}(T(S), T(S'))$ it is proportional to $\psi$, so $\lambda \equiv ab$. Since $\lambda$ is not a square, $a$ or $b$ is $\ge 2$. This proves (a).

*Step 5: (b).* Under the stated assumption, $a = b = 1$. That contradicts $ab \equiv \lambda \notin \mathbb Q^{\times 2}$. $\square$

**Remark 5.1** (refinement). Suppose $p_g(\tilde\Gamma) \ge 2$ and $f^*T(S)_{\mathbb Q} = g^*T(S')_{\mathbb Q}$ in $H^2(\tilde\Gamma)$. Then $(g^*)^{-1} f^*$ is a similarity of multiplier $\deg f/\deg g$. Since it is proportional to $\psi$, $\lambda \equiv \deg f \cdot \deg g \pmod{\mathbb Q^{\times 2}}$. Otherwise the two K3-type pieces are different summands of $T(\tilde\Gamma)$, and $\psi$ is realized by a Hodge correspondence on $\tilde\Gamma$ between distinct summands. This is the shape of the Kuga–Satake mechanism, where $\tilde\Gamma$ is replaced by the abelian variety $\mathrm{KS}(S)$.

**Remark 5.2** (status of the input to (b)). [KK, Thm 1.1] states that a dominant rational map of degree $\ge 2$ from a surface with trivial canonical class onto a K3 surface $S$ forces $\rho(S) > 1$. Version 5 (October 2025) corrects an earlier version. Its proof rests on [KK, Prop 2.5]: for a general $C \in |H_A|$ with $H_A = \phi^*H_S$, the image $\phi(C)$ is smooth. We cannot follow the final step of that proof. There $\tilde H = \phi^{-1}(\tilde D)$, where $\tilde D \in |H_S|$ is tangent to one branch of $\phi(C)$ at $p$, and it is claimed that $\phi(\tilde H \cap A')$ is singular at $p$. But $\phi(\tilde H \cap A') = \tilde D \cap S'$, and a general such $\tilde D$ is smooth at $p$. The argument also proves too much. It uses only that $\phi$ is a local isomorphism on $A'$, that $H_A = \phi^*H_S$ is very ample, and that $C$ is general. These hold for an isogeny $\phi\colon A \to B$ of abelian surfaces of degree $d \ge 2$. There a general $C$ is not invariant under any nonzero translation in $\ker\phi$, so $\phi|_C$ is birational and $\phi_*[C] = \phi_*\phi^*H_B = d H_B$. Hence $p_a(\phi(C)) - g(C) = d(d-1)H_B^2/2 > 0$, and $\phi(C)$ is singular. This is a counterexample to the mechanism of the proof under these three hypotheses, not to [KK, Prop 2.5] for K3 targets: a correct proof must use more than they provide. We do not claim that [KK, Thm 1.1] is false. We use it only as an explicitly stated hypothesis. Chen [Ch] proves the case of self-maps of a very general K3 surface by a different method. Part (a) does not depend on any of this.

**Remark 5.3** (hyperkähler fourfolds). Step 4 has an analogue in higher dimension, and there the square-class constraint disappears. Let $f\colon M \dashrightarrow M'$ be a dominant rational map of degree $d$ between projective hyperkähler manifolds of $K3^{[n]}$-type. Then $f^*$ multiplies the Beauville–Bogomolov form on $T(M')_{\mathbb Q}$ by $d^{1/n}$.

*Proof.* Resolve $f$ by $\tilde f\colon \tilde M \to M'$, where $\pi\colon \tilde M \to M$ is a composition of blow-ups along smooth centres, and put $f^* = \pi_*\tilde f^*$. Then $H^2(\tilde M) = \pi^*H^2(M) \oplus \bigoplus_i \mathbb Q[E_i]$. For $x \in T(M')$, the component of $\tilde f^*x$ along the $E_i$ is a morphism of Hodge structures from $\tilde f^*T(M') \cong T(M')$, which is irreducible and not of type $(1,1)$, to a structure of type $(1,1)$. So it is $0$, and $\tilde f^*x = \pi^*f^*x$. Hence $\int_M (f^*x)^{2n} = d\int_{M'} x^{2n}$. The Fujiki relation $\int y^{2n} = c_n\, q(y)^n$ [Fj], with the same $c_n$ for $M$ and $M'$, gives $(q\circ f^*)^n = d\, q^n$ as polynomials on $T(M')$. The polynomial ring is a domain, so $q \circ f^* = \zeta d^{1/n} q$ with $\zeta^n = 1$, and $\zeta$ is real. Since $f^*\sigma'$ is a nonzero multiple of $\sigma$, $q(f^*(\sigma' + \bar\sigma')) > 0$, so $\zeta = 1$. $\square$

For $n = 1$ this is Step 4. For $n = 2$ the multiplier is $\sqrt d$, so every positive rational $\lambda$ arises from degree $d = \lambda^2$. Maps of degree $\ge 2$ onto hyperkähler fourfolds of Picard number $1$ also exist. Voisin's rational self-map of the Fano variety of lines of a very general cubic fourfold has degree $16$ and acts by $-2$ on $H^{2,0}$ [Vo], [Am], so its multiplier on $T$ is $4 = \sqrt{16}$. On $NS$ the relation fails because of indeterminacy: the first dynamical degree is $7$ [Am]. Neither half of Theorem 4 therefore extends to dimension $4$.

This gives a route to ($\Psi_{\mathrm{ns}}$) that Theorem 4 does not block. Let $M$ and $M'$ be hyperkähler fourfolds of $K3^{[2]}$-type with rational Hodge isometries $\theta\colon T(S) \cong T(M)$ and $\theta'\colon T(S') \cong T(M')$, for example moduli spaces of stable sheaves on $S$ and $S'$ [OG]. These isometries are algebraic, and so are their inverses. The universal subscheme identifies $T(S)$ with $T(S^{[2]})$, with algebraic inverse because $\int_{S^{[2]}} x\,y\,c_2$ is a nonzero multiple of $q(x,y)$ [Fj]. Rational Hodge isometries between transcendental lattices of manifolds of $K3^{[n]}$-type are algebraic [Ma]. A dominant rational map $M \dashrightarrow M'$ of degree $d$ with $\sqrt d \in \lambda\,\mathbb Q^{\times 2}$ would therefore make $\theta^{-1} f^* \theta'$ an algebraic Hodge similarity $T(S') \to T(S)$ of multiplier $\sqrt d$. By the one-dimensionality of $\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathrm{Hdg}}$, this is a multiple of $\psi^{-1}$. We know no such map for Hecke partners.

Fibrewise isogenies of Lagrangian fibrations are the obvious candidates, and the simplest case gives only square multipliers. Let $S$ be a double plane branched along a smooth sextic $C_6$, and let $\pi\colon M = M_S(0,h,1) \to |h| = \mathbb P^{2\vee}$ be its Beauville–Mukai system. Over the complement $U$ of the dual curve $C_6^\vee$, the fibre over a line $\ell$ is a torsor under $A_\ell = \operatorname{Jac}(C_\ell)$, where $C_\ell$ is the genus-$2$ curve over $\ell$. A fibrewise isogeny over a dense open subset of $U$ is a quotient by a finite subgroup $K_\ell \subset A_\ell$ that is invariant under $\pi_1$ of that subset, and this $\pi_1$ surjects onto $\pi_1(U)$. We claim that $\pi_1(U)$ acts irreducibly on $A_\ell[p] = H_1(C_\ell, \mathbb F_p)$ for every prime $p$. The local monodromy at a general point of $C_6^\vee$, where $C_\ell$ has one node, is a transvection $x \mapsto x + (x\cdot\delta)\delta$. Since $C_6^\vee$ is irreducible, the vanishing cycles $\delta$ form one orbit up to sign. They span $H_1(C_\ell, \mathbb Z)$. Indeed, a general pencil of lines contains $30 = 6\cdot 5$ lines tangent to $C_6$, so the blow-up $\tilde S$ of $S$ at its two base points has $30$ nodal fibres. As a check, $\chi(\tilde S) = 26 = 2\chi(C_\ell) + 30$. This blow-up is a fibred neighbourhood of $C_\ell$ with one $2$-handle attached along each vanishing cycle, one more $2$-handle attached along a loop that bounds a section disc, and handles of index $3$ and $4$. So $H_1(C_\ell)/\langle\delta\rangle \cong H_1(\tilde S) = 0$. Now let $V \ne 0$ be an invariant subspace. If $x\cdot\delta \ne 0$ for some $x \in V$ and some $\delta$, then $\delta \in V$, so every vanishing cycle lies in $V$ and $V$ is everything. Otherwise $V$ is orthogonal to a spanning set, which is impossible. Hence $K_\ell \cap A_\ell[p]$ is $0$ or $A_\ell[p]$ for each $p$, and induction on the order gives $K_\ell = A_\ell[m]$. So every fibrewise isogeny of $M$ is, up to an isomorphism of the target, fibrewise multiplication by $m$. It has degree $m^4$, and if the target is of $K3^{[2]}$-type the formula above gives multiplier $m^2$. Invariant proper subgroups do exist after a finite base change of $U$, but the resulting map does not start from $M$. A nonsquare multiplier therefore needs a map from $M$ that is not a fibrewise isogeny of this fibration.

## 6. A transport route to ($\Psi_{\mathrm{ns}}$) and where it stops
This section is a programme. Nothing in it is used in Sections 2–5.

**6.1 Buskin's method extends formally to similarities.** Buskin's proof for rational isometries [Bu] runs as follows:
- (i) Fix $\varphi \in \mathrm O(\Lambda_{\mathbb Q})$ and form the twisted period domain $\Omega_\varphi$, the graph of $\tilde\varphi$ on $\Omega_\Lambda$.
- (ii) Cover the corresponding locus of marked pairs by twistor lines, and show it is connected.
- (iii) Find a seed: a pair with a stable bundle $E$ on $S_0 \times S_0'$ whose $\kappa_2(E)$ induces $\varphi$.
- (iv) Deform $\mathrm{End}\,E$ along twistor lines using Verbitsky's theorem on hyperholomorphic bundles [Bu, Thm 5.2], with $c_2(\mathrm{End}\,E) = -2r\,\kappa_2(E)$.

Step (i) needs only $\varphi \in \mathrm{GO}(\Lambda_{\mathbb Q})$, and similarities of every positive multiplier exist there, because $\Lambda_{\mathbb Q}(\lambda) \cong \Lambda_{\mathbb Q}$ by the proof of Theorem 2(a). Step (ii) also goes through.

**Lemma 6.1.** Let $\varphi \in \mathrm{GO}(\Lambda_{\mathbb Q})$ be signed with multiplier $\lambda > 0$, and define $\mathcal M_\varphi$ as in [Bu, Def 4.11], with "Hodge isometry" replaced by "Hodge similarity of multiplier $\lambda$". Then $\mathcal M_\varphi$ has two connected components, $\mathcal M_\varphi \cap \mathcal M^{++}$ and $\mathcal M_\varphi \cap \mathcal M^{--}$. Any two points of one component are joined by a twistor path whose lines are associated to hyperkähler structures on products $S \times S'$.

*Proof.* The proof of [Bu, Prop 4.17] uses the isometry property only in three places. (1) [Bu, Rem 4.8]: if $\operatorname{Pic}(S) = 0$, a twistor line through $(\sigma, \varphi\sigma)$ with Kähler class $h$ lifts to the hyperkähler structure on $S \times S'$ given by the product of the Ricci-flat metrics with Kähler classes $h$ and $\psi(h)$. For a similarity, $\psi$ maps the triple $(h, \operatorname{Re}\sigma, \operatorname{Im}\sigma)$ to an orthogonal triple whose squares are all multiplied by $\lambda$. This is a hyperkähler triple on $S'$ for the metric scaled by $\lambda$, and the complex structures $aI + bJ + cK$ on the two factors have periods related by $\psi$. (2) [Bu, Lem 4.15]: $\psi$ maps a positively oriented basis of a positive $3$-plane to a positively oriented basis, because it is conformal with $\lambda > 0$ and signed. The continuity argument for Kähler-ness is unchanged. (3) [Bu, Lem 4.16] and the Torelli step: a rational Hodge similarity maps $NS_{\mathbb Q}$ onto $NS_{\mathbb Q}$, so $\operatorname{Pic}(S) = 0$ if and only if $\operatorname{Pic}(S') = 0$, and points with trivial Picard group are again the only points in their period fibres. Generic twistor paths in $\Omega_\Lambda$ [Bu, after Def 4.14] map under $\tilde\varphi$ to generic twistor paths in $\Omega_\varphi$, because $\tilde\varphi$ maps $\mathbb P(W_{\mathbb C}) \cap \Omega_\Lambda$ to $\mathbb P(\varphi W_{\mathbb C}) \cap \Omega_\Lambda$ and $\varphi W$ is positive. The rest of the proof is formal. $\square$

Along each such line, $\psi \in H^2(S)^\vee \otimes H^2(S')$ intertwines the two $\mathfrak{su}(2)$-actions, because $A \mapsto \psi A \psi^{-1}$ maps $\mathfrak{so}(W)$ to $\mathfrak{so}(\psi W)$. So $\psi$ is $\mathrm{SU}(2)$-invariant, as in the isometric case. The new difficulty is step (iii).

One seed for each prime $p$ and each of the two components would suffice. By Corollary 3.2 only prime multipliers matter. Fix $\varphi_0 \in \mathrm{GO}(\Lambda_{\mathbb Q})$ of multiplier $p$ for which a seed exists. Let $(S, S')$ be a target, and let $\varphi$ be its full similarity $\Phi$ of multiplier $p$ (Section 6.2) read through markings. Then $\gamma = \varphi_0^{-1}\varphi \in \mathrm O(\Lambda_{\mathbb Q})$. By surjectivity of the period map there is a K3 surface $S''$ whose marked period is $\gamma$ applied to that of $S$. It is projective, because $\gamma(h)$ is a rational $(1,1)$-class of positive square. Now $\gamma\colon H^2(S) \to H^2(S'')$ is a rational Hodge isometry, algebraic by [Bu], and $(S'', S')$ lies in $\Omega_{\varphi_0}$. Since $\varphi_0$ is chosen at the seed, its $NS$-part may be any rational similarity of multiplier $p$. For $p = 2$ and $p = 3$ there are natural places to look. If an automorphism $g$ of $S_0$ acts on $H^{2,0}$ by $\pm i$, then $g^2 = -1$ on $T(S_0)_{\mathbb Q}$, because $\ker(g^2 + 1)$ is a rational Hodge substructure containing $\sigma$. So the correspondence $\Delta + \Gamma_g$ acts on $T$ by $1 + g$, with $(1+g)^t(1+g) = 2 + g + g^{-1} = 2$. If $g$ acts on $H^{2,0}$ by a primitive cube root of unity, then $g^2 + g + 1 = 0$ on $T$ for the same reason, and $\Delta - \Gamma_g$ gives $2 - g - g^{-1} = 3$. On $NS$ these classes must be corrected, because $g$ fixes an ample class.

**6.2 The seed condition.** Let the target be a pair $(S, S')$ with $\rho(S) = 1$ and endomorphism field $\mathbb Q$, and let $\Phi$ be the similarity of $H^2$ given by $\varphi$, so that $\Phi = \psi$ on $T(S)$ and $\Phi(h) \in \mathbb Q h'$. Let $\Theta = \kappa_2(E)_{(2,2)}$ be the Künneth part in $H^2 \otimes H^2$ of the transported class. It is a fixed class on the underlying manifold, viewed as a map $H^2(S) \to H^2(S')$. At the target it is Hodge, so $\Theta = c\,\psi + a\, h \otimes h'$, and $c \neq 0$ if the transport is to produce $\psi$. A generic twistor path passes through points $p$ with $\operatorname{Pic}(S_p) = 0$ [Bu, Def 4.14, Lem 4.16], and $\Theta$ is Hodge there too. Write $\Theta = \Phi \circ B$. Then $B$ is the scalar $c$ on $h^\perp$ and a scalar $c'$ on $h$, and $B\sigma_p \in \mathbb C\sigma_p$. If $c' \neq c$, then $\sigma_p \in h^\perp_{\mathbb C}$, so $h \in NS(S_p) = 0$, which is absurd. Hence
$$\kappa_2(E)_{(2,2)} = c\, \Psi_{\mathrm{full}}, \qquad c \neq 0,$$
where $\Psi_{\mathrm{full}}\colon H^2(S_0) \to H^2(S_0')$ is $\Phi$ transported to the seed, a similarity on all of $H^2$. A similarity on $T$ corrected by an independent block on $NS$ is not enough.

Polystable seeds reduce to stable ones. The Hermite–Einstein connection of $E = \bigoplus E_i$ is the direct sum of those of the summands. So if $\operatorname{End}E$ is hyperholomorphic, so is every block $\operatorname{Hom}(E_i, E_j)$. Put $\delta_i = c_1(E_i)/r_i - c_1(E)/r$. Then $c_1(\operatorname{Hom}(E_i, E_j)) = r_i r_j(\delta_j - \delta_i)$ is $\mathrm{SU}(2)$-invariant for every line of the path. So it is a rational class of type $(1,1)$ at every point of the path, and it vanishes at a point with $\operatorname{Pic} = 0$. Since $\sum r_i \delta_i = 0$, every $\delta_i$ is $0$. Then $\kappa(E) = \sum_i \kappa(E_i)\, e^{\delta_i} = \sum_i \kappa(E_i)$, and the argument above gives $\kappa_2(E_i)_{(2,2)} = c_i \Psi_{\mathrm{full}}$ with $\sum c_i = c \neq 0$. So some stable summand is already a seed. In particular, adding line bundles of equal slope to a partial similarity does not help.

**6.3 The natural seeds are graphs, and they fail.** By Theorem 4(a), a graph seed between Picard-rank-one surfaces needs a rational map of degree $\ge 2$, which [KK, Thm 1.1] would exclude (Remark 5.2). So graph seeds must sit at special points with $\rho \ge 2$. We note the mechanism of failure in two cases.
- *Nikulin quotients* ($p = 2$). For a symplectic involution $\iota$ on $X$ with $Y$ the resolution of $X/\iota$, the correspondence $\Gamma_*$ has multiplier $2$ on $E_8(-2)^\perp$ and kills $E_8(-2)$. At a Nikulin pair, $\Psi_{\mathrm{full}}$ is still *algebraic*: by Witt cancellation $NS(X)_{\mathbb Q}(2) \cong NS(Y)_{\mathbb Q}$, and generically $\Psi_{\mathrm{full}} = \pm\Gamma_* + N$ with $N \in NS(X) \otimes NS(Y)$. But the graph is trianalytic only for $\iota$-invariant $\omega$, with the orbifold metric on $X/\iota$. Twistor lines through such points stay inside the $12$-dimensional Nikulin locus.
- *Kummer isogenies* ($p$ odd). An isogeny $A \to B$ of degree $p$ induces $\mathrm{Km}A \dashrightarrow \mathrm{Km}B$. Near a point $x \notin A[2]$ with $f(x) \in B[2]$, the blown-up map is a double cover branched along the zero section of $\mathcal O(-2)$. This gives $q^* E_b = E_a + \sum 2C_x$, so $\Gamma^* E_b = E_a$, with multiplier $1$ on the Kummer lattice. The full similarity on the Kummer block exists, as $4 \times 4$ quaternion blocks that mix nodes, and it is algebraic. It is realized only at the flat orbifold metric, where $\omega \cdot E_i = 0$.

In both cases the graph is a similarity on part of $H^2$ only. The defect sits on the nodal classes, and these disappear under generic deformation. The determinant problem (D) meets the same nodes: under a Kummer degeneration $\det T$ specializes to $\pm\operatorname{Alt}(E_1 \boxtimes \cdots \boxtimes E_{16} \boxtimes \det T(\mathrm{Km}))$. We therefore ask:

**Question 6.2.** For each prime $p$, is there a stable bundle on some $S_0 \times S_0'$ with $\kappa_2(E)_{(2,2)}$ a nonzero multiple of a full similarity of multiplier $p$? For $p = 2, 3$, is there one whose class is an $NS$-correction of $\Delta \pm \Gamma_g$ as in Section 6.1? Is there a desingularization theorem for hyperholomorphic bundles across the Kummer degeneration that preserves a prescribed algebraic class on the nodal block?

**Remark 6.3** (both problems are variational). Let $Z$ be a component of the moduli of triples $(S, S', \psi)$ in Corollary 3.2, and let $\tilde Z \to Z$ be a finite cover on which the monodromy of $\Lambda_{2d}$ lies in $\mathrm{SO}$. Over $\tilde Z$, both $\psi_b \in \Lambda_{2d}^\vee \otimes \Lambda_{2dp}$ and $\det \Lambda_{2d} \in H^{42}(S_b^{21})$ are flat, and they are Hodge on every fibre. Both are algebraic at every point $b$ with $\rho(S_b) = 20$.
- Write $\Lambda_{2d,\mathbb Q} = T_b \oplus N_b$ with $N_b$ algebraic, and similarly $\Lambda_{2dp,\mathbb Q} = T'_b \oplus N'_b$. Since $\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathrm{Hdg}}(T_b, N'_b) = 0$, we have $\psi_b = \psi_b|_{T_b} + \psi_b|_{N_b}$. The second term is a sum of products of divisors.
- By the Shioda–Inose structure [Mo, §6] (degree-$2$ maps $S \dashrightarrow \mathrm{Km}(A)$ inducing $T(S)(2) \cong T(\mathrm{Km}A) = T(A)(2)$), there are algebraic Hodge isomorphisms $T_b \cong T(A)_{\mathbb Q}$ and $T'_b \cong T(A')_{\mathbb Q}$ with $A$, $A'$ products of two elliptic curves. So $\psi_b|_{T_b}$ is algebraic once every Hodge class on $A \times A'$ is, and that holds by [RM, Prop 2.18].
- $\det \Lambda_{2d} = \pm \det T_b \wedge \det N_b$ is an antisymmetrization of products of $\det T_b \in H^4(S_b^2)$ with divisor classes. $\det T_b$ is algebraic because HC holds for all powers of a K3 surface with CM field $E$ [RM, Thm 3.3].

Points with $\rho = 20$ are dense in $\tilde Z$. So (D) and ($\Psi_{\mathrm{ns}}$) each ask that one flat Hodge class, algebraic on a dense countable set of fibres, be algebraic on every fibre. This is an instance of the variational Hodge conjecture. Density alone does not settle it, because algebraicity on countably many proper closed subsets says nothing about the very general fibre. It also explains why Theorem 3 needs the full monodromy: on $\tilde Z$ the class $\det \Lambda_{2d}$ is flat, and the barrier is only that no cycle on the whole family $Z$ can produce it.

## 8. Bearing on the Hodge conjecture
No new case of HC is proved. Theorem 1 says HC for all products of K3 surfaces with trivial endomorphism field and $\operatorname{rk} T$ odd or $\ge 10$ is equivalent to two statements:
- (D) the determinant is algebraic;
- ($\Psi_{\mathrm{ns}}$) nonsquare Hodge similarities are algebraic.

Both follow from the Kuga–Satake Hodge conjecture for $\rho = 1$, and the two problems behave differently.
- (D) is invisible to every construction over moduli (Theorem 3).
- ($\Psi_{\mathrm{ns}}$) is visible over moduli, but by Theorem 4 it is supplied by a surface with $p_g = 1$ only through a dominant rational map of degree $\ge 2$ between Picard-rank-one K3 surfaces (which [KK, Thm 1.1] excludes, if its proof holds; Remark 5.2), and by Remark 3.1 it is not supplied by (twisted) Fourier–Mukai theory. Remark 5.3 leaves a route open: a rational map of degree $\lambda^2$ between hyperkähler fourfolds birational to moduli spaces of sheaves on $S$ and $S'$. Fibrewise isogenies of Beauville–Mukai systems give only square multipliers, so such a map must come from elsewhere.

Both problems already arise for every Picard-rank-one K3 surface (Theorem 2). We do not know whether either implies the other.
