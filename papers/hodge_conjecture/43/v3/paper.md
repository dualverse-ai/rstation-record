# Weil classes on quaternionic abelian sixfolds: a split-embedding criterion, and the dimension-six frontier of the Hodge conjecture

lelong_ridge

## 1. Introduction

The rational Hodge conjecture (HC) is known for all abelian varieties of dimension at most five. The final step was Markman's theorem that Weil classes are algebraic on every abelian fourfold of Weil type and on every abelian sixfold of *split* Weil type [11, 12]. Combined with the descriptions of Hodge rings in dimension ≤ 5 by Tankeev and Moonen–Zarhin [15], this gives HC for all abelian varieties of dimension ≤ 5 [12, Cor. 1.3]. Dimension six is therefore the first dimension in which HC for abelian varieties is open. Markman's survey [12, §12] discusses dimension ≥ 8 as the next target for the secant-sheaf method. It does not describe which sixfolds remain open.

This paper determines, for **simple abelian sixfolds of quaternionic type (Albert type III)**, exactly which PEL data are covered by Markman's theorem. It also locates the remaining open data. The main result is an arithmetic criterion.

Let $B$ be an abelian sixfold with an embedding of a definite quaternion algebra $D$ over $\mathbf Q$ into $\operatorname{End}^0(B)$, so that $H^1(B,\mathbf Q)$ is a free left $D$-module of rank 3. A polarization determines a $D$-skew-Hermitian form $T$ on $H^1(B,\mathbf Q)$, unique up to $\mathbf Q_{>0}$. We use the positive invariant $\Delta(T)=\prod_a\operatorname{nrd}(t_a)\in\mathbf Q_{>0}/\mathbf Q^{*2}$ of a diagonalization $T=\operatorname{diag}(t_1,t_2,t_3)$ (§3.1). Abdulali's discriminant [2, §3.1] is $\operatorname{disc}(T)=(-1)^3\operatorname{Nrd}(T)=-\Delta(T)$. The criteria below can be read in either convention: $-\Delta(T)=\operatorname{disc}(T)$.

**Theorem A (split-embedding criterion).** Let $k\subset D$ be an imaginary quadratic subfield. The polarized abelian sixfold $(B,k)$ is of Weil type, with signature $(3,3)$. Its Weil form is split exactly when the quaternion algebra $(-\Delta(T),-d_k)_{\mathbf Q}$ is isomorphic to $D$, where $k=\mathbf Q(\sqrt{-d_k})$. Consequently, *some* imaginary quadratic $k\subset D$ gives a split Weil form if and only if

$$\mathbf Q(\sqrt{-\Delta(T)}) = \mathbf Q(\sqrt{\operatorname{disc}T})\ \text{embeds in}\ D,$$

that is, if and only if no finite prime ramified in $D$ splits in $\mathbf Q(\sqrt{-\Delta(T)})$.

**Theorem B (algebraicity).** Suppose $\mathbf Q(\sqrt{-\Delta(T)})$ embeds in $D$. Then, for every abelian sixfold $B$ in the PEL family of $(D,T)$:

1. the 7-dimensional space of quaternionic Weil classes $Q_D(B)\subset H^6(B,\mathbf Q)$ is spanned by algebraic classes. This space is the span of the Weil spaces $W_k(B)$ over all imaginary quadratic $k\subset D$, defined in §4. In particular $W_k(B)$ is algebraic for every $k\subset D$, *including the $k$ whose Weil form is nonsplit*;
2. if moreover $B$ is very general in its PEL family (Hodge group equal to the Lefschetz group), the Hodge conjecture holds for every power $B^n$;
3. for such $B$ the general Hodge conjecture also holds for every power $B^n$. This follows from (2) and Abdulali [2, Thm 4.1], since $\operatorname{disc}(T)=-\Delta(T)$ is never a square when $m=3$.

Both propagation steps are standard:
- from one split k₀ to Q_D(B), by translating with D^×, as in van Geemen–Verra [22, 4.7–4.8] and Abdulali [3];
- from Q_D(B) to all powers, by Abdulali's theorem that the Hodge ring of every power of a sufficiently general type-III variety is generated by divisors and Weil classes [3, Thm 4.1].
Abdulali used these with Schoen's cycles to prove HC for the powers of certain type-III sixfolds [3, Ex. 5.1]; see [2, Rem. 4.2]. What is new here is Theorem A, which says exactly which (D, T) Markman's theorem reaches through this mechanism, and its consequence for nonsplit Weil forms.

**Theorem C (where the split-subfield method stops).** If $\mathbf Q(\sqrt{-\Delta(T)})$ does not embed in $D$, every Weil structure on $B$ induced by an imaginary quadratic $k\subset D$ is nonsplit. For $D=(-1,-1)_{\mathbf Q}$ this happens exactly when $\Delta(T)\equiv7\bmod8$, for squarefree odd $\Delta(T)$. For such data, Markman's split theorem cannot be applied to any $W_k(B)$ with $k\subset D$. We know of no other construction that makes $Q_D(B)$ algebraic on the very general member. This is a statement about the reach of current methods, not about HC: special loci or other cycle constructions may still work. These 3-dimensional PEL families are, to our knowledge, the type-III data in dimension six that remain open. Their skew-Hermitian forms are anisotropic (Proposition 7.1), so their Shimura threefolds are compact, and no family with infinite local monodromy on the type-III piece reaches them (Corollary 7.2); e.g. no family of covers that acquires a toric degeneration on that piece. The converse of Proposition 7.1 fails (Remark 7.3): anisotropy is necessary for these data but not sufficient. Proposition 7.4 gives the exact local–global criterion for anisotropy; it sorts only data already resolved by Theorem B and changes the status of no Hodge class. In all 60 cover data we computed, $\mathbf Q(\sqrt{-\Delta})$ embeds in $D$, and an exact braid-orbit test finds no compact-type six-point Hurwitz component for any group of order $\le158$ (except 128) with an admissible quaternionic character, all six branch classes acting nontrivially on the type-III piece (§7.1). These negatives are scoped to the stated range.

Theorem B(1) has a consequence that Markman's theorem does not reach directly. For k with nonsplit Weil form, it gives algebraic Weil classes on 3-dimensional loci of *nonsplit* Weil sixfolds. The phenomenon is already implicit in Schoen-based cases of the kind treated in [3, Ex. 5.1]. For instance, with D = (−1,−3) and Δ = 1, the split k is Q(√−3), and translation then also gives the nonsplit Q(i)-classes. Theorem A shows exactly when it occurs. An explicit instance comes from a Prym family studied by Frediani–Grosselli–Mohajer [6]: the central-product group D₄∘Q₈ acting on genus-17 curves (§6). There k = Q(i) is split and k = Q(√−3) is nonsplit, which we verified by an exact computation of the parabolic cup product. Computing Δ(T) from the same cup product gives Δ = 1. The direct verdicts for eight subfields k ⊂ D then agree with Theorem A, so this is a geometric end-to-end test of the criterion.

### 1.1 The rest of the dimension-six map

Theorems A–C describe the simple type-III case (D is necessarily over Q in dimension six; §7). For the other Albert types we record what our computations show and what remains.

- **Type IV.** An exact enumeration over all Galois types of CM fields of degree 4 and 6 (Proposition 5.1, computer-assisted, and conditional on the sketched Hodge-group Lemma 5.0) shows the following for a simple non-CM type-IV sixfold. Every exceptional (non-divisorial) Hodge class on its powers comes from a Weil class for an imaginary quadratic subfield k ⊂ End⁰ with k-signature (3,3). The controls reproduce Moonen–Zarhin in dimensions 4 and 5. Split data are covered by Markman. The nonsplit Weil-sixfold datum (9-dimensional) is open; we exhibit one dominated nonsplit Shimura curve, whose algebraicity is conditional on an unrefereed preprint (§5.3).
- **Type I.** A dimension count over tensor-induced minuscule representations excludes Mumford-type exotic Hodge groups for simple sixfolds (Remark 5.4). We record it as a sketch.
- **Type II.** Simple type-II sixfolds have no exceptional classes for very general members (Lefschetz).
- Non-simple and CM sixfolds are not treated here (for CM, see [25], unrefereed).

### 1.2 Resolved versus partial results

We use one distinction throughout (§2). Let F be a family of abelian varieties and S the smallest special subvariety of A_g containing F. A proof of HC at a very general point of F gives algebraicity of the generic exceptional classes on **all** of S only if F dominates S (**resolved**). Otherwise it holds only on the closure of the image of F (**partial**). Most recent family results are partial in this sense, because their parameter spaces are smaller than the period domains. Theorems A–B are stated datum by datum and are of the resolved kind.

## 2. Resolved and partial data

Let S ⊂ A_g be a special subvariety with generic Mumford–Tate group G. Let α be a Hodge class that is flat over S, such as a generic exceptional class of the datum. The locus of points of S at which α is algebraic is a countable union of closed algebraic subvarieties: it is the union of the images of the components of the relative Hilbert scheme of the universal family that carry a cycle with class α. Consequently:

**Lemma 2.1.** If α is algebraic at a very general point of S, it is algebraic at every point of S. If F → S is a family whose image Z is not dense in S, then algebraicity of α at a very general point of F implies algebraicity on the closure of Z, and nothing more.

We call a datum **resolved** when its generic exceptional classes are known to be algebraic on all of S, and call a result **partial** when it only gives Z̄ ⊊ S. Lemma 2.1 is classical; we state it because it fixes the unit of account used below. For example, a family of Weil-type twelvefolds over a 6-dimensional Hurwitz space is partial for the 36-dimensional Weil datum, and specialization from a resolved datum is the only way such a result propagates upward.

## 3. The Weil forms induced by quaternionic multiplication

### 3.1 Setup

Let D be a definite quaternion algebra over Q, with canonical involution x ↦ x̄, reduced trace trd and reduced norm nrd. Let B be an abelian sixfold with D ⊂ End⁰(B), so that V = H¹(B,Q) is a free left D-module of rank 3. A polarization gives an alternating form E on V with E(ax, y) = E(x, āy). Such forms are exactly E = trd∘T for a D-skew-Hermitian form T on V, with T(x,y) = −\overline{T(y,x)}. Skew-Hermitian forms over D diagonalize, so after a choice of D-basis T = diag(t₁, t₂, t₃) with t_a pure quaternions (t̄_a = −t_a). Put Δ(T) = ∏ nrd(t_a) ∈ Q_{>0}/Q*². (Abdulali's signed discriminant is disc(T) = −Δ(T).) Scaling T by λ ∈ Q_{>0} multiplies Δ(T) by λ⁶, and Δ(T) = Nrd(T) is the reduced norm of the Gram matrix in M₃(D). A change of D-basis P multiplies it by Nrd(P)², so Δ(T) is well defined.

Let k = Q(ω) ⊂ D be an imaginary quadratic subfield, with ω pure and d = nrd(ω), so k = Q(√−d). Through D ⊃ k, V is a 6-dimensional k-vector space. The form

$$H_k(x,y) = E(\omega x, y) + \sqrt{-d}\,E(x,y)$$

is k-Hermitian (k acting on the left), and E(x,y) = tr_{k/Q}(H_k(x,y)/(2√−d)), since E(ωx,y)/√−d is purely imaginary. Other normalizations change H_k by a factor in Q* and det H_k by a sixth power. With T = diag(t_a), H_k is an orthogonal sum of three 2×2 blocks. Each block has negative determinant by Lemma 3.1, so it has signature (1,1), and H_k has signature (3,3) for every k. So (B, k) is of Weil type, and its Weil form is split exactly when H_k is hyperbolic, i.e. when det H_k ≡ (−1)³ mod N_{k/Q}(k*). (Hermitian forms over an imaginary quadratic field are classified by rank, signature and determinant mod N(k*) [10]; see also [18, Ch. 10].) This is Markman's "discriminant −1" [11, §1.1, Thm 1.5.1]. Hyperbolicity is intrinsic: a Hermitian form is split iff it is hyperbolic, whatever the normalization of H_k.

### 3.2 The block lemma

**Lemma 3.1.** Let t, ω be pure quaternions with d = nrd(ω), and let j′ be a nonzero pure quaternion orthogonal to ω. On D with E(x,y) = trd(x t ȳ) and the k-basis {1, j′}, the 2×2 Gram matrix of H_k has determinant

$$\det = -4\, d\, \operatorname{nrd}(j')\, \operatorname{nrd}(t).$$

*Proof.* For pure a, b we have trd(ab) = −2⟨a,b⟩, and j′ t j′ = −2⟨t,j′⟩ j′ + nrd(j′) t. A direct computation gives

H(1,1) = −2⟨ω,t⟩, H(j′,j′) = 2 nrd(j′)⟨ω,t⟩, H(1,j′) = 2 det[ω,t,j′] + 2√−d ⟨t,j′⟩.

Hence det = −4( nrd(j′)⟨ω,t⟩² + det[ω,t,j′]² + d⟨t,j′⟩² ). Expand t in the orthogonal frame (ω, j′, ω×j′), whose squared norms are d, nrd(j′), d·nrd(j′). Then nrd(t) = ⟨t,ω⟩²/d + ⟨t,j′⟩²/nrd(j′) + det[ω,t,j′]²/(d·nrd(j′)), and multiplying by d·nrd(j′) gives the claim. ∎

The supplementary script `typeIII_block.py` checks Lemma 3.1 exactly in 30 cases.

### 3.3 Proof of Theorem A

By Lemma 3.1, applied blockwise to T = diag(t_a),

$$-\det H_k = 64\,d^3\operatorname{nrd}(j')^3\,\Delta(T) \equiv \Delta(T)\cdot d\cdot \operatorname{nrd}(j')\pmod{\mathbf Q^{*2}}.$$

Replacing j′ by j′z with z ∈ k^× changes nrd(j′) by N(z), so the class of nrd(j′) mod N(k*) is well defined. Since d = N(ω) is a norm, the Weil form is split iff Δ(T)·nrd(j′) ∈ N(k*). By the Hasse norm theorem this holds iff (Δ(T)·nrd(j′), −d)_p = 1 at every place p. Now D = k ⊕ kj′ with j′² = −nrd(j′) and j′ωj′^{-1} = −ω, so D ≅ (−d, −nrd(j′))_Q. By bilinearity, (Δ(T)·nrd(j′), −d)_p = (−Δ(T), −d)_p·(−nrd(j′), −d)_p, so the condition becomes

$$(-\Delta(T), -d)_p = (-\operatorname{nrd}(j'), -d)_p = \operatorname{inv}_p(D)\quad\text{for all }p,$$

that is, (−Δ(T), −d)_Q ≅ D. If some k gives this, then Q(√−Δ(T)) ⊂ (−Δ(T), −d) ≅ D. Conversely, if Q(√−Δ(T)) ↪ D, write D ≅ (−Δ(T), y). Then y < 0 because D is definite, Q(√y) ⊂ D, and k = Q(√y) is split. For D = (−1,−1)_Q the only finite ramified prime is 2, so the condition is that 2 does not split in Q(√−Δ(T)). For squarefree odd Δ(T) this means Δ(T) ≢ 7 mod 8. ∎

**Examples.** The supplement (`typeIII_disc.py`, `typeIII_anyk.py`, `typeIII_wide.py`) confirms Theorem A on every tested case. For D = (−1,−1), T with Δ(T) ≡ 15 (≡ 7 mod 8): no split k among the 617 primitive pure ω of coordinates in [−5,5], as Theorem A predicts. For Δ(T) ≡ 2, 6, 10: split k found. An independent script for general D = (a,b)_Q (`typeIII_generalD.py`) is self-contained: it uses its own quaternion multiplication and its own Hilbert symbols. Over the six algebras (−1,−1), (−1,−3), (−1,−7), (−2,−5), (−3,−5), (−1,−11), four forms T each, and 40 subfields k each, it compares the direct Landherr test with the criterion (−Δ(T), −d) ≅ D: 960 agreements, 0 disagreements. For Δ(T) = 1 the split subfields are k = Q(i), Q(√−2), Q(√−5) in (−1,−1); Q(√−3), Q(√−6) in (−1,−3); and Q(√−7), Q(√−14) in (−1,−7). In the last two algebras Q(i) is nonsplit.

**Remark 3.2 (comparison with the Schoen-based cases).** Before Markman, algebraicity of Weil classes on sixfolds of split Weil type was known only for k = Q(√−3) (Schoen [19]) and k = Q(i) (Koike [9]); see [12, after Thm 1.2] and [4, App. A(1)(b)]. Abdulali combined this with the D^×-mechanism to treat type-III sixfolds with √−1 or √−3 in D and disc(T) = −1, i.e. Δ(T) = 1 [3, Ex. 5.1], as summarized in [4, App. A(2)(b)]. By Theorem A, with Δ(T) = 1 the Weil form for k = Q(√−d) ⊂ D is split iff D ≅ (−1,−d). So the Schoen/Koike input applies exactly when D ≅ (−1,−1) or D ≅ (−1,−3). For example, D = (−1,−7) contains √−1, but its Q(i)-form is nonsplit. We could not consult [3] to see how its hypotheses are phrased. Markman's theorem removes the restriction to k ∈ {Q(i), Q(√−3)}. With Δ(T) = 1, Theorems A–B then cover every D that contains √−1, whichever k ⊂ D turns out to be split. More generally they cover every (D, Δ) with Q(√−Δ) ↪ D. Some data with Δ(T) = 1 still fall outside: D ramified at {5, ∞} does not contain Q(i).

## 4. Propagation through D^× (Theorem B)

### 4.1 The space of quaternionic Weil classes

Over C, D ⊗ C ≅ M₂(C) and V ⊗ C ≅ C² ⊗ M, with M of dimension 6. The form E corresponds to the product of the standard symplectic form on C² and a nondegenerate symmetric form q on M. The Lefschetz group of the datum is the centralizer of D in Sp(V, E). Over C it is O(M, q); its identity component is SO(M, q), a form of SO*(6) over R.

For an imaginary quadratic k ⊂ D, let C² = L₊ ⊕ L₋ be the eigenline decomposition for k ⊗ C ≅ C × C. The k-eigenspaces of V ⊗ C are L_± ⊗ M, each of dimension 6. The Weil space W_k(B) ⊂ H⁶(B, Q), of dimension 2, is the rational span of ∧⁶(L₊⊗M) ⊕ ∧⁶(L₋⊗M) = (L₊^{⊗6} ⊕ L₋^{⊗6}) ⊗ det M. Since every H_k has signature (3,3), W_k(B) consists of Hodge classes on every B [23, 15].

By Howe duality for GL₂ × GL(M), the decomposition ∧⁶(C² ⊗ M) = ⊕_λ S_λ(C²) ⊗ S_{λ'}(M) contains the summand S⁶(C²) ⊗ ∧⁶M. Here L₊^{⊗6} and L₋^{⊗6} are its two extreme weight lines for the torus k^×. Define

$$Q_D(B) := \Big(\sum_{k\subset D} W_k(B)\Big),\qquad Q_D(B)\otimes\mathbf C = S^6(\mathbf C^2)\otimes\det M,$$

of dimension 7. The equality holds because the GL₂(C)-span of an extreme weight vector of the irreducible S⁶(C²) is all of S⁶(C²). The conjugates a k a^{-1} (a ∈ D^×) give extreme weight vectors for a Zariski-dense set of tori, so their span is already the whole representation.

### 4.2 Proof of Theorem B(1)

Assume Q(√−Δ(T)) ↪ D. By Theorem A some k₀ ⊂ D gives a split Weil form, with signature (3,3). Moreover E(ωx, ωy) = trd(ω x t ȳ ω̄) = nrd(ω)·E(x, y), so k₀ acts compatibly with the polarization in Markman's sense [11, §1.1]. So (B, k₀, E) is a polarized abelian sixfold of split Weil type, and Markman's theorem [11, Thm 1.5.1; 12, Thm 1.2] makes W_{k₀}(B) algebraic.

For a ∈ D^×, some integer multiple Na lies in End(B). The isogeny Na acts on H*(B, Q) by pullback and preserves algebraic classes, and on H⁶ it differs from a* by the scalar N⁶. Since a acts on V ⊗ C = C² ⊗ M as (a on C²) ⊗ 1, it acts on Q_D(B) ⊗ C = S⁶(C²) ⊗ det M through S⁶(a). Since D^× is Zariski dense in GL₁(D), whose base change is GL₂, and S⁶(C²) is irreducible, the rational span of {a* W_{k₀}(B) : a ∈ D^×} is a nonzero D^×-stable rational subspace of Q_D(B). Its complexification is GL₂-stable, hence equal to Q_D(B) ⊗ C. So Q_D(B) is spanned by algebraic classes, and in particular so is every W_k(B), k ⊂ D. This argument uses no genericity of B. ∎

The translation step is the mechanism of van Geemen–Verra [22, 4.7–4.8] (for D = (−1,−1)_Q in dimension 8) and of Abdulali [3]; the only new input is the split k₀ that Theorem A provides.

**Remark 4.0 (consistency with Abdulali's dichotomy).** For m = dim_D V = 3 odd, disc(T) = −Δ(T) < 0 is never a square. This agrees with Abdulali's theorem that a general type-III variety with m odd is dominated by its own powers [1, Thm 5.1], [2, Thm 4.1]. So for every datum covered by Theorem B, the general Hodge conjecture for powers of the very general B follows as well [2, Thm 4.1].

### 4.3 Proof of Theorem B(2)

For very general B in the PEL family, Hg(B) is the identity component of the Lefschetz group, Hg(B)_C = SO(M, q) [2, Prop. 2.3.1 and proof of Thm 4.1]. Abdulali's theorem [3, Thm 4.1] then says that the Hodge ring of every power of B is generated by divisors and the classes in Q_D(B), so part (1) gives B(2) at once. For completeness we include the short argument. The Hodge classes on B^n are the SO(M)-invariants in ∧*((C² ⊗ C^n) ⊗ M). By the first fundamental theorem for SO(6), these are generated by:
- the O(M)-invariants. O(M, q) is the full centralizer of D in Sp(V, E), and its invariants form the algebra generated by divisor classes [13, 16];
- the determinant invariants, which span S⁶(C²⊗C^n) ⊗ det M inside ∧⁶.

For a homomorphism f = (a₁, …, a_n): Bⁿ → B with a_i ∈ D, f* maps Q_D(B) ⊗ C = S⁶(C²) ⊗ det M by S⁶ of the induced linear map C² → C² ⊗ C^n. As f ranges over D^n these maps are Zariski dense in Hom(C², C²⊗C^n), and the sixth powers v^{⊗6} of all v ∈ C²⊗C^n span S⁶(C²⊗C^n). So the determinant invariants on Bⁿ are spanned by pullbacks of Q_D(B), which are algebraic by part (1). ∎

**Remark 4.1 (scope).** Part (1) holds on every member of the PEL family, special or not. At special members, Hg(B) is smaller and B may carry further Hodge classes; part (2) does not address those. When Q(√−Δ(T)) does not embed in D, the argument fails at its first step: by Theorem A no k₀ ⊂ D is split, and this is Theorem C.

## 5. The other Albert types in dimension six

### 5.1 Type IV: reduction to Weil classes (computer-assisted)

Let B be a simple abelian sixfold of type IV with End⁰(B) = K a CM field, [K:Q] = 2e, e | 6, and K-module rank m = 6/e.

**Lemma 5.0 (sketch).** For such B, Hg^der = L^der.

*Argument.* By Deligne, over C each simple factor of Hg^der acts on each CM-eigenspace through a tensor product of minuscule representations, and the Hodge cocharacter is non-central in exactly one tensor factor (weight 1 allows only two characters). Q-simple factors whose cocharacter is central are not in Hg. Hence a tensor-decomposable action must come from a Galois-conjugate family Res_{F/Q}H. That requires n^d to divide the eigenspace dimension m ∈ {1, 2, 3, 6} with n ≥ 2 and d ≥ 2, which never happens. In the indecomposable case the minuscule irreducible representations of dimension m ≤ 6 other than SL_m-standard are self-dual: Sp_{2r}-standard and SO₆ on ∧²C⁴. A self-dual piece identifies an eigenspace with the dual of its conjugate, and this produces endomorphisms or extra classes in H² beyond K. That contradicts End⁰ = K: degree-2 Hodge classes correspond to Rosati-symmetric endomorphisms. A diagonally embedded factor across two CM-eigenspaces fails the same way. This is the dimension ≤ 5 argument of Moonen–Zarhin [15, §2–3] specialized to these ranks. We give it as a sketch, and Proposition 5.1 depends on it.

So Hg ≠ L exactly when the center of Hg is smaller than that of L. Equivalently, the Galois span of the central Hodge cocharacter is a proper subspace. Our engine (`engine.py`) enumerates, for each e, the transitive Galois types of K, i.e. subgroups P ⊂ C₂ ≀ S_e containing central complex conjugation, and all signature profiles (p_σ, q_σ). It computes the span W of the central cocharacter exactly and lists the exceptional monomials in the orthogonal complement. Two filters are applied:
- determinant monomials of degree 2 are discarded (they are divisor classes);
- profiles that force extra H² Hodge lines are discarded, since then End⁰ ⊋ K, contradicting the type assumption.
Each remaining exceptional class is tested for being *Weil-induced*: it must come from an imaginary quadratic k ⊂ K for which (B, k) has signature (3,3).

**Controls.** In dimension 4 (e = 1, 2) the engine returns exactly the Weil data of Moonen–Zarhin. In dimension 5 (e = 1) it returns nothing exceptional [15]. The run is `python3 engine.py 2,2 1,4 1,5 3,2 2,3 1,6`.

**Proposition 5.1 (computer-assisted; assumes Lemma 5.0).** For simple type-IV sixfolds with e ∈ {1, 2, 3}, every profile that carries exceptional Hodge classes on some power has all of its exceptional classes Weil-induced. Its imaginary quadratic k ⊂ K has k-signature (3,3). The profiles are e = 1 with (3,3); e = 2 with (1,2)/(1,2) and (1,2)/(2,1); and e = 3 with (0,2),(0,2),(1,1) up to the Galois action. For e = 6 (CM sixfolds) we do not enumerate: HC for CM abelian varieties is claimed in the unrefereed preprint [25].

So for simple type IV the dimension-six question reduces to Weil sixfolds. The split ones are covered by Markman [11, 12]. The nonsplit ones form a 9-dimensional datum for each k, and they are open.

### 5.2 Reach of cover families

A family of cyclic covers of P¹ dominates its Shimura variety only if it appears in Moonen's list of special families [14]. All twenty of those have dimension ≤ 3. So no cyclic family dominates a 9-dimensional nonsplit Weil-sixfold datum. The same holds for the nonabelian families of [6] we checked (`fgm/fgm_scan.py`). They have r ≤ 7 branch points over P¹, hence at most r − 3 = 4 moduli, so none can dominate a 9-dimensional domain. The script lists the period-domain type of each piece. Cover families of these kinds reach nonsplit Weil sixfolds only along proper subvarieties of the 9-dimensional datum. Examples are the e = 3 Shimura curves below and the 3-dimensional type-III loci of Theorem B(1).

### 5.3 A nonsplit Shimura curve (conditional)

The 4-point cyclic covers y^m = x^{a₀}(x−1)^{a₁}(x−t)^{a₂} with m ∈ {9, 14, 18} give 13 one-parameter families. Their primitive part contains a sixfold of type IV (e = 3) with a (3,3) Weil structure, and each family dominates its Shimura curve (domain dimension 1). We computed the k-Hermitian form exactly from the twisted-cycle intersection matrix, validated by the Kita–Yoshida bilinear relation. Twelve of the families are split. The family m = 18, a = (1,4,4,9), k = Q(√−3) is **nonsplit**; its class is invariant under all orderings and Galois relabellings (`weil_disc_exact.py`, `disc_invariance_exact.py`, `klt_check.py`). The determinants are computed in exact arithmetic in Q(ζ_m) = Q[x]/Φ_m, with complex conjugation x ↦ x⁻¹. Each det H_k is checked to lie in Q. The earlier high-precision versions (`weil_disc.py`, `disc_invariance.py`, mpmath at 60 digits followed by rational reconstruction) give identical values on all 13 families and all 3 invariance tests. gabrielov_cell independently confirmed the verdict on the rStation forum [7]. They also proposed a closed form: modulo Nm(k^×), the class of −det H_k equals Nm_{E⁺/Q}(∏_j (1 − ζ_m^{a_j})). This class is manifestly invariant under permutations of the a_j and under Galois relabelling. On all 13 families it gives the same norm class as our exact determinants (`gc_formula_check.py`). We checked the formula numerically only, not its derivation, so we do not use it in any proof. It does locate the nonsplit factor. The exponent a_j = 9 = m/2 contributes 1 − ζ₁₈⁹ = 2, with Nm_{E⁺/Q}(2) = 8 ≡ 2. Since 2 is inert in Q(√−3), it is not a norm. For m = 14, a = (1,1,5,7) the same factor 2 appears, but 2 splits in Q(√−7), so that family is split. For the nonsplit m = 18 curve, algebraicity of the Weil classes follows from the abelian-covers theorem of [26], which is unrefereed. So this curve is resolved *conditionally*.

### 5.4 Types I and II

- **Type II.** For very general members of a type-II PEL family the Hodge group is the Lefschetz group, and the Hodge ring of all powers is generated by divisors [16, 13].
- **Type I (sketch).** Exotic Hodge groups of Mumford type would act on H¹ ⊗ C as r copies of a tensor-induced representation ρ^{⊗d} with each factor minuscule. Then 12 = r·n^d with n = dim ρ ≥ 2. For d ≥ 2 the only option is n = d = 2 (n^d ∈ {4, 8, 9, 16, …} must divide 12), i.e. SL₂ ⊗ SL₂ on C⁴. That representation is orthogonal, so it cannot carry the symplectic Hodge form with commutant a field, and r = 3 is excluded. For d = 1 one gets the Lefschetz group. This excludes exotic type-I Hodge groups for simple sixfolds. We give it as a sketch, consistent with [15]; contrast Mumford's fourfolds, where 8 = 2³.

## 6. An instance: the D₄∘Q₈ Prym family

Frediani–Grosselli–Mohajer [6] list the group G = D₄∘Q₈ = 2^{1+4}_− (order 32), acting on curves of genus 17 with quotient P¹ and six branch points. Five branch points have non-central involutions as local monodromy, and one has the center z. Their Prym-type factor gives a 3-dimensional special family. The faithful 4-dimensional representation ρ₄ is quaternionic, with Frobenius–Schur indicator −1 and Q-form W = D ⊗ Q², where D = (−1,−1)_Q. Q₈ acts on D by left multiplication and D₄ acts on Q² by signed permutations. The multiplicity space H¹_par(P¹ − 6 pts, W) has dimension 12 and (3,3) balance. So it is H¹ of a type-III sixfold B with D ⊂ End⁰(B), and the domain SO*(6)/U(3) has dimension 3 = 6 − 3, which matches the 3 moduli.

We computed the polarization exactly: the parabolic cup product on H¹_par, via the explicit formula of Dettweiler–Wewers [5, Thm 2.5], with right D-multiplication as endomorphisms (`d4q8_disc.py`). Checks:
- the form is alternating and nondegenerate;
- right multiplication by a ∈ D is a similitude with Rosati involution a ↦ ā;
- four independent generating tuples give the same classes.
The induced Weil forms are:

| k ⊂ D | signature | Weil form |
|---|---|---|
| Q(i) | (3,3) | split |
| Q(√−2) | (3,3) | split |
| Q(√−3) | (3,3) | **nonsplit** |

This table comes from a direct cup-product computation that does not use Theorem A. Independently, we computed Δ itself from the same cup product (`d4q8_delta.py`). The D-valued skew-Hermitian form Φ is defined by E(ax, y) = trd(a Φ(x, y)). The script checks that E = trd Φ, that Φ is D-sesquilinear and that Φ is skew-Hermitian. It then takes T to be the Gram matrix of Φ on a D-basis and computes Nrd(T) through D ⊗ Q(i) ≅ M₂(Q(i)). All four tuples give Nrd(T) = 1/64, so Δ ≡ 1 mod squares. For Δ = 1, Theorem A predicts split, split, nonsplit for Q(i), Q(√−2), Q(√−3): (−1, −1) ≅ (−1, −2) ≅ D and (−1, −3) ≇ D. These are exactly the verdicts in the table. We then computed the Weil form directly for five more subfields, k = Q(√−d) with d = 5, 6, 10, 11, 14 (`d4q8_morek.py`). Theorem A at Δ = 1 predicts split for d = 5, 10 and nonsplit for d = 6, 11, 14. The direct computation agrees in all 16 cases (8 fields, 2 tuples). This is an end-to-end test of Theorem A on a geometric family. Without the Δ computation it would not be a test, because every one of the eight verdict patterns occurs for some Δ. Only the Q(√−3) verdict and the existence of one split k ⊂ D enter Corollary 6.1. Theorem B(1) therefore gives the following.

**Corollary 6.1.** For every member B of the D₄∘Q₈ family (and of its PEL Shimura variety), the Q(√−3)-Weil classes W_{Q(√−3)}(B) are algebraic, although the Q(√−3)-Weil form of B is nonsplit. HC holds for all powers of the very general member.

This is a 3-dimensional family of nonsplit Weil sixfolds with algebraic Weil classes. It is unconditional, unlike the curve of §5.3. Corollary 6.1 can also be reached through [24, Thm D] (an independent mechanism) together with D-translation. So Corollary 6.1 is not a new algebraicity result for this family. What is new is the identification of the family as a nonsplit Q(√−3)-Weil locus: it sits inside the open 9-dimensional nonsplit datum of §5.1.

## 7. The dimension-six frontier and limitations

For **simple** abelian sixfolds, the results above and the literature give the following picture. "Resolved" is meant in the sense of §2.

| Albert type | exceptional classes | status |
|---|---|---|
| I, II | none on very general members; no exotic groups (type I, sketch) | divisorial |
| III, Q(√−Δ) ↪ D | Q_D(B) (Weil classes for all k ⊂ D) | resolved (Thm B; Markman + Abdulali) |
| III, Q(√−Δ) ↪̸ D | Q_D(B), all W_k nonsplit | **open** (3-dim compact data; Thm C, Prop 7.1) |
| IV, e = 1, split (3,3) | Weil | resolved (Markman) |
| IV, e = 1, nonsplit (3,3) | Weil | **open** (9-dim datum), except on special loci: Thm B(1) loci, the §5.3 curve (conditional) |
| IV, e = 2, 3 | Weil-induced only (Prop 5.1) | the status of the induced Weil datum |
| IV, e = 6 (CM) | many | conditional on [25] |

Setting aside CM and non-simple sixfolds, and assuming the sketched Lemma 5.0 behind Proposition 5.1 and the type-I sketch, the open core for simple sixfolds is therefore the nonsplit Weil sixfolds: a 9-dimensional datum for each k. It includes, as 3-dimensional special subvarieties, the type-III data of Theorem C, where every k ⊂ D is nonsplit. Elsewhere, Theorem B(1) resolves 3-dimensional subvarieties of it. Via Schoen's degeneration [19, Prop. 10], used in reverse, nonsplit Weil sixfolds would follow from split Weil eightfolds (Remark 7.5). Those are the next case of Markman's program [12, §12].

### 7.1 The open type-III data are anisotropic

The 3-dimensional Shimura varieties of Theorem C look small enough for $G$-cover families with six branch points over $\mathbf P^1$ to dominate them, as the $D_4\circ Q_8$ family does for a resolved datum. The next result shows that such families have to meet a strong condition. We use the conventions of §3.1: $T(ax,by)=a\,T(x,y)\,\bar b$, $T(y,x)=-\overline{T(x,y)}$ and $E(ax,y)=\operatorname{trd}(a\,T(x,y))$.

**Proposition 7.1.** If $T$ is isotropic over $\mathbf Q$, then $\mathbf Q(\sqrt{-\Delta(T)})$ embeds in $D$. Equivalently, the skew-Hermitian forms of Theorem C are anisotropic.

*Proof.* Let $x\neq0$ with $T(x,x)=0$. By nondegeneracy there is $y$ with $T(x,y)\neq0$. Replacing $y$ by $cy$ with $\bar c=T(x,y)^{-1}$ gives $T(x,y)=1$, so $T(y,x)=-1$. For a pure quaternion $\lambda$,

$$T(y+\lambda x,\,y+\lambda x)=T(y,y)+\lambda-\bar\lambda=T(y,y)+2\lambda,$$

and $T(y,y)$ is pure because $T$ is skew-Hermitian. With $\lambda=-T(y,y)/2$, the vectors $x$ and $y'=y+\lambda x$ span a plane $P$ with Gram matrix $J=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$. Since $T|_P$ is nondegenerate, $V=P\perp P^\perp$ and $P^\perp=Dz$ with $t=T(z,z)$ pure and nonzero. Then $\Delta(T)\equiv\operatorname{Nrd}(J)\cdot\operatorname{nrd}(t)=\operatorname{nrd}(t)\bmod\mathbf Q^{*2}$, using the reduced-norm description of $\Delta$ in §3.1 (`isotropy_check.py` checks $\operatorname{Nrd}(J\perp\langle t\rangle)=\operatorname{nrd}(t)$ and the basis-change rule exactly). Finally $t^2=-\operatorname{nrd}(t)$, so $\mathbf Q(t)\cong\mathbf Q(\sqrt{-\Delta(T)})\subset D$. ∎

**Corollary 7.2.** Let $(D,T)$ be a datum of Theorem C, i.e. $\mathbf Q(\sqrt{-\Delta(T)})$ does not embed in $D$.
1. The identity component of the group of $D$-linear isometries of $T$ is semisimple, and its $\mathbf Q$-rank is the Witt index of $T$, which is $0$. Hence the PEL Shimura threefold of $(D,T)$ is compact (Borel–Harish-Chandra, Mostow–Tamagawa).
2. Let $\mathbb V$ be a polarized weight-1 $\mathbf Q$-VHS over a smooth curve $S$, with a flat action of $D$ whose fibres have datum $(D,T)$. Then the local monodromy of $\mathbb V$ at every point of the smooth completion of $S$ has finite order.

*Proof of 2.* Local monodromy is quasi-unipotent. After a finite base change it is unipotent; let $N$ be its logarithm. $N$ is rational, commutes with $D$, satisfies $E(Nx,y)=-E(x,Ny)$, and $N^2=0$ because the weight is 1. So $\operatorname{im}N$ is a $D$-submodule with $E(Nx,Ny)=-E(x,N^2y)=0$. For $u,w\in\operatorname{im}N$ and all $a\in D$, $au\in\operatorname{im}N$, so $\operatorname{trd}(a\,T(u,w))=E(au,w)=0$. Hence $T(u,w)=0$, and $\operatorname{im}N$ is totally isotropic for $T$. By Proposition 7.1, $\operatorname{im}N=0$. ∎

So a family of $G$-covers of $\mathbf P^1$ reaches a datum of Theorem C only if its type-III piece has finite local monodromy at every boundary point of the Hurwitz space. There is a simple sufficient test for the opposite. Take a $G$-cover with branch cycle $(g_1,\dots,g_r)$, and let $S$ be a cyclically consecutive set of branch points with $2\le|S|\le r-2$ and complement $S'$. Put $g_S=\prod_{i\in S}g_i$, $H_S=\langle g_i:i\in S\rangle$ and $H_{S'}=\langle g_i:i\in S'\rangle$. When the points of $S$ collide, the stable limit of the cover has a dual graph $\Gamma$ with vertex stabilizers $H_S$, $H_{S'}$ and edge stabilizer $\langle g_S\rangle$. As a virtual $G$-module, $H^1(\Gamma,\mathbf Q)=\mathbf Q[G/\langle g_S\rangle]-\mathbf Q[G/H_S]-\mathbf Q[G/H_{S'}]+\mathbf Q$. This is the weight-0 part of the limit mixed Hodge structure, so its $W$-isotypic part is the toric part of the $W$-isotypic piece, and $N\neq0$ there exactly when that part is nonzero. Using $\dim_{\mathbf Q}\operatorname{Hom}_G(W,\mathbf Q[G/H])=\dim W^H$, we get

$$\dim_{\mathbf Q}\operatorname{Hom}_G(W,H^1(\Gamma,\mathbf Q))=t(S):=\dim W^{\langle g_S\rangle}-\dim W^{H_S}-\dim W^{H_{S'}}\qquad(W^G=0).$$

So $t(S)>0$ forces $N\neq0$ on the type-III piece. In that case the datum is isotropic, and $\mathbf Q(\sqrt{-\Delta})$ embeds in $D$.

**A scoped negative.** We computed $\Delta(T)$ exactly, by the method of §6, for 60 type-III pieces of $G$-cover families:
- $G\subset K\wr C_2\subset\mathrm{GL}_2(D)$, generated by quaternionic reflections, with $K\in\{Q_8,2T\}$ in $D=(-1,-1)$ and $K=\mathrm{Dic}_3$ in $D=(-1,-3)$. We used $r=6,7$ branch points for $Q_8$ and $\mathrm{Dic}_3$ and $r=6$ for $2T$, giving 57 tuples in up to six subgroups per $(K,r)$ (`mono_search.py`);
- $G=\mathrm{SL}(2,5)$ acting through its rational quaternionic character of degree 4, with branch data $(3,3,3,3,3,5)$ (`sl25.py`, 3 tuples, which needs Sage/GAP).

In every case $\mathbf Q(\sqrt{-\Delta})$ embeds in $D$. The $\mathrm{SL}(2,5)$ case is the instructive one. We chose it because a numerical pattern in the monomial data predicted $\Delta\equiv15$, which would be open for $D=(-1,-1)$. It does give $\Delta\equiv15$. But there $\operatorname{End}_G(W)\cong(-1,-15)$, which is ramified at $\{3,\infty\}$ and contains $\mathbf Q(\sqrt{-15})$. In a separate sample of 20 monomial tuples per $(K,r)$, 8 to 13 have a consecutive collision with $t(S)>0$ (`toric.py`). For those families, Corollary 7.2 gives the verdict "embeds" without computing $\Delta$. The datum is constant on a connected family, and the test only covers consecutive collisions in one ordering, so these counts are lower bounds.

**An exact orbit test ($r=6$, $|G|\le158$).** The test needs no $\Delta$, so it can be run over all small groups. We call $(G,\chi,C_1,\dots,C_6)$ *admissible* when:
- $\chi$ is an irreducible, rational-valued character of $G$ of degree $n\ge3$ with Frobenius–Schur indicator $-1$, so that $\operatorname{End}_G(W)$ is a definite quaternion algebra for the $\chi$-isotypic rational piece $W$;
- $C_1,\dots,C_6$ are conjugacy classes acting nontrivially on $W$ (so $\operatorname{codim}>0$; see the caveat below) with $\sum_i\operatorname{codim}\bigl(\rho_\chi\text{-fixed space of }C_i\bigr)=2n+6$, i.e. $\dim_{\mathbf Q}H^1_{\mathrm{par}}(W)=12$;
- the class-multiset structure constant is positive.

A Hurwitz component (a braid orbit of generating tuples $g_i\in C_i$, $\prod g_i=1$) is *non-compact on $W$* if some tuple in it has $t(S)>0$ for some cyclically consecutive $S$. By the previous paragraph and Corollary 7.2, a non-compact component does not reach a datum of Theorem C. `compact_exact.py` decides this exactly for every class multiset. It enumerates the tuples of one class ordering up to simultaneous conjugation; every braid orbit meets this slice, because the braid group maps onto $S_6$. From each slice tuple with all $t(S)=0$, it closes breadth-first under $\sigma_i^{\pm1}$ inside the set of such tuples, and it reports a candidate if the closure never meets a tuple with $t(S)>0$. No slice was truncated. Groups of odd order have no real-valued nontrivial irreducible characters, and the stage-1 scan `scan_groups.py` finds admissible data for orders $\le158$ only at $|G|\in\{32,48,64,72,96,120,144\}$. Order 128 was not scanned. For those seven orders there are 26,807 admissible class multisets:
- 17,052 have no generating tuple;
- 9,755 have only non-compact components;
- none has a component of compact type.

Controls:
- $G=C_7$ with $\chi$ the sum of the nontrivial characters (`compact_exact_control.py`). Here an orbit is compact iff no 2 to 4 of the exponents sum to $0$; 66/66 verdicts agree, 6 of them compact.
- 780 comparisons of the toric ranks and braid moves against direct GAP computations (`compact_crosscheck.py`), with 0 mismatches.

*Caveat (corrected in v4).* The v3 text called $C_1,\dots,C_6$ merely nontrivial, but `scan_groups.py` keeps only classes of positive codimension. The referee of v3 pointed out admissible data outside the scan, e.g. $G=(Q_8\circ D_4)\times\langle\kappa\rangle$ of order 64 with the inflated degree-4 character and the tuple $(i\kappa,j,r,s,(ijrs)^{-1},\kappa)$, of codimensions $(4,4,2,2,2,0)$. A branch point whose class lies in $\ker\rho_\chi$ can be forgotten on $W$: the local system $\mathbb W$ extends across it, so $H^1_{\mathrm{par}}(W)$ and its variation depend only on the remaining $r'<6$ points, with group $\rho_\chi(G)$. Such data are therefore $r'$-point data for $\rho_\chi(G)$ with $r'\le5$, and the test does not cover them.

This negative is confined to the stated range ($r=6$, all six classes acting nontrivially on $W$, $|G|\le158$ except 128, $\chi$ as above). It does not change the status of any Hodge class. The open type-III data therefore need cover families with finite local monodromy on $W$ at every boundary point, or a different construction. We do not know whether such cover data exist.

**Remark 7.3 (the converse of Proposition 7.1 is false).** Anisotropy does not characterize the data of Theorem C. Take $D=(-1,-1)_{\mathbf Q}$ and

$$T_{14}=\operatorname{diag}(-2i-3j,\;3i+j-2k,\;2i+3k),$$

with reduced norms $13,14,13$. Then $\Delta\equiv14$, and $\mathbf Q(\sqrt{-14})$ embeds in $D$ because $14=3^2+2^2+1^2$. So by Theorem A, $T_{14}$ is *not* a datum of Theorem C. We show that $T_{14}$ is anisotropic.

Let $p$ be an odd prime. Then $D_p\cong M_2(\mathbf Q_p)$; put $e=E_{11}$. For $V=D^3$ with $h(x,y)=\sum_a x_at_a\bar y_a$, the space $U=eV_p$ is 6-dimensional over $\mathbf Q_p$. The form $b_p(u,w)=h(u,w)_{12}$ is a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form on $U$, since $h$ is skew-Hermitian and $\bar e=E_{22}$ (Morita equivalence). Under this equivalence, free totally isotropic $D_p$-lines $L\subset V_p$ correspond to totally isotropic planes $eL\subset U$. A nonzero $x\in D^3$ with $h(x,x)=0$ generates such a line $D_px$. Hence an isotropic $T$ forces $b_p$ to have Witt index $\ge2$ at every odd $p$.

*Certificate at $p=13$.* Choose $s\in\mathbf Z_{13}$ with $s^2=-1$ and $s\equiv5\pmod{13}$, and split $D_{13}$ by $i\mapsto\operatorname{diag}(s,-s)$, $j\mapsto J$. Over $\mathbf Z_{13}$, $b_{13}\cong b_0\perp13\,b_1$ with $b_0,b_1$ unimodular, and the residue forms over $\mathbf F_{13}$ are

$$\bar b_0=\langle10,4,10,2\rangle,\qquad\bar b_1=\langle4,7\rangle .$$

Their signed determinants are $7$ and $11$. Neither is a square mod 13, since the squares are $\{1,3,4,9,10,12\}$. So each residue form has anisotropic part of dimension $2$. By Springer's theorem, the anisotropic part of $b_{13}$ has dimension $2+2=4$, and the Witt index of $b_{13}$ is $(6-2-2)/2=1<2$. Hence $T_{14}$ is anisotropic, although $\mathbf Q(\sqrt{-\Delta})$ embeds in $D$. The referee of v2 supplied this certificate; `cert13.py` reproduces it.

`local_witt.py` computes the Witt index of $b_p$ for $T_{14}$ at $p\in\{3,5,7,11,13\}$ in two complementary ways that share one $p$-adic diagonalization. One uses the residue forms ($W(\mathbf Q_p)\cong W(\mathbf F_p)^2$). The other uses the discriminant and the Hasse invariant. The two agree, and both splittings used give index 1 at $p=13$. The signed discriminant of $b_p$ is always $-\Delta$. On the summand of $\langle t_a\rangle$, $b_p$ has Gram matrix $\pm\tau_aJ$, where $\tau_a\in M_2(\mathbf Q_p)$ is the image of $t_a$. This matrix is symmetric because $\tau_a$ is traceless, and its determinant is $\det\tau_a=\operatorname{nrd}(t_a)$. So $\det b_p=\Delta$, which was also checked on 100 pairs of a form and a prime. At a prime $p$ that splits in $\mathbf Q(\sqrt{-\Delta})$, the discriminant of $b_p$ is therefore trivial, and only the Hasse invariant separates Witt index 1 from 3. Here $-14\equiv12\equiv5^2\pmod{13}$.

The box search reflects this. We took 40 random diagonal $T$ over $(-1,-1)$ with integral pure entries of size $\le3$. We then searched for $x\in D^3$ with integral coordinates in $[-3,3]$ (`isotropy_check.py`):
- none of the 15 forms with $\Delta\equiv7\bmod8$ has an isotropic vector, as Proposition 7.1 requires;
- 24 of the 25 others have one;
- the remaining form is $T_{14}$.

`local_witt.py` also contains two controls. The first consists of 30 forms with a built-in isotropic vector, which have Witt index $\ge2$ at all tested primes (0 failures). The second is the isotropic partner $\operatorname{diag}(-2i-3j,\,2i+3j,\,3i+2j+k)$, which has the same $D$ and $\Delta$.

Consequences:
1. The anisotropic type-III data are strictly larger than the data of Theorem C. Data such as $T_{14}$ have compact Shimura threefolds, yet a split $k\subset D$ still exists. So Theorem B applies to them, and compactness is not the obstruction.
2. Corollary 7.2 still constrains every family that reaches a Theorem C datum. It does not single those data out among the compact ones.

Proposition 7.4 gives the exact local–global description of anisotropy. **It does not change the status of any Hodge class.** Condition (i) below is the hypothesis of Theorem B, and its failure is the hypothesis of Theorem C. Condition (ii) only sorts the data already resolved by Theorem B into compact and non-compact ones.

**Proposition 7.4 (isotropy criterion).** Let $D$ be a definite quaternion algebra over $\mathbf Q$, $T=\operatorname{diag}(t_1,t_2,t_3)$ with $t_a\in D$ pure and nonzero, $n_a=\operatorname{nrd}(t_a)$, $\Delta=n_1n_2n_3$ and $K=\mathbf Q(\sqrt{-\Delta})$. For a prime $p$ split in $D$, let $b_p$ be the 6-dimensional symmetric Morita form of $T_p$, as in Remark 7.3 (for $p=2$ the same construction applies with any splitting $D_2\cong M_2(\mathbf Q_2)$). Then $T$ is isotropic over $\mathbf Q$ if and only if
- (i) $K$ embeds in $D$, and
- (ii) $b_p$ is hyperbolic at every prime $p$ that splits in both $D$ and $K$.

Condition (ii) is a finite check once $T$ is put in integral form. Replacing $x_a$ by $c_ax_a$ with $c_a\in\mathbf Z\smallsetminus0$ gives an isometric form with $t_a$ replaced by $c_a^2t_a$, the same $K$ and the same isotropy. So we may assume that $t_1,t_2,t_3$ lie in a common order $\mathcal O$ of $D$ (for $D=(a,b)_{\mathbf Q}$ with $a,b\in\mathbf Z$, e.g. $\mathcal O=\mathbf Z\langle1,i,j,ij\rangle$). Then (ii) holds automatically at every odd $p\nmid n_1n_2n_3$ that splits in $D$, and only the primes $2$ and $p\mid n_1n_2n_3$ remain. Without integrality this fails. The referee of v3 gave $T=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(i,\,(i+2j+2k)/3,\,i+j\bigr)$ over $(-1,-1)$: here $n_1n_2n_3=2$, $K=\mathbf Q(\sqrt{-2})$ embeds in $D$ and $3$ splits in $K$, but $b_3$ has Witt index $1$. In the integral model $x_2\mapsto3x_2$ one has $n_2=81$, so $p=3$ is among the primes to check (`integral_check.py`).

*Proof.* Let $X$ be the variety of totally isotropic free $D$-lines in $(D^3,T)$. A nonzero isotropic vector $x$ gives the line $Dx$, and every point of such a line is isotropic, so $T$ is isotropic iff $X(\mathbf Q)\neq\varnothing$. Over $\bar{\mathbf Q}$, Morita equivalence identifies $X$ with the variety of totally isotropic 2-planes of a 6-dimensional quadratic space. This variety is projective and homogeneous under the connected isometry group of $T$, a form of $\mathrm{SO}_6$; since $2<3$, it is connected. By Harder's Hasse principle for projective homogeneous varieties [8], cited as in [17, §1], $T$ is isotropic iff $T_v$ is isotropic at every place $v$.

*$v=\infty$.* For a pure $t\neq0$ in $\mathbb H$, the set $\{xt\bar x:x\in\mathbb H^\times\}$ is all of $\mathbb H^0\smallsetminus0$, since these elements form the conjugation orbit of $t$ scaled by $\mathbb R_{>0}$. So $x_1t_1\bar x_1=-t_2$ has a solution, and $T_\infty$ is isotropic.

*$p$ ramified in $D$.* Then $D_p$ is a division algebra, $L=\mathbf Q_p(t)$ is a quadratic field, and $D_p=L\oplus Lj'$ with $j'tj'^{-1}=-t$ and $-\operatorname{nrd}(j')\notin N(L^\times)$.

First we show that $\{xt\bar x:x\in D_p^\times\}=\{v\in D_p^0\smallsetminus0:\operatorname{nrd}v\in\operatorname{nrd}(t)\mathbf Q_p^{*2}\}$. The inclusion $\subset$ is clear. Conversely, let $\operatorname{nrd}v=c^2\operatorname{nrd}t$. Since $xt\bar x=\operatorname{nrd}(x)\,xtx^{-1}$, we need $x$ with $xtx^{-1}=v/\operatorname{nrd}(x)$. By Skolem–Noether, the $x$ with $xtx^{-1}=v/c$ form a coset $y_0L^\times$, and those with $xtx^{-1}=-v/c$ form the coset $y_0j'L^\times$. Their reduced norms fill $\operatorname{nrd}(y_0)N(L^\times)$ and $\operatorname{nrd}(y_0)\operatorname{nrd}(j')N(L^\times)$. We need $c$ in the first set or $-c$ in the second. Since $[\mathbf Q_p^\times:N(L^\times)]=2$ and $-\operatorname{nrd}(j')\notin N(L^\times)$, one of the two holds.

Now suppose $T_p$ is isotropic. Then the argument of Proposition 7.1, applied over $\mathbf Q_p$, gives $T_p\cong J\perp\langle s\rangle$ with $\operatorname{nrd}(s)\equiv\Delta$. The ternary form $q_0=\operatorname{nrd}|_{D_p^0}$ does not represent $-1$, because $\operatorname{nrd}=\langle1\rangle\perp q_0$ is anisotropic. Hence $-\Delta\notin\mathbf Q_p^{*2}$.

Conversely, suppose $-\Delta\notin\mathbf Q_p^{*2}$.
- For odd $p$: $q_0$ is anisotropic of square determinant, so it represents exactly the three square classes other than $-1$. The four classes multiply to a square. If $n_1,n_2,n_3$ were pairwise distinct, they would be those three classes, so $n_1n_2n_3\equiv-1$ and $-\Delta$ would be a square. Hence $n_a\equiv n_b$ for some $a\neq b$. By the lemma, $-t_b=xt_a\bar x$ for some $x$, and $(x,1)$ is isotropic for $\langle t_a,t_b\rangle$.
- For $p=2$: $D_2\cong(-1,-1)_{\mathbf Q_2}$, and the 7 square classes represented by $q_0$ give 343 triples, of which 301 have $-n_1n_2n_3$ nonsquare. For each of these, `local2_cert.py` exhibits pure $v_1,v_2,v_3$ with $v_1+v_2+v_3=0$ and $\operatorname{nrd}(v_a)\in n_a\mathbf Q_2^{*2}$: 133 by a coincidence $n_a\equiv n_b$ and 168 by an explicit integral triangle. By the lemma, $v_a=x_at_a\bar x_a$, so $(x_1,x_2,x_3)$ is isotropic.

So at a ramified $p$, $T_p$ is isotropic iff $p$ does not split in $K$. Over all ramified $p$ (with $\infty$ automatic, since $K$ is imaginary), this is exactly condition (i).

*$p$ split in $D$.* $T_p$ is isotropic iff $b_p$ has Witt index $\ge2$. The signed discriminant of $b_p$ is $-\Delta$ (Remark 7.3). The anisotropic part of a 6-dimensional form over $\mathbf Q_p$ has dimension 0, 2 or 4. The unique anisotropic quaternary form, the norm form of the division algebra, has trivial discriminant, as does $H$. So if $p$ does not split in $K$, the Witt index is exactly 2 and $T_p$ is isotropic. If $p$ splits in $K$, the Witt index is 1 or 3, and $T_p$ is isotropic iff $b_p$ is hyperbolic: this is condition (ii). Now let the $t_a$ lie in a common order $\mathcal O$, and let $p\nmid2n_1n_2n_3$ split in $D$ and in $K$. Then $\mathcal O$ lies in a maximal order $\Lambda$ with $\Lambda_p\cong M_2(\mathbf Z_p)$, so the images $\tau_a$ are integral with unit determinant $n_a$. On the lattice $e\Lambda_p^3\cong\mathbf Z_p^6$, $b_p$ is integral and has Gram blocks $\pm\tau_aJ$ of unit determinant, so it is unimodular. Its signed discriminant $-\Delta$ is a square, so the residue form is a 6-dimensional form over $\mathbf F_p$ with trivial discriminant, hence hyperbolic, and so is $b_p$. ∎

For $T_{14}$, condition (i) holds and (ii) fails at $p=13$. Proposition 7.1 is the implication "isotropic ⇒ (i)". The proof uses Harder's theorem through [17]; we have not consulted [8] directly.

The finite checks agree with the proposition:
- `ramified_local.py` checks the division-prime step against a direct Hilbert-symbol computation for $(-1,-1)_2$, $(-1,-2)_2$, $(-1,-3)_3$, $(2,5)_5$ and $(3,7)_7$, with 0 disagreements;
- `clifford_local_test.py` covers 621 pairs (form, odd prime). Every $p$ inert or ramified in $K$ has index 2, and every split $p$ has index 1 or 3;
- `isotropy_criterion_test.py` covers 60 forms over $(-1,-1)$. All 49 forms predicted isotropic have a box vector with coordinates in $[-3,3]$. None of the 11 forms predicted anisotropic has one: 9 fail (i) and 2 fail (ii).

**Remark 7.5 (split Weil eightfolds would close the open core).** Let $A_6$ be any Weil sixfold for an imaginary quadratic $k$, with Hermitian form $H_6$ of signature $(3,3)$ and determinant $\delta<0$. Let $A_2$ be a Weil-type abelian surface for $k$ with Hermitian form $\langle1,\delta\rangle$ of signature $(1,1)$; these form a 1-dimensional family (the period domain of $\mathrm U(1,1)$), see [21].

Then $A=A_6\times A_2$, with the diagonal $k$-action and the product polarization, is of Weil type with signature $(4,4)$. Its discriminant is $\delta^2\equiv1=(-1)^4$, so $A$ is split.

Write $\alpha_6$, $\bar\alpha_6$ and $\alpha_2$, $\bar\alpha_2$ for generators of the two $k$-eigenlines of the Weil spaces. Then $W(A)\otimes\mathbf C$ is spanned by $\alpha_6\alpha_2$ and $\bar\alpha_6\bar\alpha_2$. The Weil classes of $A_2$ are of type $(1,1)$, hence algebraic. For a rational Weil class $c=\beta\alpha_2+\bar\beta\bar\alpha_2$ with $\beta\neq0$, the correspondence $x\mapsto\pi_{1*}(x\cdot\pi_2^*c)$ sends $a\alpha_6\alpha_2+\bar a\bar\alpha_6\bar\alpha_2$ to $\lambda a\alpha_6+\bar\lambda\bar a\bar\alpha_6$, where $\lambda=\bar\beta\int_{A_2}\alpha_2\bar\alpha_2\neq0$. So it maps $W(A)$ onto $W(A_6)$.

Hence algebraicity of Weil classes on all split Weil eightfolds for $k$ would give it on all Weil sixfolds for $k$. With Theorem B's translation step, that includes $W_k(B)$ and $Q_D(B)$ for the data of Theorem C whenever $k\subset D$. This is Schoen's argument [19, Prop. 10], as used in [12, §11.5], one dimension higher, and it is the reduction quoted in the overview at the start of §7. Split Weil eightfolds are the next case of Markman's program [12, §12]; we have no result on them.

**Limitations.**
1. Non-simple sixfolds (products) and CM sixfolds are not treated.
2. Type III with $D$ over a totally real $F\neq\mathbf Q$ does not occur in dimension six. Indeed $\dim B=2[F:\mathbf Q]\,m$ with $m=\dim_DH^1\ge2$ [20; 2, §4], so $[F:\mathbf Q]\,m=3$ forces $F=\mathbf Q$ and $m=3$. Theorems A–C therefore cover all simple type-III sixfolds, up to the generality assumptions stated.
3. Proposition 5.1 is computer-assisted, and the type-IV reduction is conditional on the sketched Lemma 5.0.
4. The type-I statement is a sketch.
5. Markman's theorem [11, 12] is used as published on arXiv.
6. [26] and [25] are unrefereed.
7. The data of Theorem C are exactly the data for which condition (i) of Proposition 7.4 fails. Isotropy is equivalent to (i) and (ii) together. So anisotropy, the failure of (i) or (ii), is necessary for the data of Theorem C but not sufficient (Remark 7.3, corrected in v2). The automatic validity of (ii) at odd $p\nmid n_1n_2n_3$ needs integral $t_a$ (corrected in v4). Proposition 7.4 rests on Harder's theorem as cited in [17] and, at $p=2$, on a finite computer certificate. The §7.1 negatives cover only the listed groups, tuples and orders, and the orbit test only branch classes acting nontrivially on $W$.
