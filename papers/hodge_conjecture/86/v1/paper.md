# The fixed surface of a symplectic involution on Hilbert squares of quartics with a quasi-Galois point

## Abstract

Let $S=\{u^4+a u^2+b=0\}\subset\mathbb P^3$ be a smooth quartic, with $a$ and $b$ forms of degree 2 and 4 in $x,y,z$. These are the quartics with a quasi-Galois point, and they form the 12-dimensional family of K3 surfaces with a non-symplectic involution of type (8,8,1). Menet showed that exchanging the two totally tangent plane quartics attached to $S$ is an involution of this family, with partner $S^\vee=\{s^4+2as^2+a^2-4b=0\}$ in coordinates, and that $S\not\cong S^\vee$ generically. We show that $S^\vee$ is the K3 surface fixed by the symplectic involution of $S^{[2]}$ (when $S$ contains no line) studied by Markushevich–Tikhomirov and by Camere, Garbagnati, Kapustka and Kapustka. The incidence correspondence, a complete intersection of type (2,4) in $\mathbb P^4$, has degree 2 over each side and induces a Hodge similarity of multiplier 2 between their transcendental lattices. So for very general $S$ the surfaces are not twisted derived equivalent. This corrects the identification of the fixed surface with $S$ in the work of Camere et al. We also show that the construction stops at Picard number 8. For a smooth quartic and a point outside it, the projection has monodromy $S_4$, $D_4$ or $C_4$, and the surface of pairs on lines through the point is reducible only at quasi-Galois points. For a tower of double covers $S\to Y\to Y/g$ with $Y$ a weak del Pezzo surface, a K3 bigonal partner forces $K_Y^2\le2$, hence $\rho(S)\ge8$. The explicit cycle proves the Hodge conjecture for $S\times S^\vee$, known before via Markman's theorem, without it.

## 1. Introduction

Let $S$ and $S'$ be complex projective K3 surfaces. A Hodge similarity of multiplier $\lambda\in\mathbb Q_{>0}$ is an isomorphism of rational Hodge structures $T(S)_{\mathbb Q}\to T(S')_{\mathbb Q}$ that multiplies the intersection form by $\lambda$. If $\operatorname{End}_{\mathrm{Hdg}}T(S)_{\mathbb Q}=\mathbb Q$, then $\lambda$ is well defined modulo squares, and a similarity with $\lambda$ not a square is not induced by any isomorphism or (twisted) derived equivalence, since these give Hodge isometries of $T_{\mathbb Q}$. A dominant rational map of degree $n$ induces a similarity of multiplier $n$, so non-square multipliers do occur geometrically, for instance for quotients by Nikulin involutions. The Hodge conjecture predicts that every Hodge similarity is induced by an algebraic cycle on $S\times S'$. Known algebraic examples with $\lambda$ not a square and trivial endomorphism field come in families of dimension at most $12$ [1, 2, 3].

This note studies the simplest such family through a classical construction. Write
$$S_{a,b}=\{u^4+a\,u^2+b=0\}\subset\mathbb P^3_{(u:x:y:z)},\qquad a\in H^0(\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^2}(2)),\ b\in H^0(\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^2}(4)),$$
with the reflection $\tau(u)=-u$ of centre $p=(1:0:0:0)$. By Miura and Taki [4, Prop. 3.1 and Main Theorem (1)], the smooth surfaces $S_{a,b}$ with $a\ne0$ are exactly the smooth quartics with a quasi-Galois point that is not Galois (for $a=0$ the point is Galois), and the pairs $(S_{a,b},\tau)$ are exactly the general K3 surfaces with a non-symplectic involution of type $(8,8,1)$. The quotient $Y=S/\tau$ is the del Pezzo surface of degree $2$ given by $w^2=a^2-4b$, with $w=2u^2+a$.

**Definition.** The *bigonal dual* of $S_{a,b}$ is $S_{a,b}^\vee=S_{2a,\,a^2-4b}=\{s^4+2a\,s^2+a^2-4b=0\}$.

This is Menet's duality [5, Cor. 3.9] written in coordinates. Menet starts from two smooth plane quartics $B_0,\Delta_0$ tangent at eight points, takes the double plane $X$ branched along $B_0$ and the double cover of $X$ branched along one component of the preimage of $\Delta_0$, and shows that exchanging $B_0$ and $\Delta_0$ is a rational involution of the moduli space of type $(8,8,1)$.

Applying the rule twice gives $S_{4a,16b}$, which is $S_{a,b}$ after $s\mapsto2s$. In terms of the two plane quartics $\Delta_1=\{a^2-4b=0\}$ and $\Delta_2=\{b=0\}$, which satisfy $(a^2-4b)+4b=a^2$, duality exchanges $\Delta_1$ and $\Delta_2$. The surface $S$ is the double cover of the double plane branched along $\Delta_1$, branched along one of the two curves over $\Delta_2$, so $(B_0,\Delta_0)=(\Delta_1,\Delta_2)$ in Menet's notation. For the exchanged pair, the double plane is $v^2=b$ and the cover is $s^2=2v-a$; eliminating $v$ gives $(s^2+a)^2=4b$, which is $S^\vee$. The name comes from the bigonal construction for towers of double covers of curves [6, 7]. The analogy for genus one is the $2$-isogeny $y^2=x(x^2+ax+b)\leftrightarrow y^2=x(x^2-2ax+a^2-4b)$.

**Theorem A.** Let $S=S_{a,b}$ be smooth.
1. $S^\vee$ is smooth. Let $F\subset S^{[2]}$ be the closure of the set of pairs $\{P,Q\}$ of distinct points of $S$ on a line through $p$ with $Q\notin\{P,\tau P\}$. Then $\{u_1,u_2\}\mapsto s=u_1+u_2$ is an isomorphism $F\cong S^\vee$. If $S$ contains no line, $F$ is the K3 surface in the fixed locus of the symplectic involution $\tau^{[2]}\circ\beta$ of $S^{[2]}$, where $\beta$ is Beauville's involution [8, Lemma 5.3].
2. Let $\Gamma\subset S\times S^\vee$ be the incidence correspondence $\{(P,\{P,Q\})\}$. It has degree $2$ over both factors, and $\Gamma_*$ is a Hodge similarity of multiplier $2$ from $H^2(S,\mathbb Q)^{\tau=-1}$ onto $H^2(S^\vee,\mathbb Q)^{\tau=-1}$, hence from $T(S)_{\mathbb Q}$ onto $T(S^\vee)_{\mathbb Q}$.
3. If $S$ is very general, then $\operatorname{End}_{\mathrm{Hdg}}T(S)_{\mathbb Q}=\mathbb Q$, and $T(S)_{\mathbb Q}$ and $T(S^\vee)_{\mathbb Q}$ are not Hodge isometric. In particular $S$ and $S^\vee$ are not twisted derived equivalent. (That they are generically not isomorphic and not derived equivalent is [5, Cor. 3.10, Cor. 3.12], proved there with integral lattice theory.)
4. If $a=0$, then $S^\vee\cong S$. In that case $\iota(u)=iu$ makes $\operatorname{End}_{\mathrm{Hdg}}T(S)_{\mathbb Q}$ contain $\mathbb Q(i)$, and after this isomorphism the similarity is $1+\iota^*$, that is, multiplication by $1+i$.

Parts 1 and 3 contradict [9, proof of Prop. 3.15 in arXiv v1] and [10, Section 7.3], which state that the fixed surface of $\tau^{[2]}\circ\beta$ is isomorphic to $S$. The gap is a count: a point $P\in S$ lies in two cross pairs, $\{P,Q\}$ and $\{P,\tau Q\}$, so "$P\mapsto\{P,Q\}$" is a correspondence of degree $2$, not a birational map. The lattice-theoretic conclusions of [9, Prop. 3.15] still hold, because $S^\vee$ belongs to the same family as $S$. More generally, the fixed surface $F_X$ and the surface $V_X$ with $X=M_v(V_X,\beta)$ never have Hodge isometric rational transcendental lattices for very general $X$ in any of the families of [10, Table (6)]. This follows from [3, Prop. 2.1], as in the proof of [3, Thm. 1.1] with $V_X$ in place of $S_1$, or from [10, Thm. 1.2] itself (Section 4), and it answers [10, Remark 6.9]. The paper [3] did not notice that its Theorem 1.1, applied to $X=S^{[2]}$ with $S_1=S$, contradicts [9, Prop. 3.15].

**Theorem B.** Let $S\subset\mathbb P^3$ be a smooth quartic surface and $p\notin S$. The monodromy group of the projection $S\to\mathbb P^2$ from $p$ is $S_4$, $D_4$ or $C_4$. The surface $\Sigma_p\subset S^{[2]}$ of pairs of points of $S$ on lines through $p$ is reducible if and only if the group is $D_4$ or $C_4$, that is, if and only if $p$ is a quasi-Galois point. Then $\Sigma_p=Y\cup F$ with $F\cong S^\vee$.

**Theorem C.** Let $S$ be a K3 surface with a non-symplectic involution $\tau$ such that $Y=S/\tau$ is a weak del Pezzo surface, and let $g\neq\mathrm{id}$ be an involution of $Y$ such that the branch curve $C$ and $g(C)$ have no common component. If the bigonal partner of $S\to Y\to Y/g$ (Section 6) is birational to a K3 surface, then $K_Y^2\le2$, so $\rho(S)\ge8$. If $K_Y^2=2$, then $g$ is the Geiser involution, and if moreover $-K_Y$ is ample, then $S=S_{a,b}$ and the partner is $S^\vee$. Without the weak del Pezzo hypothesis the conclusion $\rho(S)\ge8$ still holds, except possibly when $g$ permutes the fibres of a conic bundle on $Y$ (Proposition 6.2).

So the pair surfaces of quartics, and the bigonal construction over weak del Pezzo surfaces, give K3 partners only at $\rho\ge8$, that is, in families of dimension at most $12$. Section 7 discusses what this means for the Hodge conjecture.

**What is new.** The duality, and the facts that $S$ and $S^\vee$ are generically neither isomorphic nor derived equivalent, are due to Menet [5, Cor. 3.9, 3.10, 3.12]. The splitting $\Sigma_p=Y\cup F$ is [8, Lemma 5.3], and Menet recalls it [5, Prop. 1.6] without identifying the K3 component. The normal form $u^4+au^2+b$ and the link with type $(8,8,1)$ are [4]. That $F$ is not Hodge isometric to $S$ for very general $S$ follows from [3, Prop. 2.1], and [3, Thm. 1.1] produces a multiplier-$2$ cycle on $S\times F$ by Markman's theorem. The identity behind the multiplier is the classical composition-by-two identity for dihedral double towers [11, Sec. 7.1], which [12, Sec. 5.1] applies to cohomology of surface quotients; Proposition 3.1 is an instance of it. The following is new as far as we know: the identification $F\cong S^\vee$ of the fixed surface with Menet's dual, the explicit correspondence $\Gamma$ on $S\times S^\vee$ and the resulting rational non-isometry, which rules out twisted derived equivalence, the observation that the identification $F\cong S$ in [9, 10] fails and where its proof breaks, the exclusion of $A_4$ and $V_4$ in Theorem B, and Theorem C with Proposition 6.2. Section 4.3 also corrects a sentence of [3]: at Hilbert squares with a natural involution $\iota_S^{[2]}$ one has $\rho(X)\ge10$, and [3] concluded that the twelfth dimension of its families comes from leaving the locus of Hilbert schemes. That conclusion overlooks non-natural involutions such as $\tau^{[2]}\circ\beta$. The family above consists of Hilbert squares $S^{[2]}$ with $\rho(S^{[2]})=9$, and it is $12$-dimensional.

## 2. The bigonal dual

Throughout, $a$ and $b$ are forms of degree $2$ and $4$ in $x=(x,y,z)$, and
$$S=S_{a,b}=\{F=u^4+a\,u^2+b=0\}\subset\mathbb P^3_{(u:x)},\qquad p=(1:0:0:0)\notin S.$$
The projection from $p$ is $\varphi\colon S\to\mathbb P^2$, $(u:x)\mapsto x$. Since $p\notin S$, every point of $S$ has $x\ne0$, and we may view $S$ inside the total space of $\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^2}(1)$, with $u$ as the fibre coordinate on the line $\ell_x=\overline{px}$. Put $\Delta_1=\{a^2-4b=0\}$ and $\Delta_2=\{b=0\}$, plane quartics.

**Lemma 2.1.** $S_{a,b}$ is smooth if and only if $\Delta_1$ and $\Delta_2$ are smooth curves. In particular $S_{a,b}$ is smooth if and only if $S_{a,b}^\vee=S_{2a,a^2-4b}$ is.

*Proof.* We have $\partial_uF=2u(2u^2+a)$ and $\partial_iF=a_iu^2+b_i$, where $a_i,b_i$ are the partial derivatives in the variables $x,y,z$. Let $(u:x)$ be a singular point of $S$. If $u=0$, then $b(x)=0$ and $b_i(x)=0$, so $x$ is a singular point of $\Delta_2$. If $u\ne0$, then $u^2=-a(x)/2$ and $a(x)\neq0$; substituting, $F=b-a^2/4$ and $\partial_iF=b_i-a\,a_i/2=-\tfrac14\partial_i(a^2-4b)$, so $x$ is a singular point of $\Delta_1$. Conversely, a singular point $x$ of $\Delta_2$ gives the singular point $(0:x)$ of $S$. Let $x$ be a singular point of $\Delta_1$. If $a(x)\ne0$, then $(u:x)$ with $u^2=-a(x)/2$ is a singular point of $S$. If $a(x)=0$, then $b(x)=0$ and $\nabla(a^2-4b)=-4\nabla b$ at $x$, so $x$ is a singular point of $\Delta_2$. For $S_{2a,a^2-4b}$ the two curves are $\{4a^2-4(a^2-4b)=0\}=\Delta_2$ and $\{a^2-4b=0\}=\Delta_1$. $\square$

The curves $\Delta_1$ and $\Delta_2$ meet exactly at the points of $\{a=b=0\}$, with local intersection number $I_x(a^2-4b,b)=I_x(a^2,b)=2\,I_x(a,b)$. For general $(a,b)$ these are eight points, at each of which $\Delta_1$ and $\Delta_2$ are tangent.

**The tower.** The reflection $\tau(u)=-u$ preserves $S$. Put $w=2u^2+a$. Then $w^2=4F+a^2-4b$, so $\varphi$ factors as
$$S\xrightarrow{\ \pi\ }Y=\{w^2=a^2-4b\}\xrightarrow{\ \kappa\ }\mathbb P^2,$$
where $Y=S/\tau$ is the del Pezzo surface of degree $2$ branched along $\Delta_1$ (smooth by Lemma 2.1), $\kappa$ is its anticanonical map, and $g\colon w\mapsto-w$ is its Geiser involution. The fixed curve of $\tau$ is $\{u=0\}\cap S\cong\Delta_2$, of genus $3$, and its image in $Y$ is $C=\{w=a\}\in|{-2K_Y}|$. Since $g(C)=\{w=-a\}$, we have $g(C)=C$ only if $a=0$. The same construction for $S^\vee$ gives the double plane $Y^\vee=\{w'^2=16b\}$, branched along $\Delta_2$, with $C^\vee\cong\Delta_1$.

Conversely, let $Y$ be a smooth del Pezzo surface of degree $2$ and $C\in|{-2K_Y}|$ a smooth curve with $g(C)\ne C$. Write $Y=\{w^2=q\}$. Since $H^0(Y,-2K_Y)=\kappa^*H^0(\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^2}(2))\oplus\mathbb C\,w$ and $C$ is not $g$-invariant, $C=\{w=a\}$ for a quadric $a$, after scaling. The double cover of $Y$ branched along $C$ is $\{t^2=w-a\}$, that is, $t^4+2a\,t^2+a^2-q=0$, which is $S_{2a,a^2-q}$. So every such double cover is a quartic $S_{a',b'}$, and $(Y,C)$ determines $S$.

**Proposition 2.2.** Let $S=S_{a,b}$ be smooth, and let $V\subset S^{[2]}$ be the closed subscheme of length-two subschemes contained in a fibre of $\varphi$. Then $V=\Sigma_0\cup F$, where $\Sigma_0\cong Y$ is the surface of $\tau$-invariant pairs and $F\cong S^\vee$. The isomorphism $F\cong S^\vee$ sends a subscheme to the sum of its $u$-coordinates; on a cross pair it is $\{(u_1:x),(u_2:x)\}\mapsto(u_1+u_2:x)$.

*Proof.* A length-two subscheme of the affine line $\ell_x\setminus\{p\}$ is the zero scheme of a monic quadratic $u^2-s\,u+r$. So the relative Hilbert scheme of length-two subschemes of the fibres of $\operatorname{Tot}\mathcal O(1)\to\mathbb P^2$ is $\operatorname{Tot}(\mathcal O(1)\oplus\mathcal O(2))$, with coordinates $(s,r)$, and $V$ is the closed subscheme where $u^2-su+r$ divides $F$. Division with remainder gives
$$F\equiv s\,(s^2-2r+a)\,u+\bigl(r^2-r\,s^2-a\,r+b\bigr)\pmod{u^2-su+r}.$$
So $V=\{s(s^2-2r+a)=0,\ r^2-rs^2-ar+b=0\}$. The part $s=0$ is $\{r^2-ar+b=0\}$, the double plane of pairs $\{(\pm u_0:x)\}$ with $u_0^2=-r$, branched where $a^2-4b=0$; this is $\Sigma_0\cong Y$. On the part $2r=s^2+a$, the second equation becomes $b-r^2=0$, that is $(s^2+a)^2=4b$, or $s^4+2as^2+a^2-4b=0$. So this part is the graph of $r=(s^2+a)/2$ over $S^\vee\subset\operatorname{Tot}\mathcal O(1)$, and the projection $(s,r)\mapsto s$ is an isomorphism onto $S^\vee$. Here we used that the relative Hilbert scheme of $\varphi$ maps to $S^{[2]}$ by a proper monomorphism, hence by a closed embedding, with image $V$. $\square$

The two components meet over $\Delta_1$, where $u_1^2=u_2^2$ and a cross pair becomes a $\tau$-invariant pair. Over a general point $x$, the fibre of $\varphi$ is $\{\pm u_1,\pm u_2\}$ with $u_1^2+u_2^2=-a$ and $u_1^2u_2^2=b$, and the four cross pairs give the four roots $\pm u_1\pm u_2$ of $s^4+2as^2+a^2-4b$. The proof also shows directly that every point of $S^\vee$ gives a subscheme of $S$, with no closure needed. Proposition 2.2 makes [8, Lemma 5.3] explicit. There the fixed locus of the symplectic involution $\iota_1=\tau^{[2]}\circ\beta$ of $S^{[2]}$ (biregular when $S$ contains no line) is shown to be a nonsingular irreducible surface $\Sigma$ together with $28$ points, and the proof identifies $\Sigma$ as the surface of cross pairs, a four-sheeted cover of $\mathbb P^2$, with $\Sigma\cup\Sigma_0$ the surface of all pairs on vertical lines. The same surface is the fixed surface of [9, Prop. 3.15]. Indeed, for a cross pair $\{P,Q\}$ on $\ell_x$, Beauville's involution $\beta$ gives the residual pair $\{\tau P,\tau Q\}$, and $\tau^{[2]}$ returns $\{P,Q\}$.

**Duality and the group $D_4$.** Label the roots of $F(\cdot,x)$ as $1,2,3,4=u_1,-u_1,u_2,-u_2$. The monodromy group lies in the stabilizer $D_4=\langle(12),(34),(13)(24)\rangle$ of the blocks $\{1,2\},\{3,4\}$, and equals it for general $(a,b)$ (Section 5). Let $W\to\mathbb P^2$ be the Galois closure. The stabilizer of the root $1$ is $\langle(34)\rangle$, and the stabilizer of the cross pair $\{1,3\}$ is $\langle(13)(24)\rangle$. These are reflections of the square in the two non-conjugate classes, so
$$S\sim W/\langle(34)\rangle,\qquad S^\vee\sim W/\langle(13)(24)\rangle$$
(birationally), and an outer automorphism of $D_4$ exchanges the two classes. This is the bigonal construction [6, 7] for the tower $S\to Y\to\mathbb P^2$. The intermediate double covers are $Y=W/\langle(12),(34)\rangle$ and $Y^\vee=W/\langle(13)(24),(14)(23)\rangle$. The third one, $W/\langle(12)(34),(1324)\rangle$, is the double plane $\{v^2=b(a^2-4b)\}$, branched along $\Delta_1\cup\Delta_2$, since $\sqrt{b}\,\sqrt{a^2-4b}=u_1u_2(u_1^2-u_2^2)$ is invariant under $C_4=\langle(1324)\rangle$.

*Check by point counts.* The script `bigonal_counts.py` verifies $\#S(\mathbb F_q)-\#Y(\mathbb F_q)=\#S^\vee(\mathbb F_q)-\#Y^\vee(\mathbb F_q)$ for primes $11\le q\le31$ and three random $(a,b)$ each, $21$ of $21$ cases, and finds that the same identity fails for the wrong partner $S_{a,a^2-4b}$ in $21$ of $21$ cases. At unramified points of $\mathbb P^2(\mathbb F_q)$ the identity is the character identity $\operatorname{Ind}_{\langle(34)\rangle}^{D_4}1-\operatorname{Ind}_{V}^{D_4}1=\operatorname{Ind}_{\langle(13)(24)\rangle}^{D_4}1-\operatorname{Ind}_{V'}^{D_4}1$, both sides being the two-dimensional irreducible character, with $V=\langle(12),(34)\rangle$ and $V'=\langle(13)(24),(14)(23)\rangle$. It tests the model of Proposition 2.2, not the multiplier.

## 3. The incidence correspondence and the multiplier

Let $\Gamma\subset S\times S^\vee$ be the incidence correspondence of Proposition 2.2: a point $(s:x)$ of $S^\vee$ corresponds to the subscheme $Z_s=\{u^2-su+\tfrac12(s^2+a)=0\}$ of $\ell_x\cap S$, and $\Gamma=\{(P,s):P\in Z_s\}$. In the coordinates $(u:s:x)$ of $\mathbb P^4$,
$$\Gamma=\{F(u,x)=0,\ (s-u)^2+u^2+a=0\}=\{F^\vee(s,x)=0,\ (s-u)^2+u^2+a=0\},$$
where $F^\vee=s^4+2as^2+a^2-4b$. The two descriptions agree because $F\equiv-\tfrac14F^\vee$ modulo the quadric, by the remainder formula in the proof of Proposition 2.2. For a point $P=(u_1:x)$ of $S$, the quadric gives $s-u_1=\pm u_2$, with $u_2^2=-a-u_1^2$; for a point $s$ of $S^\vee$ it gives the two roots of $u^2-su+\frac12(s^2+a)$. So both projections $\Gamma\to S$ and $\Gamma\to S^\vee$ are finite of degree $2$. A general point of $\Gamma$ is an ordered pair $(u_1,u_2)$ of roots in different blocks. When the monodromy group is $D_4$, it acts simply transitively on these eight ordered pairs, so $\Gamma$ is irreducible and birational to the Galois closure $W$ of Section 2.

The involutions $\tau$ of $S$ and $\tau^\vee\colon s\mapsto-s$ of $S^\vee$ satisfy $(\tau\times\tau^\vee)(\Gamma)=\Gamma$, since $Z_{-s}=\tau(Z_s)$. Write $H^2(S,\mathbb Q)^\pm$ for the eigenspaces of $\tau^*$. Then $H^2(S,\mathbb Q)^+=\pi^*H^2(Y,\mathbb Q)$ has rank $8$ and consists of algebraic classes, so $T(S)_{\mathbb Q}\subset H^2(S,\mathbb Q)^-$, which has rank $14$. The same holds for $S^\vee$.

**Proposition 3.1.** As cycles on $S\times S$,
$$\Gamma^t\circ\Gamma=2\,\Delta_S+(\pi\times\pi)^*\Gamma_g,$$
where $\Gamma_g\subset Y\times Y$ is the graph of the Geiser involution. Consequently $\Gamma^t_*\Gamma_*=2$ on $H^2(S,\mathbb Q)^-$, and
$$\Gamma_*x\cdot\Gamma_*y=2\,(x\cdot y)\qquad(x,y\in H^2(S,\mathbb Q)^-).$$
The map $\Gamma_*$ is an isomorphism of Hodge structures $H^2(S,\mathbb Q)^-\to H^2(S^\vee,\mathbb Q)^-$ and restricts to a Hodge similarity $T(S)_{\mathbb Q}\to T(S^\vee)_{\mathbb Q}$ of multiplier $2$.

*Proof.* The composition is the push-forward to $S\times S$ of $\Gamma\times_{S^\vee}\Gamma=\{(P,s,P'):P,P'\in Z_s\}$. This is the intersection of $\Gamma\times S$ and $S\times\Gamma^t$ in $S\times S^\vee\times S$, of the expected dimension $2$. Over the open set where $\Gamma\to S^\vee$ is étale, the fibre product is étale over $\Gamma$, hence reduced, so the intersection multiplicities of its components are $1$. It is the union of a diagonal part and an off-diagonal part. The diagonal part $\{P=P'\}$ maps to $\Delta_S$ with degree $2$, because each $P$ lies in two subschemes $Z_s$. The off-diagonal part consists of the triples with $\{P,P'\}=Z_s$; it is birational to $\Gamma$ via $(P,s,P')\mapsto(P,s)$, so it is irreducible when $a\ne0$ (the monodromy is then $D_4$, see above and Section 5), and for $a=0$ it splits into two components, the graphs of $\iota^{\pm1}$ of Remark 3.3. The argument uses only the decomposition into the two parts. The off-diagonal part maps birationally onto the closure $R$ of $\{(P,P'):P'\in\ell_x\cap S,\ P'\notin\{P,\tau P\}\}$, because $s=u(P)+u(P')$ is determined by $(P,P')$. Over a general $x$, with fibre $\{\pm u_1,\pm u_2\}$, the points of $R$ over $(u_1:x)$ are $(\pm u_2:x)$, which are the two points of $S$ over $g(\pi(u_1:x))$. So $R=(\pi\times\pi)^{-1}(\Gamma_g)$, and since $\pi\times\pi$ is étale over a general point of $\Gamma_g$, $R=(\pi\times\pi)^*\Gamma_g$ as cycles.

For $x\in H^2(S,\mathbb Q)$, $((\pi\times\pi)^*\Gamma_g)_*x=\pi^*g_*\pi_*x$. If $\tau^*x=-x$, then $\pi_*x=\pi_*\tau^*x=-\pi_*x$, so $\pi_*x=0$. Hence $\Gamma^t_*\Gamma_*x=2x$ on $H^2(S,\mathbb Q)^-$, and $\Gamma_*x\cdot\Gamma_*y=x\cdot\Gamma^t_*\Gamma_*y=2\,x\cdot y$, because $\Gamma^t_*$ is the adjoint of $\Gamma_*$. Since $\Gamma$ is $(\tau\times\tau^\vee)$-invariant, $\Gamma_*$ maps $H^2(S,\mathbb Q)^-$ into $H^2(S^\vee,\mathbb Q)^-$; it is injective, and both spaces have rank $14$, so it is bijective. As a correspondence it preserves Hodge structures. It maps $\operatorname{NS}(S)$ into $\operatorname{NS}(S^\vee)$, and $\Gamma^t_*$ maps $\operatorname{NS}(S^\vee)$ into $\operatorname{NS}(S)$, so $\Gamma_*$ maps $T(S)_{\mathbb Q}=\operatorname{NS}(S)_{\mathbb Q}^\perp$ into $T(S^\vee)_{\mathbb Q}$, and $\Gamma^t_*$ maps $T(S^\vee)_{\mathbb Q}$ into $T(S)_{\mathbb Q}$. On $H^2(S^\vee,\mathbb Q)^-$ we have $\Gamma^t_*=2\,\Gamma_*^{-1}$, so $\Gamma_*^{-1}$ maps $T(S^\vee)_{\mathbb Q}$ into $T(S)_{\mathbb Q}$, and $\Gamma_*(T(S)_{\mathbb Q})=T(S^\vee)_{\mathbb Q}$. $\square$

*Remark (source of the identity).* On the Galois closure $W$, $S=W/\langle(34)\rangle$ and $S^\vee=W/\langle(13)(24)\rangle$ are quotients by adjacent reflections, and $\tau$ is induced by the central involution $(12)(34)$. So $H^2(S,\mathbb Q)^-$ is a sum of copies of the two-dimensional irreducible representation of $D_4$, and $\Gamma^t_*\Gamma_*=2$ there is the group-algebra identity $e_Ke_Pe_K=\tfrac12e_K$ for the idempotents of adjacent reflections. For curves this is the bigonal isogeny of Pryms [11, Thm. 7.1(iii)]; for surface quotients it is [12, (5.1)–(5.2)]. The proof above is a cycle-level version, which also identifies the correction term $(\pi\times\pi)^*\Gamma_g$.

*Symmetry.* The roots of $F^\vee(\cdot,x)$ are $\pm s_1,\pm s_2$ with $s_1=u_1+u_2$ and $s_2=u_1-u_2$. The two cross pairs of $S^\vee$ containing $s_1$ are $\{s_1,\pm s_2\}$, with sums $2u_1$ and $2u_2$. Under the isomorphism $(S^\vee)^\vee=S_{4a,16b}\cong S$, $(t:x)\mapsto(t/2:x)$, the incidence correspondence of $S^\vee$ is therefore $\Gamma^t$. So Proposition 3.1 applied to $S^\vee$ gives $\Gamma_*\Gamma^t_*=2$ on $H^2(S^\vee,\mathbb Q)^-$, and duality is an involution on the level of correspondences as well.

*Remark 3.2 (an explicit class).* Proposition 3.1 can be checked on a single algebraic class. Take $b=-m^4-a\,m^2+z\,c$, with $m$ a linear form and $c$ a cubic. Then $S$ contains the lines $L=\{u=m,\ z=0\}$ and $\tau L=\{u=-m,\ z=0\}$, which meet in one point, so $(L-\tau L)^2=-2-2-2=-6$, and $L-\tau L\in H^2(S,\mathbb Q)^-$. A point $(m:x)$ of $L$ has the cross partners $(\pm u_2:x)$ with $u_2^2=-a-m^2$, so $\Gamma_*L$ is the conic $D_L=\{z=0,\ (s-m)^2+m^2+a=0\}$, with multiplicity one because a general $s\in D_L$ determines the point of $L$ in $Z_s$, and $\Gamma_*\tau L$ is $D_{\tau L}=\{z=0,\ (s+m)^2+m^2+a=0\}$. Indeed
$$F^\vee|_{z=0}=(s^2+a)^2+4m^2(m^2+a)=\bigl((s-m)^2+m^2+a\bigr)\bigl((s+m)^2+m^2+a\bigr).$$
If both conics are smooth, they are smooth rational curves on the K3 surface $S^\vee$, so $D_L^2=-2$, and $D_L\cdot D_{\tau L}=D_L\cdot(H-D_L)=2+2=4$, where $H$ is the plane class. Hence $(D_L-D_{\tau L})^2=-2-2-8=-12=2\cdot(-6)$, as Proposition 3.1 predicts. The script `line_check.py` verifies, for a random choice of $(a,m,c)$ over $\mathbb Q$, that $S$ and $S^\vee$ are smooth (Gröbner bases on four affine charts) and that both conics are smooth.

*Remark 3.3 ($a=0$).* If $a=0$, then $F^\vee=s^4-4b$, and $h\colon S^\vee\to S$, $(s:x)\mapsto(s/(1+i):x)$, is an isomorphism, since $(1+i)^4=-4$. The surface $S=\{u^4+b=0\}$ has the automorphism $\iota\colon u\mapsto iu$, with $\iota^2=\tau$. The roots over $x$ are $\pm u_1,\pm iu_1$, so the cross partners of $u_1$ are $\pm iu_1$, and $\Gamma$ sends $(u_1:x)$ to $s=(1\pm i)u_1$. Hence $h\circ\Gamma=\Delta_S+\Gamma_{\iota^{-1}}$ as correspondences, and $h_*\Gamma_*=1+\iota^*$ on $H^2(S,\mathbb Q)$. On $H^2(S,\mathbb Q)^-$, $J=\iota^*$ satisfies $J^2=\tau^*=-1$ and $J^t=J^{-1}=-J$, so $(1+J)^t(1+J)=2$, in agreement with Proposition 3.1. So on the $6$-dimensional locus $a=0$ the similarity is $1+J$ with $J^2=-1$, that is, "multiplication by $1+i$" for the action of $\mathbb Q(i)\subseteq\operatorname{End}_{\mathrm{Hdg}}T(S)_{\mathbb Q}$; the multiplier $2$ is a norm from $\mathbb Q(i)$. Section 4 shows that $S^\vee\not\cong S$ on the $12$-dimensional family.

## 4. Consequences and corrections

### 4.1. Very general members

Let $M=\langle2\rangle\oplus\langle-2\rangle^{\oplus7}$. For smooth $S=S_{a,b}$, $\pi^*\colon\operatorname{NS}(Y)(2)\to\operatorname{NS}(S)$ is a primitive embedding of $M$, and $T(S)\subseteq M^\perp$, which has signature $(2,12)$. By [4, Main Theorem (1)], the surfaces $S_{a,b}$ include the general K3 surface with a non-symplectic involution of type $(8,8,1)$, so the family has $12$-dimensional moduli and, by local Torelli, its period map has open image in the $12$-dimensional period domain $\mathcal D_M\subset\mathbb P(M^\perp\otimes\mathbb C)$. The lattice $\pi^*H^2(Y,\mathbb Z)$ equals $H^2(S,\mathbb Z)^{\tau}$, since both have rank $8$ and discriminant $2^8$, so it is primitive.

**Lemma 4.1.** For $S=S_{a,b}$ outside a countable union of proper closed analytic subsets of the parameter space, $\operatorname{NS}(S)=M$ and $\operatorname{End}_{\mathrm{Hdg}}T(S)_{\mathbb Q}=\mathbb Q$.

*Proof.* The period $\omega$ of $S$ lies in $\mathcal D_M$. If $\operatorname{NS}(S)\ne M$, then $\omega\perp\lambda$ for some $\lambda\in M^\perp\setminus\{0\}$. If $\operatorname{NS}(S)=M$ and $\varphi\in\operatorname{End}_{\mathrm{Hdg}}T(S)_{\mathbb Q}$, then $\varphi\in\operatorname{End}(M^\perp_{\mathbb Q})$ and $\omega$ is an eigenvector of $\varphi$; if $\varphi\notin\mathbb Q$, the eigenvectors of $\varphi$ form a finite union of proper linear subspaces of $\mathbb P(M^\perp\otimes\mathbb C)$. There are countably many $\lambda$ and $\varphi$, and each condition cuts $\mathcal D_M$ in a proper analytic subset, because $\mathcal D_M$ is open in a nondegenerate quadric and so is not contained in a proper linear subspace. The period map of the family is locally holomorphic with open image, and the parameter space of smooth $(a,b)$ is connected, so the preimage of each such subset is a proper closed analytic subset of the parameter space. $\square$

**Proposition 4.2 (Theorem A(3)).** Let $S$ be as in Lemma 4.1. Then $T(S)_{\mathbb Q}$ and $T(S^\vee)_{\mathbb Q}$ are not Hodge isometric. In particular $S\not\cong S^\vee$, and $S$ and $S^\vee$ are not derived equivalent, nor twisted derived equivalent. The first two consequences are [5, Cor. 3.10, Cor. 3.12]; the argument below recovers them and adds the third.

*Proof.* Let $\psi\colon T(S^\vee)_{\mathbb Q}\to T(S)_{\mathbb Q}$ be a Hodge isometry. Then $\psi\circ\Gamma_*$ is a Hodge endomorphism of $T(S)_{\mathbb Q}$, hence a scalar $r\in\mathbb Q$, and Proposition 3.1 gives $r^2=2$. This is impossible. An isomorphism $S^\vee\cong S$ induces a Hodge isometry. A derived equivalence induces a Hodge isometry $T(S)\cong T(S^\vee)$ [13]. A twisted derived equivalence $D^b(S,\alpha)\cong D^b(S^\vee,\alpha')$ induces a Hodge isometry $T(S,\alpha)\cong T(S^\vee,\alpha')$ of the twisted transcendental lattices [14, 15], which are sublattices of finite index of $T(S)$ and $T(S^\vee)$, and so a rational Hodge isometry $T(S)_{\mathbb Q}\cong T(S^\vee)_{\mathbb Q}$. $\square$

As abstract lattices, $T(S)$ and $T(S^\vee)$ are isometric, since both are $2$-elementary of signature $(2,12)$ with $\delta=1$ and discriminant group $(\mathbb Z/2)^8$ [9, Prop. 3.15]. So the obstruction in Proposition 4.2 lies in the Hodge structure, not in the lattice. Together with Proposition 3.1, this abstract isometry gives $T(S)_{\mathbb Q}(2)\cong T(S)_{\mathbb Q}$ as quadratic spaces, so there is no obstruction at the level of rational quadratic forms either.

For very general $S$ every Hodge class in $H^4(S\times S^\vee,\mathbb Q)$ is algebraic. Indeed $H^4(S\times S^\vee,\mathbb Q)$ is the sum of $H^0\otimes H^4$, $H^4\otimes H^0$ and $H^2\otimes H^2$. The cross terms $\operatorname{NS}\otimes T$ and $T\otimes\operatorname{NS}$ contain no Hodge classes, because $T_{\mathbb Q}$ has no rational classes of type $(1,1)$. So the Hodge classes in $H^2\otimes H^2$ are $\operatorname{NS}(S)_{\mathbb Q}\otimes\operatorname{NS}(S^\vee)_{\mathbb Q}$ plus the Hodge classes in $T(S)_{\mathbb Q}\otimes T(S^\vee)_{\mathbb Q}=\operatorname{Hom}(T(S)_{\mathbb Q},T(S^\vee)_{\mathbb Q})$, which form the line spanned by the class of $\Gamma_*|_{T(S)}$ since $\operatorname{End}_{\mathrm{Hdg}}T(S)_{\mathbb Q}=\mathbb Q$. The class of $\Gamma$ projects to this line with a nonzero coefficient. This is [3, Thm. 1.1] for this family, where the cycle came from Markman's theorem; here it is the explicit surface $\Gamma=\{F=0,\ (s-u)^2+u^2+a=0\}$.

### 4.2. The identification in [9] and [10]

[9, Prop. 3.15] (Prop. 3.14 in the published version) concerns $X=S_1^{[2]}$ for $S_1=S_{a,b}$ and $\sigma=\tau^{[2]}\circ\beta$. Its proof chooses, for $P\in S_1$, a point $Q$ with $r_P\cap S_1=\{P,\tau P,Q,\tau Q\}$, observes that $\{P,Q\}$ is fixed by $\sigma$, and concludes: "We determine a fixed point of $\sigma$ for each point $P\in S_1$ … So the fixed surface of $\sigma$ is parametrized by points in $S_1$ and thus it is birational to $S_1$" [9, arXiv v1, proof of Prop. 3.15]. But $\{P,\tau Q\}$ is a second fixed point with the same $P$. The assignment $P\mapsto\{P,Q\}$ is the correspondence $\Gamma$, of degree $2$ in both directions, not a rational map. By Proposition 4.2 the fixed surface, which is $S^\vee$, is not birational to $S_1$ for very general $S_1$. The statement is repeated in [10, Section 7.3]: "in [CGKK, Proposition 3.14] we proved that $S_2\simeq F$, and not just that they have the same Néron–Severi group".

What survives is the purpose of [9, Prop. 3.15], namely the lattice statement $T_F\cong T_Y$ of their Conjecture 3.13 in this case. Their proof deduces it from $T_F\cong T_{S_1}$, and this lattice isometry still holds, because $F\cong S^\vee$ is again a member of the family, so $T_F\cong T_{S_1}$ as abstract lattices. The general statement is [10, Thm. 1.2], proved by other means. The characterization [10, Thm. 5.14] (a K3 surface is a generalized Nikulin surface if and only if $\operatorname{NS}$ contains $E_7(-2)$ primitively) is not affected. For $F=S_{a,b}$, Proposition 2.2 exhibits a pair with fixed surface $F$ directly: $S_{a,b}\cong(S_{a,b}^\vee)^\vee$ is the fixed surface of $\tau^{[2]}\circ\beta$ on $(S_{a,b}^\vee)^{[2]}$.

### 4.3. The question of [10, Remark 6.9], and a correction to [3]

[10, Remark 6.9] attaches to a very general projective $X$ of $K3^{[2]}$-type with a symplectic involution $\iota$ two K3 surfaces: the fixed surface $F_X$, and a surface $V_X$ with $X\cong M_v(V_X,\beta)$, a moduli space of stable, possibly twisted, sheaves. It asks whether $F_X\cong V_X$, and states that this holds for the embedding $j_2$ and $d=2$, which is the case $X=S^{[2]}$, $V_X=S$ treated above.

**Proposition 4.3.** For very general $X$ in each of the families of [10, Table (6)], $T(F_X)_{\mathbb Q}$ is Hodge similar to $T(V_X)_{\mathbb Q}$ with multiplier $2$, and the two are not Hodge isometric. So $F_X$ and $V_X$ are neither isomorphic nor (twisted) derived equivalent.

*Proof.* The restriction $j^*\colon H^2(X,\mathbb Q)\to H^2(F_X,\mathbb Q)$ is a Hodge similarity $T(X)_{\mathbb Q}\to T(F_X)_{\mathbb Q}$ of multiplier $2$ [3, Prop. 2.1]. The same follows from [10]: Theorem 1.2 there gives a Hodge isometry $T(F_X)\cong T(Y)$ for the Nikulin orbifold $Y$, and $q_X(\pi^*a)=2\,q_Y(a)$ [10, proof of Thm. 3.1]. On the other hand $T(X)_{\mathbb Q}\cong T(V_X)_{\mathbb Q}$, because $T(M_v(V,\beta))$ is Hodge isometric to the twisted transcendental lattice $T(V,\beta)$, a sublattice of finite index in $T(V)$ [16]. For very general $X$ in a $12$-dimensional family, $\operatorname{End}_{\mathrm{Hdg}}T(X)_{\mathbb Q}=\mathbb Q$, by the argument of Lemma 4.1. Then the proof of Proposition 4.2 applies. $\square$

This is [3, Thm. 1.1] with $S_1=V_X$. At $X=S^{[2]}$ in the family $j_2$, $d=2$, the surface $F_X$ is $S^\vee$, so Theorem A makes the multiplier-$2$ cycle explicit there. In the other families of [10, Table (6)] we do not know an explicit model of $F_X$ in terms of $V_X$. Problem 8.6 of [10] asks a different question, and Proposition 4.3 does not address it.

The paper [3] says that at a Hilbert square $X=S^{[2]}$ with the natural involution induced by a Nikulin involution of $S$, $\rho(X)\ge10$, "so the twelfth dimension comes from moving $X$ off the locus of Hilbert schemes" [3, Section 1]. The first statement is correct, but the conclusion is not: in the family $j_2$, $d=2$, every $X$ is a Hilbert square $S^{[2]}$, the involution $\tau^{[2]}\circ\beta$ is not natural, and $\rho(X)=9$ for very general $S$ by Lemma 4.1. That paper also says that it knows no earlier $12$-dimensional family with an algebraic Hodge similarity of nonsquare multiplier. The cycle $\Gamma$, implicit in [8, Lemma 5.3] and [9, Prop. 3.15], is such an earlier example on the same family; it was not recognized as one, because the fixed surface was taken to be $S$.

## 5. Pair surfaces of quartics: proof of Theorem B

Let $S\subset\mathbb P^3$ be a smooth quartic surface and $p\notin S$. Choose coordinates with $p=(1:0:0:0)$, so $S=\{F=c_0u^4+c_1u^3+c_2u^2+c_3u+c_4=0\}$ with $c_i$ forms of degree $i$ in $x$ and $c_0\ne0$. The projection $\varphi\colon S\to\mathbb P^2$ from $p$ is finite of degree $4$, since no line through $p$ lies in $S$, and $\varphi^*\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^2}(1)=\mathcal O_S(1)$. Let $G\subseteq S_4$ be its monodromy group, the Galois group of the Galois closure of $\mathbb C(S)/\mathbb C(\mathbb P^2)$ acting on the four roots. It is transitive because $S$ is irreducible.

As in Proposition 2.2, let $V\subset S^{[2]}$ be the relative Hilbert scheme of length-two subschemes of the fibres of $\varphi$. It is cut out by two equations in the $4$-dimensional space $\operatorname{Tot}(\mathcal O(1)\oplus\mathcal O(2))$ of monic quadratics, so each of its components has dimension at least $2$, and it is finite over $\mathbb P^2$, because a length-four subscheme of a line has finitely many subschemes of length two. So every component of $V$ dominates $\mathbb P^2$, and the components of $V$ correspond to the $G$-orbits on the six two-element subsets of a general fibre. We write $\Sigma_p=V$.

The transitive subgroups of $S_4$ are, up to conjugacy, $S_4$, $A_4$, $D_4$, $C_4$ and the normal Klein group $V_4$. Their orbits on two-element subsets have sizes $6$ (for $S_4$ and $A_4$), $2+4$ (for $D_4$ and $C_4$), and $2+2+2$ (for $V_4$).

**Lemma 5.1.** Let $\theta\ne\mathrm{id}$ be an automorphism of $S$ with $\varphi\circ\theta=\varphi$ and $\theta^2=\mathrm{id}$. Then, after a change of the coordinate $u$ by a multiple of a linear form in $x$, $\theta$ is the reflection $u\mapsto-u$ and $S=S_{a,b}$ (up to scaling $F$). If $\theta$ has order $4$, $\varphi\circ\theta=\varphi$ and $\theta^2$ is the reflection $u\mapsto-u$, then $\theta(u)=\pm iu$ and $S=S_{0,b}$.

*Proof.* Since $\theta^*\varphi^*\mathcal O(1)\cong\varphi^*\mathcal O(1)$, $\theta^*$ acts on $H^0(\mathcal O_S(1))=H^0(\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^3}(1))$. We may normalize the linearization so that $\theta^*$ is the identity on $\varphi^*H^0(\mathcal O(1))=\langle x,y,z\rangle$; then $\theta^*u=\alpha u+l$ with $l$ linear in $x$. So $\theta$ is the restriction of the projective linear map $(u:x)\mapsto(\alpha u+l(x):x)$. If $\theta^2=\mathrm{id}$, then, since $S$ spans $\mathbb P^3$, this map squares to the identity, so $\alpha^2=1$ and $(\alpha+1)l=0$. If $\alpha=1$, then $l=0$ and $\theta=\mathrm{id}$. So $\alpha=-1$, and after $u\mapsto u-l/2$, $\theta(u)=-u$. Then $F(-u,x)=\pm F(u,x)$, and the sign is $+$ because $c_0\ne0$, so $F$ is even in $u$. If $\theta$ has order $4$ and $\theta^2(u)=-u$, write $\theta(u)=\beta u+l$; then $\beta^2=-1$ and $(\beta+1)l=0$, so $l=0$, and $F(iu,x)=F(u,x)$ forces $a=0$. $\square$

*Proof of Theorem B.* (i) $G\subseteq D_4$ (up to conjugacy) if and only if $S=S_{a,b}$ in suitable coordinates. A block system of two pairs corresponds to a field $K$ with $\mathbb C(\mathbb P^2)\subset K\subset\mathbb C(S)$ and $[\mathbb C(S):K]=2$. Let $Y$ be the normalization of $\mathbb P^2$ in $K$. Since $S$ is normal and finite over $\mathbb P^2$, it is the normalization of $Y$ in $\mathbb C(S)$, and the deck involution of $\mathbb C(S)/K$ extends to an automorphism $\theta$ of $S$ over $\mathbb P^2$. By Lemma 5.1, $S=S_{a,b}$. Conversely, for $S_{a,b}$ the roots are $\pm u_1,\pm u_2$ and $G$ preserves the blocks $\{\pm u_1\},\{\pm u_2\}$.

(ii) $G\ne V_4$. If $G=V_4$, then $\varphi$ is Galois with group $V_4$, and the three involutions of $V_4$ extend to automorphisms of $S$ over $\mathbb P^2$, as in (i). By Lemma 5.1 each has $\alpha=-1$. The product of two of them is the third and has $\alpha=(-1)^2=1$, so it is the identity by Lemma 5.1, a contradiction.

(iii) $G=C_4$ if and only if $a=0$. If $G=C_4$, a generator extends to an automorphism of order $4$ over $\mathbb P^2$ whose square is the involution of (i), and Lemma 5.1 gives $a=0$. If $a=0$, the roots are $i^ku_1$, and $G\subseteq C_4$; being transitive, $G=C_4$.

(iv) $G\ne A_4$. Suppose $G=A_4$. Since $\pi_1(\mathbb P^2\setminus\text{branch curve})$ is generated by meridians, $G$ is generated by the local monodromies around the components of the branch curve. These are non-trivial elements of $A_4$, so they have cycle type $(2,2)$ or $(3,1)$. Let $\delta$ be the union of the branch components of type $(2,2)$ and $B$ the union of those of type $(3,1)$. If $B$ were empty, all local monodromies would lie in the normal subgroup $V_4$, so $G\subseteq V_4$; hence $B\ne\emptyset$. Over $\delta$ there are two ramification points of index $2$, so $\varphi^*\delta=2\hat C$ for a reduced curve $\hat C$; over $B$ there is one point of index $3$ and one unramified point, so $\varphi^*B=3\hat B+B'$ with $\hat B\to B$ birational. The ramification divisor is $R=\hat C+2\hat B$, and $K_S=\varphi^*K_{\mathbb P^2}+R$ with $K_S=0$ gives $R\in|3h|$, where $h=c_1(\mathcal O_S(1))$. The class $h$ is primitive in $\operatorname{NS}(S)$, since $h^2=4$ and $\operatorname{NS}(S)$ is even. From $2\hat C=(\deg\delta)\,h$ we get that $\deg\delta$ is even and $\hat C=\frac{\deg\delta}2h$. Then $2\hat B=3h-\hat C=m\,h$ with $m=3-\frac{\deg\delta}2$, so $m$ is even and $\hat B=\frac m2h$. As $\hat B\ne0$, $m\ge1$, and $m\le3$, so $m=2$, $\deg\delta=2$ and $\hat C\in|h|$. So $\hat C=S\cap\Pi$ for a plane $\Pi$, and $\hat C\to\delta$ has degree $2$. If $p\in\Pi$, then $\varphi(\hat C)=\delta$ is a single line, while $\deg\delta=2$. If $p\notin\Pi$, then $\varphi|_\Pi\colon\Pi\to\mathbb P^2$ is an isomorphism, so $\hat C\to\delta$ is birational. Both contradict $\deg(\hat C\to\delta)=2$.

So $G\in\{S_4,D_4,C_4\}$. By the orbit count, $\Sigma_p$ is irreducible for $G=S_4$ and has two components for $G\in\{D_4,C_4\}$, which happens exactly when $S=S_{a,b}$ with centre $p$, that is, when $p$ is quasi-Galois [4, Prop. 3.1]. In that case $\Sigma_p=\Sigma_0\cup F$ with $\Sigma_0\cong Y$ and $F\cong S^\vee$ by Proposition 2.2. $\square$

*Check.* The script `galois_t5.py` computes, with SymPy, the Galois group over $\mathbb Q$ of the quartic in $t$ obtained by restricting the projection to $12$ random rational points of $\mathbb P^2$. Each such group is, up to conjugacy, a subgroup of the Galois group of the quartic over $\mathbb Q(\mathbb P^2)$, which contains the monodromy group as a normal subgroup. For a random integral quartic and $p=(1:0:0:0)$ all $12$ groups are $S_4$. For a random $S_{a,b}$ projected from its centre all $12$ are $D_4$, and projected from the points $(1:1:0:0)$ and $(1:2:0:0)$, which are not on $S$, all are $S_4$. This agrees with Theorem B and with the fact that a quasi-Galois point is the centre of the reflection.

## 6. Bigonal partners over weak del Pezzo surfaces: proof of Theorem C

Let $S$ be a K3 surface with a non-symplectic involution $\tau$ whose fixed locus $C$ is non-empty. Then $Y=S/\tau$ is a smooth rational surface, $\pi\colon S\to Y$ is branched along the smooth curve $C$, and $C\in|{-2K_Y}|$ because $K_S=\pi^*(K_Y+\frac12C)=0$. Since $\operatorname{Pic}(Y)$ is torsion-free, $S=\{t^2=s_C\}\subset\operatorname{Tot}(-K_Y)$, where $s_C\in H^0(Y,-2K_Y)$ defines $C$. Let $g\ne\mathrm{id}$ be an involution of $Y$ such that $C$ and $g(C)$ have no common component.

**The partner.** Let $W$ be the normalization of $S\times_{\pi,Y,g\circ\pi}S=\{(P,P'):\pi(P')=g(\pi(P))\}$. Transporting the second factor by $g$, $W=\{t_1^2=s_C,\ t_2^2=g^*s_C\}$, a bidouble cover of $Y$ with branch curves $D_1=C$, $D_2=g(C)$, $D_3=0$, and $L_1=L_2=-K_Y$, $L_3=-2K_Y$ in the notation of [17, Sec. 2]. Its Galois group $\langle\tau_1,\tau_2\rangle\cong(\mathbb Z/2)^2$ acts by $t_i\mapsto-t_i$. The swap $\iota(P,P')=(P',P)$ is an involution of $W$ over $g$, and $\iota\tau_1\iota=\tau_2$, so $\langle\tau_1,\tau_2,\iota\rangle\cong D_4$. We call a resolution $\tilde F$ of $W/\iota$ the *bigonal partner* of the tower $S\to Y\to Y/g$. For $Y$ a del Pezzo surface of degree $2$ and $g$ the Geiser involution, $S=S_{a,b}$ by Section 2, $W$ is the incidence correspondence $\Gamma$ of Section 3 (ordered cross pairs), and $W/\iota\cong S^\vee$ by Proposition 2.2.

Since $C$ and $g(C)$ are smooth without common components, $W$ has at most rational double points: locally $W=\{z_2^2=h(z_1^2,x)\}$, where $y$ is a local equation of $D_1$, $z_1^2=y$, and $h(y,x)$ is a local equation of $D_2$, and the preimage of the smooth curve $D_2$ under the double cover branched along $D_1$ has only $A_k$ singularities. So $p_g$ of a resolution of $W$ is $h^0(\omega_W)$, and by [17, Sec. 2],
$$H^0(\omega_W)=H^0(K_Y)\oplus H^0(K_Y+L_1)\oplus H^0(K_Y+L_2)\oplus H^0(K_Y+L_3)=0\oplus\mathbb C\oplus\mathbb C\oplus H^0(-K_Y),$$
where the summands are the eigenspaces of $\langle\tau_1,\tau_2\rangle$. So $p_g(W)=2+h^0(-K_Y)$. For a del Pezzo surface of degree $2$ this is $5$, which agrees with $\Gamma$ being a complete intersection of type $(2,4)$ in $\mathbb P^4$, with $K_\Gamma=\mathcal O(1)$.

**Lemma 6.1.** There is a sign $\varepsilon$ such that $p_g(\tilde F)=1+\dim\{\xi\in H^0(-K_Y):g^*\xi=\varepsilon\xi\}$, where $g^*$ is the natural action on $H^0(-K_Y)$.

*Proof.* The holomorphic $2$-forms on $\tilde F$ are the $\iota$-invariant $2$-forms on an $\iota$-equivariant resolution of $W$. The summands $H^0(K_Y+L_1)$ and $H^0(K_Y+L_2)$ are spanned by $\mathrm{pr}_1^*\sigma_S$ and $\mathrm{pr}_2^*\sigma_S$, which $\iota$ exchanges; they contribute one invariant form. The summand $H^0(K_Y+L_3)$ consists of the forms $\xi/z$ on the third intermediate cover $\{z^2=s_C\,g^*s_C\}$, $z=t_1t_2$, with $\xi\in H^0(K_Y\otimes L_3)=H^0(-K_Y)$. The section $s_C\,g^*s_C$ is $g$-invariant, and $\iota$ maps $z$ to $\pm$ its transport by $g$, so $\iota$ acts on this summand by $\pm g^*$; put $\varepsilon$ equal to this sign. $\square$

*Proof of Theorem C.* If $\tilde F$ is birational to a K3 surface, then $p_g(\tilde F)=1$, so $g^*$ has no eigenvector with eigenvalue $\varepsilon$ on $H^0(-K_Y)$. Since $g^*$ is an involution, $g^*=-\varepsilon$ is a scalar, and $g$ maps every member of $|{-K_Y}|$ to itself. Suppose $K_Y^2\ge3$. Then $|{-K_Y}|$ is base point free and defines a birational morphism $\phi$ onto the anticanonical model [18, V, Thm. 1], see [19, Rem. 2.2 and Thm. 2.3]. As $g^*$ is a scalar, $\phi\circ g=\phi$, so $g$ is the identity on the open set where $\phi$ is injective, hence $g=\mathrm{id}$, a contradiction. So $K_Y^2\le2$, and since $\pi^*\colon\operatorname{NS}(Y)\to\operatorname{NS}(S)$ is injective, $\rho(S)\ge\rho(Y)=10-K_Y^2\ge8$. If $K_Y^2=2$, then $\phi\colon Y\to\mathbb P^2$ is a morphism of degree $2$, the composite of the birational morphism to the anticanonical model, a quartic in $\mathbb P(1,1,1,2)$, with the projection to $\mathbb P^2$ [19, Sec. 2.1], $g$ preserves its fibres, and so $g$ is its deck involution, the Geiser involution. If moreover $-K_Y$ is ample, then $S=S_{a,b}$ by Section 2 and the partner is $S^\vee$. $\square$

For a del Pezzo surface of degree $2$ and the Geiser involution, $g^*=1$ on $H^0(-K_Y)=\kappa^*H^0(\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^2}(1))$, and since $S^\vee$ is a K3 surface, Lemma 6.1 forces $\varepsilon=-1$. In terms of $D_4$-characters, with the notation of Section 2: $W/\langle(12),(34)\rangle=Y$ and $W/\langle(13)(24),(14)(23)\rangle=Y^\vee$ are rational, and $W/C_4$ is the double plane branched along the octic $\Delta_1\cup\Delta_2$, with $p_g=3$. So $H^{2,0}(\Gamma)=\rho\oplus3\chi$, where $\rho$ is the two-dimensional irreducible character and $\chi$ the character with kernel $C_4$; this has dimension $5$. Each reflection fixes a line in $\rho$ and acts by $\chi=-1$, so $p_g(S)=p_g(S^\vee)=1$. If $K_Y^2=1$, the Bertini involution acts on the pencil $|{-K_Y}|$ trivially and is the natural candidate; then $\rho(S)\ge9$.

*Scope.* Theorem C assumes that $-K_Y$ is nef and big. This holds when the fixed locus of $\tau$ is a single curve of genus $h\ge2$, since then $K_Y^2=h-1>0$ and $-2K_Y=C$ is irreducible with positive square. The next proposition drops the hypothesis, at the cost of one exceptional configuration.

**Proposition 6.2.** Let $S,\tau,Y,g$ be as at the start of this section, without assuming that $Y$ is a weak del Pezzo surface, and suppose that the bigonal partner is birational to a K3 surface. If every fixed curve of $\tau$ has genus at most $1$, then $\rho(S)\ge\rho(Y)\ge10$. Otherwise the fixed locus is a curve of genus $h\ge2$ and $k$ smooth rational curves, $\rho(S)\ge\rho(Y)=11-h+k$, and $\rho(Y)\ge8$, except possibly when $g$ permutes the fibres of a conic bundle $Y\to\mathbb P^1$; in that case $S\to Y\to\mathbb P^1$ is an elliptic fibration.

*Proof.* The fixed locus is a disjoint union $R$ of smooth curves $R_j$, and $\pi^*C=2R$ gives $C^2=2R^2$, so $K_Y^2=\frac14C^2=\frac12\sum_jR_j^2$ and $\rho(Y)=10-K_Y^2$ by Noether's formula. If every $R_j$ has genus at most $1$, then $R_j^2\le0$, so $K_Y^2\le0$ and $\rho(Y)\ge10$. Otherwise some $R_j=C_h$ has genus $h\ge2$ and $C_h^2>0$; by the Hodge index theorem every other $R_j$ has negative square, so $R=C_h+\sum_{i=1}^kE_i$ with $E_i\cong\mathbb P^1$, and $K_Y^2=h-1-k$, that is, $\rho(Y)=11-h+k$. This is at least $9$ if $h=2$, so we may assume $h\ge3$.

Since $C\in|{-2K_Y}|$, $\pi^*(-K_Y)=R$, and $\pi_*\mathcal O_S=\mathcal O_Y\oplus\mathcal O_Y(K_Y)$ gives $h^0(Y,-K_Y)=h^0(S,R)-1$. Each $E_i$ satisfies $R\cdot E_i=-2$, so the $E_i$ are fixed components of $|R|$, and $h^0(S,R)=h^0(S,C_h)=h+1$. So $h^0(-K_Y)=h$. As in the proof of Theorem C, Lemma 6.1 makes $g^*$ a scalar on $H^0(-K_Y)$, so $g$ maps every member of $|{-K_Y}|$ to itself. Write $|{-K_Y}|=\Phi+|M|$ with fixed part $\Phi$. Then $g$ maps every member of $|M|$ to itself, and so preserves the fibres of $\phi_M\colon Y\dashrightarrow\mathbb P^{h-1}$. The images $E_i'=\pi(E_i)$ satisfy $-K_Y\cdot E_i'=R\cdot E_i=-2$, so they lie in $\Phi$, and $\pi^*E_i'=2E_i$. Hence $N=\pi^*M=R-\pi^*\Phi=C_h-Z$ with $Z\ge0$. Both $N$ and $C_h$ are nef, so $C_h^2-N^2=Z\cdot C_h+Z\cdot N\ge0$, and $M^2=\frac12N^2\le h-1$.

If the image $\Sigma$ of $\phi_M$ is a surface, it is non-degenerate in $\mathbb P^{h-1}$, so $\deg\Sigma\ge h-2$. The map $Y\dashrightarrow\Sigma$ has degree $d\ge2$, since otherwise $g$ is the identity on a dense open set. After resolving the base points of $|M|$, the moving part on the blow-up has square $d\deg\Sigma$, and this is at most $M^2$ because $M$ is nef. So $2(h-2)\le h-1$ and $h\le3$. Then $h=3$ and $\rho(Y)=8+k\ge8$.

If the image is a curve, it spans $\mathbb P^{h-1}$, so it has degree at least $h-1$. In the Stein factorization of $\phi_M$ the base curve is $\mathbb P^1$, because $q(Y)=0$, so $M\equiv eG$ for a fibre $G$ of a pencil without fixed part, with $e\ge h-1\ge2$. Since $G$ is nef, $0\le G^2\le(h-1)/e^2\le1/(h-1)<1$, so $G^2=0$, and $|G|$ is a base point free pencil $Y\to\mathbb P^1$. Since $g$ preserves the fibres of $\phi_M$, it permutes the fibres of this pencil. Since $-K_Y\cdot G=\Phi\cdot G\ge0$, adjunction gives $p_a(G)\le1$. If $p_a(G)=1$, then $K_Y\cdot G=0$ with $G^2=0$ and $G\not\equiv0$, so $K_Y^2\le0$ by the Hodge index theorem, and $\rho(Y)\ge10$. If $p_a(G)=0$, then $|G|$ is a conic bundle, $C\cdot G=-2K_Y\cdot G=4$, and the curves $\pi^{-1}(G)$ are double covers of $\mathbb P^1$ branched in four points, so they have genus $1$. $\square$

In the conic bundle case we do not know a bound on $\rho$. The natural source of such towers, translation by a $2$-torsion section of an elliptic K3 surface composed with $-1$, does not satisfy the hypotheses: the fixed curve of $-1$ contains the bisection of non-zero $2$-torsion points, which is mapped to itself by the translation, so $C$ and $g(C)$ have a common component.

## 7. Bearing on the Hodge conjecture

For very general $S$ in the $12$-dimensional family, the Hodge conjecture for $S\times S^\vee$ is known from [3, Thm. 1.1], whose proof goes through Markman's theorem on Hodge isometries of hyper-Kähler manifolds of $K3^{[n]}$-type. Theorem A gives the cycle directly: the surface $\Gamma=\{F=0,\ (s-u)^2+u^2+a=0\}\subset\mathbb P^4$, a complete intersection of type $(2,4)$, induces the similarity, and Section 4.1 shows that it spans the transcendental Hodge classes. This changes no Hodge-class status. It shows that the multiplier-$2$ similarity on this family comes from Menet's duality [5], a bigonal construction, through the dihedral identity of [11, 12], and it locates the error in [9, 10], where the same cycle was present but the fixed surface was identified with $S$, so that the multiplier $2$ went unnoticed.

The open problem behind this note is to find algebraic Hodge similarities of non-square multiplier between K3 surfaces with trivial endomorphism field in families of dimension greater than $12$, that is, at Picard number below $8$. Theorems B and C say where the bigonal mechanism stops. For pair surfaces of quartics, a reducible pair surface forces a reflection and $\rho\ge8$ (Theorem B). For towers $S\to Y\to Y/g$ over weak del Pezzo surfaces, a K3 partner forces $K_Y^2\le2$ and $\rho\ge8$ (Theorem C). Proposition 6.2 extends this to arbitrary $Y$, with one open case: $g$ permutes the fibres of a conic bundle on $Y$, so that $S$ is elliptic. When $g$ maps each fibre to itself, the tower is a family over $\mathbb P^1$ of bigonal constructions for genus-one curves. Also not covered is the irreducible pair surface of Theorem B for $G=S_4$, whose quotients by subgroups of $S_4$ we have not studied here.
