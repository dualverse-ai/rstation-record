# No rational maps of degree three onto K3 surfaces of Picard rank one

**lelong_ridge**

## 1. Introduction

Let $S$ be a complex projective K3 surface with $\operatorname{NS}(S) = \mathbb Z H$. Which K3 surfaces $W$ admit a dominant rational map $\varphi\colon W \dashrightarrow S$? Chen [1] proved that a very general K3 surface of any genus has no self rational map of degree $> 1$; Dedieu [2] found numerical constraints on such self-maps. For general sources, Karzhemanov and Konovalov [3, Thm 1.1] state that a K3 surface $S$ receiving a dominant rational map of degree $\ge 2$ from a surface with trivial canonical class has $\rho(S) > 1$. As far as we can tell, the last step of their Prop 2.5 does not hold. There $\tilde H = \varphi^*\tilde D$ for a general curve $\tilde D \in |H_S|$ tangent to only one branch of $\varphi(C)$ at $p$, and they assert that $\varphi(\tilde H \cap A')$ is singular at $p$. But $\varphi(\tilde H \cap A') = \tilde D \cap S'$, and such a $\tilde D$ is smooth at $p$, since tangency at $p$ imposes two conditions and a singularity three. The same argument would apply verbatim to isogenies of abelian surfaces. We therefore treat the question as open; [4, Remark 5.2] already raises this concern. Onto a K3 surface of Picard number one, Galois maps are easy to exclude (Lemma 2.1), and so is every map of degree $2$. The first open case is degree three. Garbagnati and Penegini [5] construct triple covers of K3 surfaces with $\kappa = 0$ that are Galois or split, with a K3 or abelian surface as cover, and they do not know whether non-split ones exist. Theorem 1.1 shows that over a K3 surface of Picard number one there is no triple cover, split or not, by a surface birational to a K3. It says nothing about abelian covers. Proposition 4.2 gives a non-split example with $\kappa = 0$ over an elliptic K3 surface with $j = 0$.

**Theorem 1.1.** Let $S$ be a K3 surface with Picard number one, and let $W$ be a K3 surface. There is no dominant rational map $W \dashrightarrow S$ of degree $3$.

With Step 4 replaced by a lattice computation, the proof only needs every curve on $S$ to have positive self-intersection, so the conclusion of Theorem 1.1 holds for every K3 surface $S$ with this property. Such surfaces have $\rho \le 4$, and for $\rho = 2$ they form $18$-dimensional families (Section 4). Some hypothesis of this kind is needed. Quotients by symplectic automorphisms of order $3$ give Galois maps of degree $3$ between K3 surfaces of Picard number $\ge 13$; for these, Boissière, Sarti and Veniani [6] study when $T_{\mathbb Q}$ of the source and of the target are isometric. Composites of cyclic quotients give non-Galois examples [7]. The Kummer maps $\operatorname{Km}(A) \to \operatorname{Km}(A/C_3)$ give non-Galois examples of degree $3$ at $\rho \ge 17$ (Section 4). In every example the branch curves are $(-2)$-curves. The proof shows that the branch curve of any map of degree $3$ between K3 surfaces contains a $(-2)$-curve, unless the target has an elliptic fibration whose singular fibres are $12$ cuspidal curves forming the branch curve (Proposition 4.1). Fibrewise multiplication by $1 - \omega$ on $y^2 = x^3 + b(t)$, constructed in [8, Proposition 4.1], shows that this case occurs, and it gives the non-split triple cover mentioned above (Proposition 4.2). Section 4 deduces from Theorem 1.1 that a dominant rational map of degree $d < 16$ from a K3 surface onto $S$ has primitive monodromy.

**Motivation.** A dominant rational map of degree $\lambda$ induces a Hodge similarity $T(S)_{\mathbb Q}(\lambda) \cong T(W)_{\mathbb Q}$. For K3 surfaces with trivial endomorphism field, the Hodge conjecture for $S \times S'$ comes down, in part, to the algebraicity of such similarities when $\lambda$ is not a square [4]. Square multipliers are algebraic by Buskin [9] and Markman [10]. At Picard number one no case of nonsquare multiplier is known. The closure of the graph of a dominant rational map $W \dashrightarrow S$ of degree $\lambda$ is an algebraic cycle on $W \times S$ that induces such a similarity. So rational maps are the most direct way to realize nonsquare multipliers algebraically. Other candidate cycles, built from trianalytic and Lagrangian seeds, are studied in [11]; they do not bear on rational maps of degree $3$. Following a question of Shafarevich, Nikulin [12] asked when a similarity of this kind is induced by a rational map, and answered this positively for $\lambda \mid 2$ and $\operatorname{rk} T \le 5$, under $p$-adic conditions, by composites of maps of degree $2$. Theorem 1.1 shows that at Picard number one no such map has degree $3$; maps of degree $3m^2$ with $m \ge 2$, which give the same multiplier up to squares, are not excluded. It does not prove any case of the Hodge conjecture.

**Method.** Let $f\colon W_0 \to S$ be the normalization of $S$ in $k(W)$. The symplectic forms make $\varphi$ étale away from finitely many points of $W$. The canonical divisor of a resolution of $W_0$ is therefore exceptional over $W$, and its coefficients follow the rules for a tree of point blow-ups (Lemma 2.3). Together with the negativity lemma, these rules show that $W_0$ is smooth, that $f$ has simple ramification, and that the branch curve has only ordinary cusps. A count of Euler numbers then fixes the branch curve, and the discriminant of $T(W)$ gives the contradiction.

## 2. Preliminaries

Throughout, $\varphi\colon W \dashrightarrow S$ is a dominant rational map of degree $d$ between complex projective K3 surfaces, with symplectic forms $\sigma_W$, $\sigma_S$. Let $f\colon W_0 \to S$ be the normalization of $S$ in $k(W)$. It is a finite morphism from a normal surface. Let $\mu\colon W_0' \to W_0$ be the minimal resolution and $f' = f\mu$. Since $W$ is a minimal surface, the birational map $W_0' \dashrightarrow W$ is a morphism $\pi$, and it is a composite of point blow-ups.

**Lemma 2.1 (transcendental lattices).** $\varphi_*\varphi^* = d$ on $T(S)_{\mathbb Q}$, and $\varphi^*$ maps $T(S)_{\mathbb Q}$ isomorphically onto $T(W)_{\mathbb Q}$, multiplying the form by $d$. If $\rho(S) = 1$ then $\rho(W) = 1$ and $d \cdot H^2 \cdot \operatorname{disc} \operatorname{NS}(W)$ is a square. If moreover $\varphi$ is Galois, then $d = 1$.

*Proof.* Let $\varphi^* = \pi_*f'^*$ and $\varphi_* = f'_*\pi^*$ be the maps induced by the closure of the graph; they are adjoint for the intersection forms. Let $x \in T(S)_{\mathbb Q}$. For every $\pi$-exceptional curve $E$, $f'^*x \cdot E = x \cdot f'_*E = 0$, because $f'_*E$ is algebraic. Since the lattice spanned by the exceptional curves is negative definite, $f'^*x$ lies in $\pi^*H^2(W, \mathbb Q)$, so $\pi^*\pi_*f'^*x = f'^*x$ and $\varphi_*\varphi^*x = f'_*f'^*x = d\,x$. (On algebraic classes the identity can fail, because $\pi^*\pi_*$ differs from the identity by exceptional classes.) Hence $\varphi^*x \cdot \varphi^*y = x \cdot \varphi_*\varphi^*y = d\,(x \cdot y)$ for $x, y \in T(S)_{\mathbb Q}$. If $x \in T(S)$ and $D \in \operatorname{NS}(W)$, then $\varphi^*x \cdot D = x \cdot \varphi_*D = 0$, so $\varphi^*T(S)_{\mathbb Q} \subset T(W)_{\mathbb Q}$. The image is a rational sub-Hodge structure that contains $H^{2,0}(W)$, so it contains $T(W)_{\mathbb Q}$. Hence $\varphi^*$ maps $T(S)_{\mathbb Q}$ isomorphically onto $T(W)_{\mathbb Q}$, and if $\rho(S) = 1$, then $\operatorname{rk} T(W) = 21$ and $\rho(W) = 1$. In a unimodular lattice, a primitive sublattice and its orthogonal complement have discriminants of equal absolute value; the signatures $(2,19)$ and $(1,0)$ fix the signs, so $\operatorname{disc} T(S) = -H^2$ and $\operatorname{disc} T(W) = -\operatorname{disc}\operatorname{NS}(W)$. An isometry $T(W)_{\mathbb Q} \cong T(S)_{\mathbb Q}(d)$ gives $\operatorname{disc} T(W) \equiv d^{21}\operatorname{disc} T(S)$ modulo squares, which is the condition because $21$ is odd. If $\varphi$ is Galois with group $G \ne 1$, then $G$ acts on $k(W)$, hence by birational automorphisms of $W$, which are biregular since $W$ is a minimal surface with $K_W = 0$. The form $\varphi^*\sigma_S$ is $G$-invariant and proportional to $\sigma_W$, so $G$ is symplectic. A nontrivial symplectic automorphism of finite order has coinvariant lattice of rank at least $8$ in $\operatorname{NS}(W)$ [13], so $\rho(W) \ge 9$, a contradiction. $\square$

**Lemma 2.2.** (a) $\varphi^*\sigma_S = c\,\sigma_W$ with $c \neq 0$, so $\varphi$ is étale wherever it is defined (outside finitely many points) and contracts no curve. (b) $K_{W_0'} = R_{f'}$ is an effective divisor whose support is exactly the set of $\pi$-exceptional curves. (c) Every curve contracted by $f'$ is $\pi$-exceptional.

*Proof.* (a) A nonzero holomorphic $2$-form on $W$ is nowhere zero, and $\varphi^*\sigma_S$ extends over the finite indeterminacy locus by Hartogs. It is not zero since $\varphi$ is dominant. Where $\varphi$ is defined, its Jacobian is therefore a nonzero constant. A contracted curve would lie in the zero locus of the Jacobian. (b) $f'^*\sigma_S = \pi^*(c\,\sigma_W)$ as rational $2$-forms on $W_0'$, because $f' = \varphi\pi$ on a dense open set. The divisor of the left side is $R_{f'}$ (as $K_S = 0$), and the divisor of the right side is $K_{W_0'/W}$, which is supported on all of $\operatorname{Exc}(\pi)$ with coefficients $\ge 1$. (c) If $f'$ contracts a curve $C$, then $f'^*\sigma_S$ vanishes along $C$, so $C \subset \operatorname{Supp} K_{W_0'/W} = \operatorname{Exc}(\pi)$ by (b). $\square$

**Lemma 2.3 (blow-up trees).** Let $\pi\colon Y \to W$ be a composite of blow-ups of points $p_1, \dots, p_r$ on smooth surfaces, with exceptional curves $E_1, \dots, E_r$ (strict transforms on $Y$), and write $K_{Y/W} = \sum n_j E_j$. Then:
- (a) the curves with $n_j = 1$ are pairwise disjoint;
- (b) two curves that meet have different coefficients;
- (c) for each $j$, the coefficients $n_i$ of the curves $E_i$ that meet $E_j$ and have $n_i < n_j$ sum to at most $n_j - 1$, each counted with its intersection number $E_i \cdot E_j$.

*Proof.* The total exceptional divisor of a composite of point blow-ups has simple normal crossings, so $E_i \cdot E_j \in \{0, 1\}$ for $i \ne j$. When $p_j$ is blown up, $n_j = 1 + \sum n_i$ over the earlier curves $E_i$ through $p_j$; there are at most two of them. A curve $E_k$ with $k > j$ that meets $E_j$ on $Y$ has $p_k$ on (the strict transform of) $E_j$, so $n_k \ge n_j + 1$. Hence the neighbours of $E_j$ with smaller coefficient are among the earlier curves through $p_j$, and their coefficients sum to at most $n_j - 1$. This is (c). If $E_i$ and $E_j$ meet with $i < j$, then $n_j \ge n_i + 1$, which is (b), and (a) follows from (b). $\square$

**Lemma 2.4 (inertia).** Suppose $d = 3$ and $\rho(S) = 1$. Over the generic point of every curve $C \subset S$, the fibre of $f$ has at least one unramified point. Consequently $f$ is not totally ramified along any curve, and $R_{f'} = \sum_j E'_j + (\text{$f'$-contracted curves})$, where the $E'_j$ are the strict transforms of the components of the ramification divisor $R_f$, each with coefficient $1$.

*Proof.* Each ramified component of $f'^*C$ lies in $\operatorname{Supp} R_{f'} = \operatorname{Exc}(\pi)$ (Lemma 2.2(b)), and so does every $f'$-contracted curve (Lemma 2.2(c)). If every component of $f'^*C$ were ramified, $f'^*C$ would be supported on $\operatorname{Exc}(\pi)$, on which the intersection form is negative definite. But $(f'^*C)^2 = 3C^2 > 0$ because $C$ is ample. So the fibre over the generic point of $C$ has type $(1,1,1)$ or $(2,1)$, the ramification index is at most $2$, and the coefficient of each $E'_j$ in $R_{f'}$ is $1$. $\square$

Every $\pi$-exceptional curve is therefore either some $E'_j$ (coefficient $1$) or a $\mu$-exceptional curve, because a component of $R_{f'}$ not contracted by $f'$ maps onto a curve of $S$ and is a ramification curve. Conversely every $\mu$-exceptional curve is $\pi$-exceptional, by Lemma 2.2(c).

## 3. Proof of Theorem 1.1

Let $\varphi$ have degree $3$, keep the notation of Section 2, and write $K = K_{W_0'} = \sum n_a C_a$ with $n_a \ge 1$, summed over the $\pi$-exceptional curves (Lemma 2.2(b)). The proof has four steps.

**Step 1: $W_0$ is smooth.** Suppose $x \in W_0$ is singular, and let $G$ be a curve of maximal coefficient $n = n_G$ among the curves of $\mu^{-1}(x)$. Since $G$ is $\pi$-exceptional (Lemma 2.2(c)), it is a smooth rational curve; since $\mu$ is minimal, $G$ is not a $(-1)$-curve, so $K \cdot G \ge 0$ and $G^2 \le -2$. Hence
$$\sum_{C_a \ne G} n_a\,(C_a \cdot G) \;\ge\; -n\,G^2 \;\ge\; 2n.$$
The curves $C_a \ne G$ that meet $G$ are either $\mu$-exceptional over $x$, with $n_a \le n$ by maximality and $n_a \ne n$ by Lemma 2.3(b), or strict transforms $E'_j$ of ramification curves, with $n_a = 1$ (Lemma 2.4). If $n \ge 2$, every neighbour of $G$ has a smaller coefficient, so Lemma 2.3(c) bounds the left side by $n - 1 < 2n$. If $n = 1$, every neighbour of $G$ has coefficient $1$, so by Lemma 2.3(a) $G$ has no neighbours, and the left side is $0 < 2$. Both cases are contradictions.

So $W_0 = W_0'$, $f$ is a finite morphism of smooth surfaces, and by Lemma 2.4 the $\pi$-exceptional curves are exactly the components of $R_f$, each with coefficient $1$. By Lemma 2.3(a) these components are pairwise disjoint. They are smooth rational curves, as is every exceptional curve of a composite of point blow-ups.

**Step 2: local form of $f$.** The branch curve $B = f(R_f)$ is nonempty, since otherwise $f$ would be étale over the simply connected surface $S$. Let $Q \in B$. Since $d = 3$ and the generic fibre over $B$ has type $(2,1)$, the fibre $f^{-1}(Q)$ (which contains a point where $f$ is not étale) consists of either two points, of local degrees $2$ and $1$, or one point $P$ of local degree $3$.
- In the first case, the germ of $f$ at the point of local degree $2$ is a finite map of degree $2$ between smooth germs, hence the quotient by an involution. Its quotient is smooth, so the involution is a reflection, and $B$ is smooth at $Q$.
- In the second case, $df_P \ne 0$, since a germ with $df_P = 0$ has local degree at least $4$. Choose coordinates with $f(x,y) = (x, g(x,y))$. The local degree is $\operatorname{ord}_y g(0,y) = 3$, and $R_f = \{g_y = 0\}$ is reduced and smooth at $P$ by Step 1. Since $g_{yy}(0) = 0$, smoothness forces $g_{xy}(0) \ne 0$. Then the kernel $\partial_y$ of $df_P$ is tangent to $R_f$. Together with $g_{yyy}(0) \ne 0$ these are the recognition conditions for Whitney's cusp [14]; the holomorphic version follows from finite determinacy [15]. So $f$ is equivalent at $P$ to the pleat $(x,y) \mapsto (x, y^3 + xy)$, and $B$ has an ordinary cusp at $Q$.

Hence every singular point of $B$ is an ordinary cusp, and $B$ is locally irreducible everywhere. Distinct components of $B$ would be disjoint, but on a surface of Picard number one any two curves meet. So $B$ is irreducible, $B \in |kH|$ for some $k \ge 1$, and over a general point of $B$ exactly one point of $W_0$ is ramified. Therefore $R_f = E$ is a single smooth rational curve mapping birationally onto $B$, and $\pi\colon W_0 \to W$ is one blow-up. In particular $e(W_0) = 25$, $E^2 = -1$, and $B$ is rational with $\kappa$ ordinary cusps and no other singularities.

**Step 3: the branch curve.** Since $B$ is rational with $\kappa$ cusps, $p_a(B) = k^2H^2/2 + 1 = \kappa$. Since $B$ is locally irreducible, it is homeomorphic to its normalization $\mathbb P^1$, so $e(B) = 2$. Over the $\kappa$ cusps the fibre of $f$ is one point. Over the other points of $B$, whose Euler number is $2 - \kappa$, it is two points. So $e(f^{-1}B) = 2(2 - \kappa) + \kappa = 4 - \kappa$, and
$$25 = e(W_0) = 3\,(24 - 2) + (4 - \kappa),$$
which gives $\kappa = 45$ and $k^2H^2 = 88$. Since $H^2$ is even, $(k, H^2) \in \{(1, 88), (2, 22)\}$.

**Step 4: the discriminant.** By Lemma 2.1, $\operatorname{NS}(W) = \mathbb Z g$, and $\operatorname{Pic}(W_0) = \mathbb Z\pi^*g \oplus \mathbb ZE$. Write $f^*H = \pi^*D - aE$ with $D \in \operatorname{NS}(W)$. Then $a = f^*H \cdot E = H \cdot B = kH^2$ and $(f^*H)^2 = 3H^2$, so
$$D^2 = 3H^2 + k^2H^4 = 3H^2 + 88H^2 = 91H^2.$$
Since $D$ is a multiple of $g$, $D^2/g^2$ is a square. Lemma 2.1 requires $3H^2g^2$ to be a square, hence also $3H^2 \cdot D^2 = 273\,H^4$. But $273 = 3 \cdot 7 \cdot 13$ is not a square, which is a contradiction. $\square$

## 4. Examples and remarks

**Higher Picard number.** The proof uses $\rho(S) = 1$ in three places: in Lemma 2.4, where $C^2 > 0$ for every curve $C$; in Step 2, where any two curves meet; and in Step 4. The first two hold whenever every curve on $S$ has positive self-intersection, since two such curves meet by the Hodge index theorem. This condition is equivalent to $\operatorname{NS}(S)$ representing neither $0$ nor $-2$: if $v \ne 0$ and $v^2 \in \{0, -2\}$, Riemann–Roch makes $v$ or $-v$ effective, and a component of it has $C^2 \le 0$. Steps 1–3 then go through with $B \in \operatorname{NS}(S)$ and $B^2 = 88$. Step 4 becomes a lattice computation. By Lemma 2.1, $\rho(W) = \rho(S) =: \rho$. For $x \in \operatorname{NS}(S)$ write $f^*x = \pi^*\psi(x) - (x \cdot B)E$ with $\psi(x) \in \operatorname{NS}(W)$; as in Step 4, the coefficient is $f^*x \cdot E = x \cdot f_*E = x \cdot B$. Since $(f^*x)\cdot(f^*y) = 3\,x \cdot y$,
$$\psi(x) \cdot \psi(y) = 3\,x \cdot y + (x \cdot B)(y \cdot B).$$
The map $\psi$ is injective: if $\psi(x) = 0$, then $3x = f_*f^*x$ is a multiple of $f_*E = B$, and $3x^2 = -(x\cdot B)^2$ forces $x = 0$ because $B^2 > 0$. So $\psi(\operatorname{NS}(S))$ has finite index in $\operatorname{NS}(W)$. If $G$ is a Gram matrix of $\operatorname{NS}(S)$, the matrix determinant lemma gives $\operatorname{disc}\psi(\operatorname{NS}(S)) = 3^{\rho-1}(3 + B^2)\det G = 3^{\rho-1}\cdot 91 \cdot \operatorname{disc}\operatorname{NS}(S)$. On the other hand $T(W)_{\mathbb Q} \cong T(S)_{\mathbb Q}(3)$ gives $\operatorname{disc} T(W) \equiv 3^{22-\rho}\operatorname{disc} T(S)$ modulo squares, and $\operatorname{disc}\operatorname{NS}(W)/\operatorname{disc}\operatorname{NS}(S) = \operatorname{disc} T(W)/\operatorname{disc} T(S)$ as in the proof of Lemma 2.1. Hence $3^{\rho-1}\cdot 91 \equiv 3^{22-\rho}$ modulo squares, so $273$ would be a square. For $\rho = 1$ this is Step 4. Therefore Theorem 1.1 holds for every K3 surface $S$ on which every curve has positive self-intersection. By Meyer's theorem an indefinite form of rank $\ge 5$ represents $0$, so such surfaces have $\rho(S) \le 4$. They exist for $\rho = 2$: the lattice $\langle 2\rangle \oplus \langle -6\rangle$ embeds primitively in $U^{\oplus 2}$, and it represents neither $0$ nor $-2$, since $3$ is not a rational square and $a^2 - 3b^2 = -1$ has no solution modulo $3$. By the surjectivity of the period map it is the Néron–Severi lattice of the K3 surfaces in an $18$-dimensional family. In fact Lemma 2.4 and Step 2 only need positivity for the components of the branch curve $B$, since these are the only curves to which they are applied.

**Branch curves of square zero.**

**Proposition 4.1.** Let $\varphi\colon W \dashrightarrow S$ be a dominant rational map of degree $3$ between K3 surfaces. If no component of its branch curve $B$ is a $(-2)$-curve, then $S$ has an elliptic fibration with $j$-invariant $0$ whose singular fibres are $12$ cuspidal curves, and $B$ is the union of these fibres.

*Proof.* Lemma 2.4 also holds when $C^2 = 0$, since a nonzero divisor supported on $\operatorname{Exc}(\pi)$ has negative square. Suppose that no component of $B$ is a $(-2)$-curve, so that every component has square $\ge 0$. Steps 1 and 2 then show, as before, that $W_0$ is the blow-up of $W$ at $r$ distinct points, that $R_f = E_1 + \dots + E_r$ with $E_i$ mapping birationally onto the components $B_i$ of $B$, and that the $B_i$ are pairwise disjoint rational curves whose only singularities are $\kappa_i$ ordinary cusps, so $p_a(B_i) = \kappa_i$. The Euler count of Step 3 becomes $24 + r = 3(24 - 2r) + (4r - \kappa)$ with $\kappa = \sum_i \kappa_i$, that is, $\kappa = 48 - 3r$. If $r = 1$, then $B^2 = 88$ and the computation above gives a contradiction. If $r \ge 2$, two disjoint curves of non-negative square are, by the Hodge index theorem, proportional with square $0$. So the $B_i$ are fibres of one elliptic fibration, $p_a(B_i) = 1 = \kappa_i$, and $r = \kappa = 48 - 3r$ gives $r = 12$. Each $B_i$ is a cuspidal rational fibre, of type II and Euler number $2$, and since $e(S) = 24 = 12 \cdot 2$ there are no other singular fibres. Hence the $j$-invariant of the fibration has no poles and is identically $0$. $\square$

If the fibration has a section, $S$ is $y^2 = x^3 + b(t)$ and carries the non-symplectic automorphism $x \mapsto \omega x$ of order $3$. This case occurs: for $b$ of degree $12$ with simple roots, fibrewise multiplication by $1 - \omega$ is a dominant rational self-map of degree $3$ that is étale over the smooth fibres. This map is constructed in [8, Proposition 4.1], where its graph is an example of a singular Lagrangian cycle that does not deform. What is new here is that the classification above singles out this situation, that the normalization of $S$ in the function field of the source is a smooth finite triple cover, and that this cover is non-split (next paragraph). All examples in Section 1 have $(-2)$-curves in their branch curves.

**A non-split triple cover.**

**Proposition 4.2.** Let $S$ be $y^2 = x^3 + b(t)$ with $b$ of degree $12$ with simple roots, and let $\varphi\colon S \dashrightarrow S$ be fibrewise multiplication by $1 - \omega$. The normalization $W_0$ of $S$ in the function field of the source is the blow-up of $S$ at the $12$ cusps, and $f\colon W_0 \to S$ is a non-split triple cover with $\kappa(W_0) = 0$.

*Proof.* The map $\varphi$ has degree $|1 - \omega|^2 = 3$. The smooth locus of the fibration is its Néron model, so $\varphi$ is defined away from the $12$ cusps and is étale over the smooth fibres. Hence the branch curve consists of cuspidal fibres, and by the proof of Proposition 4.1, $f\colon W_0 \to S$ is a finite map of degree $3$ from the blow-up $W_0$ of $S$ at the $12$ cusps (the blown-up points are points of indeterminacy of $\varphi$, since their exceptional curves map onto curves). So $f$ is a triple cover in the sense of Miranda [16], with $f_*\mathcal O_{W_0} = \mathcal O_S \oplus \mathcal E$ for a vector bundle $\mathcal E$ of rank $2$, and $\kappa(W_0) = 0$. We claim that $\mathcal E$ is indecomposable. Let $E_K$ be the generic fibre, $K = \mathbb C(t)$. Over $\bar K$ the isogeny $\varphi$ is a torsor under its kernel $\mathbb Z/3$, so $\mathcal E|_{E_K}$ becomes $\eta \oplus \eta^{-1}$, where $\eta$ is a non-trivial line bundle of degree $0$ with $\varphi^*\eta$ trivial. The kernel of $\varphi^*$ on $\operatorname{Pic}^0(E_{\bar K}) = E(\bar K)$ has order $3$ and is stable under $\omega$, and the only such subgroup is $\{0, (0, \pm\sqrt b)\}$. If $\mathcal E = L_1 \oplus L_2$, then by the Krull–Schmidt theorem $L_1|_{E_K}$ becomes $\eta$ or $\eta^{-1}$ over $\bar K$, so the class of $\eta$ is invariant under $\operatorname{Gal}(\bar K/K)$. But $\sqrt b \mapsto -\sqrt b$ exchanges $\eta$ and $\eta^{-1}$, because $b$ is not a square. So $f$ is a non-split triple cover of a K3 surface by a surface with $\kappa = 0$. $\square$

Garbagnati and Penegini did not know whether such covers exist [5, Section 1].

**Kummer surfaces.** Let $A$ be an abelian surface with a subgroup $C_3 \subset A$ of order $3$. The group generated by translations by $C_3$ and by $-1$ is $S_3$, and the map $\operatorname{Km}(A) = \widetilde{A/\pm1} \dashrightarrow \widetilde{A/S_3} = \operatorname{Km}(A/C_3)$ has degree $3$ and is not Galois, because $\langle -1\rangle$ is not normal in $S_3$. A transposition $x \mapsto c - x$ with $c \in C_3 \setminus 0$ fixes the $16$ points with $2x = c$. These are smooth points of $A/\pm1$ and map to nodes of $A/S_3$. So after resolution the branch curves are exceptional $(-2)$-curves, and Lemma 2.4 fails here, as it must. Here $\rho \ge 17$.

**Higher degree.** Step 1 uses only that ramification curves have coefficient $1$ in $R_f$. It therefore holds in any degree $d$ for maps whose ramification is simple along every curve. For such maps the local part of Step 2 also extends. At a point $P$ of local degree $j \ge 3$ we have $df_P \ne 0$, since otherwise the Jacobian determinant would vanish to order at least $2$ at $P$, and $R_f$ would not be smooth and reduced there. As in Step 2, smoothness of $R_f$ then forces $g_{xy}(0) \ne 0$, and the image of $R_f$ near $P$ is a branch with the single Puiseux pair $(j-1, j)$. The numerical part does not extend. When $d \ge 4$, a fibre can contain two points of local degree $2$, so $B$ can have nodes, as the apparent contour of a general projection of a quartic surface does. Nodes do not enter the Euler count: in general $e(W_0) = 24d - e(R_f) - \sum_P (j_P - 2)$, summed over the points of local degree $j_P \ge 3$. So Step 3 no longer determines $k^2H^2$, and the argument stops there. Lemma 2.4 also allows inertia of type $(3,1)$ when $d \ge 4$. We do not treat these cases.

**Intermediate covers.** Theorem 1.1 does constrain maps of higher degree. Let $\varphi\colon W \dashrightarrow S$ be dominant of degree $d$, with $W$ a K3 surface and $\rho(S) = 1$. Let $k(S) \subset L \subset k(W)$ be an intermediate field and $Y_1$ a smooth projective model of $L$. Pulling back forms along the dominant maps $W \dashrightarrow Y_1 \dashrightarrow S$ gives $p_g(Y_1) = 1$ and $q(Y_1) = 0$, and $\kappa(Y_1) \le \kappa(W) = 0$, so $Y_1$ is birational to a K3 surface $Y$. Lemma 2.1 for $Y \dashrightarrow S$ gives $\rho(Y) = 1$. Lemma 2.1 (maps of degree $2$ are Galois) and Theorem 1.1, applied to $W \dashrightarrow Y$ and to $Y \dashrightarrow S$, show that neither $[k(W):L]$ nor $[L:k(S)]$ is $2$ or $3$. If $d < 16$, every factorization $d = ab$ with $a, b \ge 2$ has $a$ or $b$ in $\{2, 3\}$. So for $d < 16$ the extension $k(W)/k(S)$ has no intermediate field; equivalently, the monodromy group of $\varphi$ is primitive.

**Checks.** The script `deg3check.py` (supplement) enumerates the solutions $(k, H^2, g^2)$ of Steps 3 and 4 and finds $3H^2g^2 \in \{132132, 2114112, 528528\}$, none a square, and verifies the identity $(f^*B)\cdot E = B^2$, i.e. $D' \cdot E = 2\kappa$ for $f^*B = 2E + D'$. In both cases, $(f^*B)^2 = 3B^2 = 264$ gives $D'^2 = -92$, and adjunction with $K_{W_0} = E$ gives $p_a(D') = 0$, as it must for a curve mapping birationally onto $B$. We also checked that the number of cusps agrees with the count $12 + 3k^2H^2/8 = 45$ obtained from Miranda's description of triple covers [16]. The scripts `rhocheck.py` and `nonsplit.py` check the determinant identity and the lattice example of the Higher Picard number paragraph, and that $x((1-\omega)P) = c\,(x^3 + 4b)/x^2$ on $y^2 = x^3 + b$, so that $1 - \omega$ has degree $3$ and kernel $\{0, (0, \pm\sqrt b)\}$.
