# Accept

The manuscript establishes a sound, narrowly scoped empirical result: in sources trained on incomplete fictional profiles, ordinary profile generation recovers the complete taught association set more reliably than the tested confidence-based selection of separate field answers. This is a useful reduced contribution to faithful reporting because it tests both recovery of supported detail and avoidance of unsupported additions. It does not improve an Activation Oracle or explain the original model errors, and the manuscript appropriately avoids those claims.

The decisive evidence supports the conclusion. I independently recomputed field and panel calibration from the saved canonical responses in the fixed- and variable-count studies, obtaining the reported thresholds and test counts, including both failed primary criteria. I also compared all 400 saved native-profile responses against the complete taught sentence sets, rather than relying only on the released annotation summaries. These responses contain exactly the taught statements. On the designated test identities, this confirms $60/60$ complete panels and $210/210$ taught fields per fit, versus $30$, $0$, $47$, and $56$ complete panels for variable-count panel calibration. Independent calculation reproduces the paired gain of $107/240=44.58$ percentage points and the reported persona-bootstrap interval of $[40.00,48.75]$ percentage points. These checks reproduce analysis of retained outputs; they do not independently reproduce neural training or generation.

The contribution is incremental but sufficiently distinct from the closest prior work. The manuscript’s [5] already demonstrates perfect direct re-query recovery on a fresh persona adapter, [13] establishes useful native-decoder verification of AO proposals, and [9] separates assigned training content from measured behavior. The external literature also establishes difference readout [2], confidence estimation for activation oracles [7], selective classification [10], and selection of reliable components of generated responses [12]. None of those ideas should be credited here as new. The additional result is complete set recovery when some attributes were deliberately withheld and the observer does not receive the per-identity taught count. Success at answering a specified taught attribute does not establish that capability. The paired comparison shows why an evaluation that forces every field can substantially mischaracterize recoverable, supported information.

The favorable training construction is essential to the interpretation. Whole-profile training answers explicitly demonstrate which fields to include and where to stop. Thus, omission behavior is supervised through those answers even though the native inference query receives no count or gold mask. My inspection of the variable-count training records found the exact broad query for 94 of 100 identities, including 57 of the 60 test identities; all assistant training statements were taught associations. This is evidence of successful retrieval under a familiar interface, not generalization to previously untaught personas, unfamiliar query formats, or arbitrary missing knowledge. The manuscript discloses this distinction sufficiently for acceptance. Its statement that prompt, prefill, generation granularity, and stopping change together also correctly prevents a causal attribution to stopping alone.

The statistical and semantic qualifications are appropriate. The four fits share identities and training data, so the bootstrap describes variation across identities conditional on the fitted sources and selected thresholds; it does not establish robustness across independently trained source populations. The failed primaries remain failures, and total abstention is correctly assigned undefined precision. The perfect native-panel result also does not establish perfect interpretation of the earlier challenged responses: the acknowledged pronoun and residual-text failures limit discrepancy recall to recognized assertions. These are meaningful limits, but they do not invalidate the complete association-recovery comparison.

Scientific communication meets the acceptance standard. The distinction among injected association, earlier expression, and later retrieval is explicit; clean and complete panels have different, understandable definitions; and the principal comparison appears before the supporting studies. Figure 1 communicates the practical result clearly, with accessible numerical values. The abstract is informative and appropriately qualified. The manuscript is lengthy, but its central argument is identifiable without reconstructing the experiment chronology.

Useful minor improvements are:

- Quantify exact training-query overlap in the main text, and consistently describe the absence of supplied count information as an inference-time property.
- Add explicit attribution to the earlier same-problem paper, “Same activation, different answers” (paper 8, version 2), for the broader lesson that a trained prompt can expose capabilities missed by another query. The present missing-field set-recovery result remains distinct.
- Consolidate detailed registration timing and reservation accounting into an appendix, retaining the consequential timing qualifications and total costs in the main text. Pin model and software revisions alongside the released recipes to make future neural reproduction easier.

Acceptance rests on the supported benchmark finding and its practical implication for evaluation design, with the stated restrictions on training familiarity, source access, uncertainty, and semantic extraction.
