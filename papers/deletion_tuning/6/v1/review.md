# Accept

This is a useful, clearly communicated empirical advance on deletion tuning's capacity–retention trade-off. The strongest contributions are the substantial improvement in single-matrix memorization with a straight-through estimator (STE), the demonstration that successful deletion-based memorization can severely damage an existing capability, and the restoration experiments separating target recall from retention damage. These address the supplied problem substantively, despite using a synthetic target rather than MMLU-Pro. Acceptance is for these bounded findings on one model, one fact format and one retention task, not a general law that the estimator is irrelevant or that early layers universally determine forgetting.

The retained evidence supports the principal numerical results. I independently recomputed correctness from the released parsed answers and gold answers, checked paired question alignment, and recovered the reported accuracy counts. The base scores $647/1319$; the two dense STE masks score $88/1319$ and $80/1319$; the ramp-2x mask scores $545/1319$. Restoring its largest-magnitude quarter raises GSM8K to $632/1319$ while eliminating sampled fact recall, whereas restoring its smallest-magnitude quarter preserves recall at $250/256$ but yields only $569/1319$ on GSM8K. This is informative intervention evidence about the selected mask. The separately trained early- and late-layer masks score $569/1319$ and $615/1319$, respectively. An independent paired bootstrap gives an early-minus-late difference of approximately $-3.49$ percentage points with a 95% interval of $[-6.29,-0.76]$, consistent with the reported direction and uncertainty.

I inspected the trainer and evaluators and reanalyzed saved records; I did not reproduce model training or generation. The single-layer STE recalls of $235/256$ and $233/256$ are supported by retained run summaries, rather than independently regenerated outputs. Full generation texts are not distributed, so intrusion counts, output-length descriptions and illustrative failure cases cannot be independently audited from the supplied hashes alone. These are secondary observations, and the manuscript appropriately avoids making format leakage its general explanation.

The contribution is meaningfully distinct from the published same-problem work. Paper [5] already established schedule sensitivity and full-gate memorization; those are appropriately inherited baselines here. Paper [6] studied retention under selected, much smaller adaptations. External comparison with Lu et al. [1], Zhao et al. [3], Piggyback [8] and LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less [9] confirms that binary masking and adaptation–forgetting trade-offs are established ideas. The novelty here is the specific empirical interaction in high-information memorization, particularly the single-matrix improvement and the dissociation exposed by restoration. It is not the invention of STE or masking frozen weights.

The following qualifications and corrections would improve the paper without changing this acceptance decision:

1. **Keep estimator conclusions descriptive.** Sections 1 and 4.2 use stronger language than the appropriately limited discussion in Section 5. A single approximately count-matched STE comparison does not establish estimator equivalence; learning rate, update count and target recall also differ. From the released records, STE minus the two annealed seed results is approximately $-0.30$ points, 95% interval $[-2.88,2.20]$, and $-2.43$ points, interval $[-5.00,0.08]$. Meaningful differences remain possible. Say that measured costs were similar and the preregistered point-estimate criterion was not met, rather than claiming statistical refutation of an estimator effect. Similarly, restrict “the learning rate, not the estimator, sets where a run lands” to the observed operating points. Removing the sharpness schedule does not remove dependence on the fixed surrogate sharpness $c_0$.

2. **Distinguish intervention effects from isolated mechanisms.** Restoration identifies effects of particular deletion subsets in one trained mask. It does not independently identify count, norm and location as causal factors: the layer halves differ in all three, and the follow-up training uses one seed per group. The observation that early-only deletions impair GSM8K without retaining sampled facts is strong; the claim that location matters *beyond norm* is suggestive rather than isolated experimentally. Random-quarter restoration demonstrates fragility under that intervention, not that nearly every deletion is necessary: the smallest-magnitude quarter can be restored with little recall loss. The stated limitations already support a sound narrow interpretation.

3. **Correct the related-work distinction.** Section 2 contrasts [7]'s restrictions before training with this paper's restoration afterward. However, [7], Section 4.2, also reports post-training restoration of the smallest- and largest-magnitude deletions, as well as support-restricted retraining. Credit that precedent explicitly. The new distinction is the joint memorization/retention evidence and layer interventions, not restoration itself. This does not erase the substantive new results.

4. **Tighten statistical and preregistration reporting.** With average ranks for ties on the 11 released surgery conditions, I obtain Spearman correlations of approximately $0.8975$ for removed norm, $0.7153$ for count and $0.7699$ for base-solution CE, rather than $0.88$, $0.71$ and $0.76$. Specify the calculation and tie handling. The ordering survives, but correlated edits of one mask do not establish out-of-sample predictive superiority. The abstract's “all analyses were preregistered” also needs correction: the surgery document explicitly labels the norm/count correlations as beyond preregistration. The supplied dated documents describe a sequential investigation with follow-ups after earlier results; distinguish these stages from untouched confirmation. The observed seed spread should not be used as a universal significance threshold.

5. **Clarify reproducibility details.** Table 1's statement that learning rate is fixed within each row applies to deletion-estimator comparisons, not gate-FFT, which uses different rates. Define recall precisely as a generated continuation beginning with the complete value, as implemented by `startswith`, rather than unrestricted exact-string equality. Add exact recall counts and sampling uncertainty. A compact cost summary can use existing run records: full-gate 8-epoch STE takes roughly 856–890 seconds and 9.3 GiB peak allocation, versus approximately 791–797 seconds and 6.8 GiB for gate-FFT. Clarify the measurement scope rather than implying equal compute from equal updates. Publicly archiving compressed generations and a portable statistical replay script would strengthen auditability.

The scientific argument is readable and organized around comparisons and interventions rather than a run chronology. The explicit limitations, same-matrix additive control and disclosure of failed predictions are valuable. A recall-versus-retention plot would make operating points easier to compare, but the existing tables communicate the decisive evidence. These corrections concern interpretation, attribution and reporting precision; they do not undermine the supported empirical contribution.
