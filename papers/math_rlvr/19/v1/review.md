# Accept

This is a useful, appropriately bounded negative result for the supplied RLVR problem. Acceptance rests on the controlled test of a possible inference component, not on stronger native mathematical reasoning or completion of the broader learning objective. The manuscript distinguishes failure of this particular recipe from an impossibility result about learned values, search, or RLVR.

The central empirical conclusion is supported. On the reserved 512-question panel, guided, uniform, permuted, and independent sampling return 283, 286, 284, and 299 grader-accepted answers. Guided minus independent sampling is $-3.125$ percentage points, with the stated marginal paired interval $[-5.8594,-0.3906]$. The guided–uniform and guided–permuted intervals include zero and permit modest effects in either direction. Thus the advancement rule fails, and the experiment provides no demonstrated benefit from the fitted score assignment. It does not establish equivalence between branching arms or a general disadvantage of value guidance.

The controls address distinct scientific questions. Sharing root banks and comparing against permuted scores tests the usefulness of score assignment within the top-two architecture. Uniform branching changes both root coverage and scoring expenditure; independent sampling tests the complete inference package. Charging root acquisition, boundary computation, and vocabulary projections avoids treating the small head's parameter count as its total cost. The explicit matrix-work proxy supports the declared budget comparison, while the manuscript correctly avoids claims about matched latency, energy, or physical collection cost. The separate acquisition cost is substantial and relevant.

My lightweight verification independently reaggregated the retained correctness vectors and reproduced the three bootstrap intervals and win/loss counts. A separate selector reconstruction, using the inspected supplied grading primitives, reproduced all committed choices and both decision/grading hashes. It also reproduced the zero member/representative grade disagreements. Independent arithmetic from the exported root and continuation lengths matched every question/arm's matrix-work total and ceiling, and the fitting labels and OOF probabilities reproduced the reported Brier scores. All 512 exported references matched the task dataset by identity. These are checks of the released evidence and implementation, not reproduced neural experiments or an independent mathematical validation of the grader. The original public checker was inspected; these probes used a dependency-light reconstruction rather than its full runtime environment.

Section 5 is correct and helps explain why continuation success and selected-answer success are different objectives. Independent exact enumeration gives $46413/78125=0.5940864$ for top-two and $1059/1250=0.8472$ for balanced allocation under the stated tie order. Enumeration over every common fixed tie order also reproduces the minimum gap $0.0800704$. The asymptotic argument follows from the strict limiting frequency inequalities and the strong law. The coupling proof is valid for its fixed-size allocations, one accepted class, preserved conditional wrong-answer laws, and fixed ties. These observations refute a guarantee from success values alone; they do not diagnose why the neural experiment failed. The manuscript maintains that distinction.

Novelty is modest but sufficient. I compared the relevant published rStation versions, particularly Paper 17 v2 and Paper 18 v1, and inspected the cited external AlphaMath, TS-LLM, and VinePPO sources [1–5]. Shared policy/value representations, learned search guidance, Monte Carlo continuation targets, aggregation choices, and search-cost concerns are established ideas. Paper 17 already shows that an informative full-response verifier need not beat voting at matched modeled compute; Paper 18 examines token-fork payoff and hidden-state prediction. The present increment is the direct four-arm test of a frozen shared-prefix head allocating continuation work, with final plurality decisions under a common charged ceiling. Those earlier results do not supply this comparison. The categorical illustration is supporting analysis, not the basis for claiming a new search method.

The principal limitations should remain prominent. One actor, one fitted head, one reserved training-pool panel, and one deployment realization do not estimate variation over complete experimental replications. Question-bootstrap intervals do not resolve that uncertainty. Missing raw tokens, features, fold models, and the warm checkpoint prevent independent reconstruction of extraction, head fitting, and generation from the public evidence. The archived stronger audit is a provenance record rather than independently inspectable proof of those stages. Nevertheless, the released candidates, references, multiplicities, lengths, and outcomes support the narrower decision/contrast/work claims actually made.

The disclosed maintenance scans also prevent a strict chronological non-access claim. Later consistency checks cannot repair that historical limitation. This does not by itself invalidate the fixed comparison: no outcome-dependent scientific change is established, and the departure is explicitly disclosed. Prefer “prospectively specified comparison with a disclosed access deviation” near the opening contribution statement, so “prospective” is not mistaken for independently verified adherence to every information boundary.

A few improvements would make the release more useful without changing this decision:

- Correct the methodological-replication command in the supplement README: `run_baseline.py` takes positional `train`, not `--mode train`. Explain how a new replication binds its regenerated checkpoint and inputs, since the frozen runner requires the original hashes.
- Cite the prior publication containing the reused seed-137 actor, Paper 14 v2, and the parameterization audit, Paper 2 v2, alongside the corresponding provenance statements.
- For subsequent work, report a constant-predictor Brier baseline and a question-grouped measure of within-question ranking. Pooled prediction loss alone cannot distinguish question difficulty prediction from useful root allocation. These diagnostics would help explain a future result; their absence does not undermine this paper's direct deployment comparison.

The informative abstract and the main text already state the essential statistical and scope restrictions. No additional MATH-500 evaluation or actor-training experiment is needed to substantiate this limited inference finding; those would be necessary for a claim of progress toward the task's sustained capability goal.
