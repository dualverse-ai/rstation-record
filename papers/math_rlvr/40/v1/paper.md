## Research question

A 7B policy and its generated solutions compete for space with the learner on a single GPU. We study a learner that updates 196 dense matrices of Qwen2.5-Math-7B using activation-derived QR bases and four-bit feedback for BF16 rounding errors. Two questions can be answered from the saved run. How much persistent matrix state does this update require compared with alternatives on exactly the same weights? How much of the observed change in greedy MATH-500 accuracy survives a change in the evaluation prompt?

This distinction matters for mathematical reasoning. A question that the original model solves when shown as plain text can become a failure when wrapped in a request for a boxed answer. Counting that question as a training gain under the wrapper alone overlooks a solution already accessible from the starting weights. We therefore track the same 500 questions across both prompts and both checkpoints. This is an analysis of one realized training trajectory.

GaLore projects gradients to compress optimizer state [1], and Flora refreshes projections while carrying compressed momentum [4]. LoQT combines low-rank training with quantization-error compensation and demonstrates 7B training on a 24 GB GPU [5]. Our implementation fits its basis to layer inputs, updates BF16 weights shared with the rollout engine, and maintains a signed four-bit correction for each updated weight. The 0.6B rStation study [2] examines a different spectral and precision intervention. A subsequent 7B study [3] credits this implementation and tests which sampled responses to backpropagate. Its public trainer provides an independent implementation reference. Our additional measurements concern the state and arithmetic required by this update, checkpoint selection and how prompt choice changes the measured gain.

## Training on one GPU

We started from Qwen2.5-Math-7B. The rollout engine (vLLM) and the learner shared the same BF16 weight storage. The learner applied updates directly to the seven attention and feedforward matrices in each of 28 transformer blocks, covering 6,525,288,448 matrix weights. Norms and biases used a separate one-dimensional optimizer. We reclaimed the rollout key/value cache during the backward pass and restored it for generation.

**Activation-based gradient projection (ActQR).** For a linear layer with weight matrix $W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}$, let $X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}$ be its input activations and $D\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$ the derivative of the loss with respect to its outputs. Every eight optimizer steps the learner refreshed a rank-$r$ basis. It sketched the token dimension with a seeded Gaussian matrix $\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}$ of entry variance $1/n$, added a small isotropic random perturbation, and computed a reduced QR factorization of $(\Omega X + E)^\top$ in FP32. It stored the orthonormal columns $Q$ in BF16. Backpropagation accumulated $S=D^\top(XQ)$ in FP32. This computes the projected weight gradient directly. On a basis change, the first moment was transported into the new coordinates via $M_{\rm old}Q_{\rm old}^{\top}Q_{\rm new}$. The optimizer maintained a BF16 first moment per projected coefficient and one FP32 second moment per output row. The row second moment tracked the mean squared projected gradient across columns. We clipped the joint gradient norm to 1, applied bias correction, and mapped the normalized step back to weight space with $Q^\top$ and scale $\sqrt{k/r}$. The Stage-2 and Stage-3 optimizer used decay coefficients 0.9 and 0.95 and denominator offset $10^{-8}$.

**Four-bit rounding correction.** A small update can round back to the old BF16 weight. To carry its remainder to the next update, the optimizer stored a signed integer $q\in[-8,7]$ beside each updated matrix weight, two integers per byte. If $h(w)$ denotes half the local BF16 spacing (floored at $2^{-23}$ in the implementation), the reconstructed value is $w+qh(w)/8$. Each step added the projected weight change to this reconstruction, cast the result to BF16, and stochastically rounded the new remainder in units of $h(w_{\rm new})/8$. A rounded value of +8 carried into the next BF16 weight and the remainder was re-expressed there. Thus an update smaller than the BF16 spacing can accumulate across steps while rollouts use ordinary BF16 weights. Rank 128 required 3,737.89 MiB of persistent optimizer state, including 3,111.50 MiB for these corrections. Ranks 256 for attention and feedforward gate/up matrices and 512 for the 18,944-input feedforward down projection raised the total to 4,661.89 MiB. The implementation places persistent optimizer state on the device and assigns zero to the host-state accounting field. This is a storage convention. We did not measure all allocations of the host process. CPU checkpoint export and averaging used host memory separately.

**Training data and objective.** The update uses the response-length normalization of Dr. GRPO [6] and asymmetric clipping and dynamic sampling related to DAPO [7]. From the 12,000-problem MATH training file, the loader retained 8,552 Level 3–5 records after excluding exact problem texts in the 210- and 350-item selection panels and MATH-500. We sampled responses to a common prompt, scored the extracted final answer against its ground truth, and assigned truncated responses zero reward. Within each prompt group the policy advantage was the reward minus the group mean. For token likelihood ratio $\rho$ between current and pre-update policies, the learner maximized the smaller of $\rho A$ and $\mathrm{clip}(\rho,1-\epsilon_-,1+\epsilon_+)A$ over eligible response tokens. The loss denominator was the optimizer batch size in responses times the response-token limit, so short and long completions shared the same denominator. Stage 2 and Stage 3 used a 3,000-token limit, lower clip 0.20 and upper clip 0.28 (0.32 when the preceding mean token entropy fell below 0.12 nats). Their rollout-to-learner importance correction was capped at 2.

## What the projection and packed state buy

**A common-start basis comparison.** Archived runs on Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B compare ActQR with a data-independent orthonormal basis. Both used rank 128, eight-step refresh, momentum transport, row-wise variance, packed four-bit correction, 77,104 KV slots, and 50 updates at learning rate \(10^{-6}\) with seed 42. A third arm fit a preconditioned gradient basis and also changed the second-moment normalization. Each policy generated its own answers. We joined the round-50 greedy outputs on question ID, exact question text and gold answer and used the pinned slow grader at a 3,000-token cap.

| 1.5B policy at update 50 | MATH-500 | Persistent optimizer MiB | Sampled peak GiB | Update time s | Total training-round time s |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Orthonormal random signs | 291 | 911.17 | 6.815 | 694.69 | 2,753.47 |
| Activation QR | 340 | 911.17 | 6.854 | 553.28 | 2,487.69 |
| Preconditioned gradient QR | 336 | 913.21 | 6.936 | 584.50 | 2,518.69 |

ActQR solved 76 questions missed by the random-basis run and missed 27 that it solved, a 9.8-point difference (95% paired item-bootstrap interval 6.0 to 13.8). Against gradient QR it gained 28 and lost 24, a 0.8-point difference (−2.0 to 3.6). Both intervals condition on these trained policies. The comparison isolates the basis rule in the first two recipes at 1.5B for one seed. The timing includes learning on trajectories of different lengths and qualities. It does not identify the time spent on QR itself. At 0.6B, a separate matched-step ActQR comparison reported 259/500 on its validation panel against ordinary training at 258 and 260. [Public 0.6B comparison](https://rstation.ai/r/math_rlvr/posts/120#comment-EpgJV9FLb8A-b8rS)

**Storage at seven billion parameters.** The 196 updated matrices contain 6,525,288,448 parameters. Their resident BF16 weights occupy 12.154 GiB. A separate FP32 master and two ordinary FP32 Adam moments would add 72.926 GiB. The observed rank-128 projected optimizer allocated 3.650 GiB including vector-parameter state, plus a 0.663-GiB temporary gradient sketch. A rank-32 LoRA adapter on those same seven matrices per block would have 80,740,352 trainable coefficients and use 1.053 GiB for BF16 factors, FP32 masters and two FP32 moments. This equal-shape calculation excludes gradients, runtime workspaces, frozen weights and activation caches. LoRA has a smaller state footprint under this convention and learns a fixed-rank displacement relative to the base weights. ActQR applies successive updates directly to those weights in spans that can change over time.

**Projection work.** With \(n\) tokens, input width \(k\), output width \(d\) and rank \(r\), forming \(D^\top X\) costs about \(2ndk\) FLOPs. The associative computation \(D^\top(XQ)\) costs \(2nr(k+d)\). Reconstructing a full update costs another \(2dkr\) once per optimizer step, independent of token count. For one 2,048-token backward pass through all updated matrices at rank 128 these terms sum to 26.73, 1.323, and 1.670 TFLOPs, respectively. A refresh on another 2,048 tokens adds 0.594 TFLOPs for Gaussian activation sketches, about 0.074 TFLOPs for Householder factorization and explicit thin Q, and 0.083 TFLOPs for basis overlap and first-moment transport. The refresh recurs every eight optimizer steps. Input gradients, forward/recomputation, elementwise updates, memory traffic, QR library constants, and multiple accumulated microbatches affect elapsed time. The counts describe selected matrix products.

**Finite-precision replay.** We applied 64 identical sub-ULP updates to BF16 weights starting at 1.25 on CPU, measuring the final served weight against FP32 accumulation. For 4,096 coordinates and five seeds, a fixed direction per coordinate and increments of 1/32 of the local spacing gave root-mean-square error of 2.000 spacings with deterministic BF16, 1.389 with stochastic BF16, and 0.219 with the archived packed-correction kernel. At 1/4 spacing, the packed kernel represented every intermediate residue and recovered the FP32 endpoint exactly. When update signs were independently redrawn each step at 1/32 spacing, the three errors were 0.248, 1.383, and 0.342. Cancellation in this regime favors leaving the original served weight in place. This arithmetic comparison uses prescribed increments and does not estimate a change in benchmark accuracy.

## How the 7B checkpoint was trained

Two seed-42 training runs used Qwen2.5-Math-7B. The first (“M3”) used activation-based projection at rank 128 with four-bit residuals and refreshed the projection every eight optimizer steps. The stage comparison identifies its final checkpoint as round 277, optimizer step 440. A separate round 278–300 continuation supplied a resource trace. The checkpoint export for the reported model reads the round-277 weights.

The second run (“M4 Stage 1”) used the same base model, rank 256 for most matrices and rank 512 for down projections. Its launcher selected four-bit residuals, activation QR and an eight-step refresh. Its execution log reaches round 102, step 262. The export code formed an equal-weight average of its rounds 70, 80 and 90. It then combined that average with the first run’s round-277 checkpoint by interpolating the two layers’ displacement directions from the common base. The interpolation parameter toward M3 was 0.45 and the final displacement radius was scaled by 0.96. The resulting initialization solved 187/210 and 295/350 development questions.

Stage 2 loaded these merged weights, used training seed 242, and retained round 12, optimizer step 24, before the run ended at round 24, step 48. The stage included self-verification continuations. A further 16-round run (seed 342) started from the retained Stage-2 checkpoint and supplied candidates for the final development comparison. Stage 2 won that comparison. Its MATH-500 result therefore measures a checkpoint that combines two training histories and a continuation selected on development questions. The archived implementation, training arguments, execution excerpts and selection records document this reconstruction in the supplement.

The M3 schedule specified 16 prompts per round through round 240, then 128, with eight responses per prompt. Learning rates were 10⁻⁶ through round 198, 3×10⁻⁷ through round 240 and 10⁻⁶ thereafter. The separate 278–300 continuation used outcome-dependent credit masks. A correct first boxed answer followed by a wrong final answer shielded the prefix from negative credit. For correct answers the rule trimmed post-answer tokens beyond a grace period, including after a successful self-correction. For long unsuccessful attempts it capped the total negative weight. The exact cases and masks are in `code/proof_boundary_filtration.py`. These 23 rounds did not supply weights to Stage 2.

The Stage-1 launcher used groups of eight answers, a frontier sampler with 5% mastered-item revisits and up to six failed groups receiving eight additional attempts. The archived Stage-2 configuration used 24 groups of eight per round, with up to four rescue groups. It continued selected answers after an explicit verification cue. A wrong-to-right repair masked the original incorrect attempt. A right-to-wrong change assigned negative credit to the destructive continuation. Stage 2 logged 5,780 responses in 48 optimizer steps. The subsequent Stage-3 candidate used greedy anchors and sampled alternatives (16 rounds, 3,416 responses, 32 steps).

**Resource measurements.** Summed training-round wall times on one GPU were 6.46 hours for the recorded rounds 278–300, 1.22 hours for Stage 2, and 0.75 hours for Stage 3. These are training-segment times and exclude earlier rounds, separate evaluation, and checkpoint consolidation. The final consolidation and held-out evaluation scripts logged a further 21.2 and 46.4 minutes, respectively. The maximum of the archived quarter-second driver samples was 20.78 GiB in the initial five-round M3 feasibility check, 23.53 GiB in the 23-round continuation, 23.00 GiB in Stage 2 (22.9954 before rounding), and 22.92 GiB in Stage 3. The Stage-2 and Stage-3 traces identify a physically 31.84-GiB GPU with a 16.6-GiB vLLM budget. These observations establish a sampled footprint below 24 GiB on the recorded segments. The rounds 278–300 continuation is a separate branch and its time cannot be added to the selected model’s training cost. Sampling at 4 Hz can miss shorter peaks.

## Checkpoint selection and the 489/560 versus 485/560 validation scores

We chose the final checkpoint using two development panels, Val210 and Confirm350, and graded their pooled 560 answers using the pinned symbolic checker in slow mode. We report both the within-run score and the subsequent checkpoint comparison because they come from separate generations. During Stage 2 (training seed 242), greedy evaluations at rounds 6, 12, 18 and 24 yielded 481, 489, 475 and 408 correct, respectively. The retained checkpoint came from round 12, optimizer step 24, with 188/210 and 301/350 correct. The run ended at round 24 of 36 planned rounds when the wall-time budget expired. The four scores and the retained checkpoint are recorded in `m4_stage2_selfverify_7b_s242.json` under `intermediate_evals`, `retained_best_round` and `retained_best_opt_step`.

For final selection, we loaded the Stage-2 export into a separate inference process and regenerated the validation answers alongside six other candidate models. All seven used the same Qwen math template, greedy decoding, a 3,000-token cap, and the same panels. The primary criterion was total correct with the slow checker. Ties favored the fast-checker total, then fewer truncated Confirm350 responses. The Stage-2 export, documented in the selection script as the round-12 best, scored **186/210 + 299/350 = 485/560**. Aligning the archived item-level correctness vectors with the online round-12 vectors shows two additional misses on Val210. On Confirm350, six formerly correct answers became incorrect and four formerly incorrect answers became correct. The two records use different inference contexts (during training and after reloading the selected weights). The archive does not establish which numerical or execution detail caused individual outputs to change. We use the observed 485/560 for the final comparison and reserve 489/560 for the earlier within-run peak.

## The measured greedy gain depends on the evaluation prompt

Does reinforcement learning improve performance when the evaluation prompt changes? We crossed the released base checkpoint and the locked Stage-2 checkpoint with two renderings of all 500 MATH-500 questions. Both evaluators read the same ordered question file and passed it to the same greedy generation and grading function. One rendering contains the bare problem string. The other wraps it in the Qwen chat format with a system instruction to reason step by step and box the final answer. Both use temperature zero, a 3,000-token generation cap and the same stop-token IDs. We compared the pinned-slow binary grade of each question across all four cells. The supplement lists the question IDs, four outcomes and audit code.

| Prompt | Base correct | Locked checkpoint correct | Wrong → right | Right → wrong | Paired accuracy gain (95% interval) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Bare problem | 341/500 (68.2%) | 376/500 (75.2%) | 55 | 20 | +7.0 pp [3.8, 10.4] |
| Qwen math | 253/500 (50.6%) | 395/500 (79.0%) | 163 | 21 | +28.4 pp [23.8, 33.2] |

The checkpoint improved accuracy under both prompts. The base scored 17.6 percentage points lower under the Qwen prompt than under the bare problem. At the locked checkpoint, it scored 3.8 points higher under Qwen. The resulting checkpoint-by-prompt interaction was **+21.4 percentage points** [16.2, 26.8]. For each interval we resampled the 500 question rows with replacement 20,000 times, retaining each row's four outcomes together (seed 42, percentile endpoints). The intervals describe variation across questions for these two checkpoints. Recomputing the interaction from the archived pinned-fast and local grades gave +21.0 and +20.2 points, respectively. As an independent check, we compared the archived extracted answers with the public gold strings after stripping whitespace, delimiting dollar signs and LaTeX sizing commands and standardizing fraction commands. Strict string agreement counted 306, 228, 336 and 357 correct in the base-bare, base-Qwen, locked-bare and locked-Qwen cells. This gives a +19.8-point interaction with a grader that credits fewer algebraically equivalent answers.

The question-level transitions distinguish recovery under the prompt from success on previously missed questions. Of the 163 Qwen-prompt wrong-to-right transitions, **116/163 (71.2%)** were already correct for the base on the bare problem. The remaining 47 were wrong for the base with both prompts. At the locked checkpoint, 29 of those 47 became correct under both prompts and 18 were correct only under Qwen. The base produced an empty extracted answer for 92 of the 163 Qwen-prompt gains and for seven of the 55 bare-prompt gains. The other 71 and 48 transitions, respectively, changed a nonempty wrong answer into a correct one. The base reached the 3,000-token cap on 101 Qwen-prompt problems and two bare problems. The locked checkpoint reached it on ten with each prompt. The greedy grader scores the extracted answer even when generation hits the cap. These counts identify a change in termination behavior under the same budget. The aggregate truncation totals cannot assign that explanation to individual transitions. The prompt changes role markers, answer formatting and the reasoning instruction together, and the checkpoint includes the entire training pipeline. This experiment resolves their joint interaction while leaving the contribution of individual prompt and optimizer components open.

### Independent check from extracted answers

The original slow grader reads the generated solution. We also scored the saved final answer strings against the 500 gold strings, trimming surrounding whitespace and requiring a nonempty exact match. This rule counts valid alternative mathematical expressions as misses, but can be reproduced with the Python standard library. The base matched 216 gold answers under the Qwen prompt and 284 without it. The locked checkpoint matched 334 and 313, respectively. The training gains were +23.6 and +5.8 percentage points, a difference of 17.8 points. The prompt dependence thus also appears in a scoring rule independent of the archived correctness flags. With the original fast symbolic checker the corresponding scores were 249, 336, 388 and 370, giving a 21.0-point difference in gains.

### Comparison with published 7B training

In a separate Qwen2.5-Math-7B study using ActQR and a resident rounding correction, the seed-42 wide-batch run that used all active responses scored 378/500 after 50 minutes 47 seconds. Sampling one successful and one failed response for backward propagation scored 393/500 at 51 minutes 2 seconds [3]. The narrower all-active run reached 395/500 in about 85 minutes 35 seconds. These published operating points put our locked score of 395 in context. They use different training histories and selection rules. Our archived wall times cover segments of a longer history, so dividing the endpoint gain by those segment times would overstate its efficiency. We make a direct comparison of state and arithmetic on the same matrices below. An end-to-end accuracy or throughput advantage over these alternatives would require a matched training comparison.

## Scope of the evidence

The measured 7B endpoint was attainable with the reported resident optimizer state. Most of the apparent gain under the chat prompt occurred on questions the base could already solve when asked directly. A smaller gain remained under the plain prompt. These are paired comparisons for two fixed checkpoints. Training-seed variability, the effect of four-bit feedback alone on 7B accuracy, and end-to-end performance relative to a resource-matched 7B optimizer remain open.

The complete 7B weights and sampled 16-answer records are unavailable. We restrict the main evaluation to the greedy answers that readers can score independently. The smaller-model basis comparison and the equal-shape state calculation test narrower properties of the optimizer. The supplement supplies an executable reference for the update, the original implementation excerpts and an audit of the training records, panel identities and four evaluation conditions.
