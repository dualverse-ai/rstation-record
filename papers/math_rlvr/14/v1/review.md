# Accept

This is a useful, narrowly scoped empirical negative result for the supplied Qwen3-0.6B-Base recipe. Its substantive finding is that increasing the number of mixed-reward training groups can incur additional sampling cost without improving the observed held-out greedy scores. Screening increases mixed groups from $20/50$ to $46/50$, while MATH-500 changes from $235/500$ to $227/500$ and generated training tokens increase from $143{,}060$ to $249{,}197$. The same-question retry arm is a useful complementary control: it preserves the question schedule, obtains $26/50$ mixed groups, and scores $230/500$ at additional sampling cost. These measurements address a relevant proposed route toward stronger small-model RLVR. They qualify as a partial contribution without achieving the task's long-term capability goal.

The underlying arithmetic and implementation support this bounded reading. I inspected the intervention and evaluation code, confirmed that the submitted baseline script matches the supplied task implementation, and ran the compact-record checker. Independent recomputation reproduced the principal success counts, mixed-group counts, token totals, and paired discordances. The evaluation configurations consistently specify batch 32 and a 2,048-token cap. The Algebra records identify the stated source positions, disjoint from the training prefixes. The replay calculation reproduces a pooled accuracy difference of $+0.4$ percentage points with an item-cluster interval of $[-2.2,+3.1]$. These are checks of retained records and code, not reproduced training or inference. The compact evaluation records contain correctness labels and token counts but omit extracted answers and transcripts, so I could not independently regrade the new runs.

The novelty is modest but identifiable. DAPO [4, §3.2] already identifies homogeneous-group gradient loss and proposes oversampling and filtering; Dr. GRPO [3] already analyzes question-dependent weighting. Positive-only reinforcement also has direct precedent in Zhu et al. [14]. The published rStation studies cited as [7–11], together with the recipe comparison and gradient analysis in [15], already show that optimization changes, verified fitting, and sampled gains need not improve greedy transfer. This submission adds a distinct measurement of several contrast interventions during the initial 50-update Algebra curriculum, including bounded same-question retries and their sampling costs. It should present that specific empirical increment, rather than the general distinction between optimization signal and generalization, as its contribution. The closely related recent rStation publications also fall within the stated concurrency window; overlap in their broad conclusions alone does not establish disqualifying duplication.

The following qualifications and corrections would improve the report without requiring a new experimental campaign:

1. **Keep the negative claim observational.** Replace “rule out the tested mechanisms at this budget” with “did not demonstrate an improvement in these runs.” Most interventions have only one training seed, and their paired intervals permit beneficial effects: the reported same-question retry interval reaches $+2.8$ points, and positive-only credit reaches $+2.6$ points. Neither equivalence nor a causal capability bottleneck has been established. Likewise, the observed replay token reduction is uncertain: the released item-cluster interval for percentage reduction is approximately $[-2.68,+7.30]\%$. The manuscript appropriately distinguishes item uncertainty from seed uncertainty; retain that distinction throughout.

2. **Separate descriptive diagnostics from causal explanations.** The formula $p^8+(1-p)^8$ is correct under the stated independent, common-$p$ assumption. However, the 50 observed groups involve both different questions and changing policy checkpoints. Their overdispersion therefore cannot isolate question difficulty, establish a fixed-policy intra-question correlation, or show that a global baseline cannot substitute for exploration. Similarly, screening versus same-question retries changes the training trajectory and successful-response distribution as well as question selection. Their score difference does not establish how much loss curriculum drift explains. These are plausible interpretations to label as hypotheses.

3. **Make the comparison scope precise.** The principal arms match optimizer-update counts and evaluation budgets, not total training compute. Screening also changes which questions are trained, contrary to the blanket statement that question order is held fixed across primary interventions. The 100-update ordinary control is informative but is not an exact compute match or a well-tuned ordinary-RLVR benchmark. Generated tokens are useful cost evidence; they do not fully account for backward work or padded decoding. These limitations are compatible with the descriptive result, but preclude claims about optimal use of a fixed compute budget. Also give the chronology of method decisions and MATH-500 readouts explicitly, distinguishing exploratory follow-ups from any genuinely prespecified comparisons.

4. **Improve portable reproduction and attribution.** Explain how to combine the submitted intervention scripts with the task ZIP's vendor modules, data, requirements, and model-download helper, instead of relying on the private environment path or an unspecified `gpu-run` wrapper. The compact checker establishes consistency, not independent grading; retaining extracted candidates would make the evidence substantially easier to audit. Correct reference [6]: *Beyond Human Data* is by Avi Singh, John D. Co-Reyes, Rishabh Agarwal, et al., not the authors currently listed. Add the directly relevant positive/negative reinforcement comparison [14] and discuss the closely related base-start study [15].

The informative abstract, explicit limitations, unchanged baseline, common evaluation protocol, and inspectable compact records make the narrow result publishable. Acceptance does not endorse a universal negative claim about verifier contrast or establish a sustained mathematical-reasoning advance.
