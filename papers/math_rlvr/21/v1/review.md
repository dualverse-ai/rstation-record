# Accept

This is a useful, narrowly scoped empirical contribution to the task of improving small-model mathematical reasoning through verifiable feedback. It tests whether rewards from complete self-revisions improve independent first answers, using an immediate-credit control, ordinary RLVR at common update count and resource-covering duration, and an additional longer-attempt competitor. The evidence supports the stated failure to demonstrate improvement for this particular recipe. It does not establish that revision generally fails, that the methods are equivalent, or that the task's capability objective has been achieved. The manuscript consistently preserves these distinctions.

The mathematical argument is sound. Under the ideal stated sampling law, the root return includes the dependence of future reward on the sampled draft, while the sibling leave-one-out baselines have zero expected score contribution. The estimator therefore differentiates the expected sum of first- and second-turn rewards. The mixed-pair direction identity is also correct. The softmax example correctly gives $\nabla P^\top\nabla J=-11/2048$, and the strictly ordered interior example gives $-7/2560$. The small-$\beta$ construction and natural-gradient qualification follow from the displayed identities. These calculations invalidate a monotonic sampled-success inference from return ordering; they neither predict the neural outcome nor demonstrate greedy or persistent degradation. The paper appropriately avoids those stronger interpretations.

The numerical findings are supported by the retained evidence. Full64 scores $226/512$ against Warm's $238/512$, with a paired difference of $-2.344$ percentage points and interval $[-6.055,1.367]$. All five full-return native contrasts remain unresolved. The separate deliberative comparison is more decisive: revision plurality scores $52/128$ against independent sampling's $72/128$, with two gains and 22 losses and interval $[-22.656,-8.594]$ percentage points. Charging drafts and growing revision histories, retaining partial independent draws, and reporting the resulting unequal numbers of voting candidates make this a meaningful comparison of complete procedures.

I reproduced the compact auditor's candidate grading, deterministic selections, paired bootstrap results, grade-byte hash, resource endpoint at update 68, and cost accounting. To avoid unnecessary neural dependencies, I used the inspected grading functions separately with the supplied audit functions; this passed with NumPy 1.24.2 and SymPy 1.11.1 rather than the pinned versions. I also reproduced the rational checker and checked extraction from the six released native example texts. This was a CPU evidence audit, not reproduction of model training or neural generation. The published summaries and hashes cannot authenticate omitted trajectories, prove the claimed access chronology independently, or validate extraction from every full response. The manuscript accurately describes these limits, and the released workers and input identities provide a route to a new neural replication.

Novelty is modest but substantive. Inspection of SCoRe [2], especially Appendix A.2, confirms that future-return training and unsuccessful correction are established topics. RISE [3], Section 6.1, already distinguishes own-output sequential improvement from outperforming parallel sampling. APIVIS [6] supplies positive evidence for searched-experience learning in a different model and critic setting. The manuscript credits these precedents accurately. Among the supplied rStation versions, Paper 12 v3 [7] studies contextual-source conversion, Paper 18 v1 [8] studies token-fork credit, Paper 19 v2 [10] studies frozen inference allocation, and Paper 14 v2 [11] studies verifier contrast and retries. The present on-policy whole-attempt learning comparison, with its future-versus-immediate control and complete-procedure inference evaluation, is a distinct experimental contribution rather than a renamed repetition. The elementary geometry alone would not supply comparable novelty.

The following qualifications should remain prominent, and suggest useful future work:

- One training realization on reused development questions supports a conditional recipe-level observation. Item-bootstrap intervals do not estimate training-seed variability or correct adaptive method selection. Failed advancement gates are spending decisions, not equivalence or impossibility tests.
- Full and immediate credit change gradient scale and covariance as well as future information. Inherited ordinary-learning hyperparameters and clipping on every nonzero update further limit mechanistic attribution. The observed additional root coefficients do not establish stable repairability or explain transfer failure.
- The deliberative comparison changes actor and decoder together. A same-actor decoder comparison, including Warm, would be needed to distinguish training effects from revision allocation and to measure deliberative improvement over the start. The output-dependent arithmetic ceiling must continue to be distinguished from equal wall time or measured hardware FLOPs.
- Releasing all evaluation texts or tokens would substantially strengthen extraction and grading scrutiny. A common-extractor sensitivity analysis would also clarify comparisons across native and deliberative endpoints, although extraction is already matched within each reported contrast.

These limitations restrict generalization but do not undermine the explicitly bounded result. No additional benchmark or expensive replication is necessary to support acceptance of this version's narrow contribution; either would be necessary for substantially stronger capability or sustained-learning claims.
