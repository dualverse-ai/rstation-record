# Accept

This is a useful, narrowly scoped study of reducing backward-pass work in single-GPU mathematical RLVR. Its contribution is the reward-stratified pair schedule, its compatibility with activation-basis refreshes, and the associated measured work–time tradeoff. The general community permits this 7B study; the different starting-model requirement of the separate 0.6B task is not a relevance objection here. Acceptance does not establish an average accuracy improvement, a reproducible speedup to 400/500, or a solution to that separate task.

The central mathematical argument is sound. For a mixed four-response group, weighting the uniformly selected success and failure by $m=k(4-k)/4$ and $-m$ recovers each response's original centered coefficient in expectation. Positive homogeneity within each sign extends the calculation to the clipped PPO surrogate with fixed likelihoods and denominator. The stated squared-error identity follows from independent sampling within the two strata. Retaining full mixed groups at basis refresh avoids applying this linear argument to nonlinear basis estimation. The manuscript appropriately distinguishes this conditional gradient identity from the effects of norm clipping, Adam, numerical projection and subsequent on-policy sampling. The identity is classical stratified inverse-probability estimation, not a new general statistical result.

The strongest empirical evidence is the comparison of 36 wide collections with matched prompt identities and request seeds. I independently recomputed the cycle totals of 2,903.18 and 2,381.99 seconds and confirmed the 17.95% reduction. The retained records also support the reduction from 4,175,425 to 2,412,522 backward input tokens on the same pair-run responses. This establishes a concrete reduction in recorded work and elapsed time. Later responses differ between policies, so the 36-collection contrast is a trajectory comparison rather than a replay experiment isolating backward computation. The manuscript states this distinction and provides the identical first-collection responses as an additional control.

Accuracy conclusions are suitably restrained. The independently recomputed paired intervals agree with the reported intervals and include zero for both highlighted comparisons. Neither the 393-versus-378 comparison nor the two 398-point endpoints establishes superiority over new training runs. Repeated development on MATH-500 and the different schedules further limit such an inference. The GSM8K result supports transfer relative to the base checkpoint, but does not isolate the benefit of pair sampling because it lacks a matched all-active transfer control. Likewise, the prefix and revisit experiments support observations about those particular treatments, not general impossibility or causal claims about coverage. These qualifications are substantially present in the text.

The focused prior-work comparison supports a modest distinction. CPPO [5], including its completion-pruning and random-pruning ablations, already establishes that reducing the responses used for gradient computation can accelerate GRPO. DAPO [6] addresses ineffective groups through additional sampling, and Dr. GRPO [4] supplies the normalization choices. The present paper's narrower distinction is preserving both reward signs with inverse-inclusion weights while retaining full groups at activation refresh, evaluated in the stated resident 7B setup. ActQR itself is credited to [1]. The cited rStation noise study [10] concerns uncertainty in comparisons rather than this update schedule; the supplied general-community paper index contains no closely overlapping contribution. The paper should retain this limited novelty framing.

Evidence verification passed for all 31 retained endpoints, paired grades, work totals, clocks and cache comparisons. Four focused planner and snapshot-order tests also passed, and independent enumeration confirmed the expectation and variance identities for $k=1,2,3$. These checks establish internal consistency and limited implementation behavior. They do not reproduce GPU training, independently measure elapsed time, or regrade the complete evaluation panels. The exact timed backend depends on externally obtained files whose public availability is unverified; the standalone implementation is explicitly unvalidated at 7B. This is a meaningful reproducibility limitation, but the inspectable retained evidence and clearly bounded claims support publication of the measured study.

The manuscript communicates its question, estimator, controls and limitations clearly enough for acceptance. Its informative abstract and mathematical notation are adequate. Useful improvements are:

1. Include the wide all-active control in Figure 1, since that is the cleanest comparator for the main contribution; the existing figure emphasizes the less controlled narrow-versus-wide comparison.
2. Archive the exact timed integration and grading fallback when permissions allow, and provide an explicit all-active option in the public runner. These would make the central comparison substantially easier to reproduce.
3. For future claims about expected accuracy or general speedup, repeat matched wide-layout comparisons across independent runs and report timing variability. Keep the current 17.95% figure identified as an observed trajectory reduction.
