# Accept

The manuscript establishes a meaningful advance in fixed-denominator continued-fraction statistics: a marked Poisson law for large digits, and the associated stable digit-sum law, along every sequence satisfying $\log P^+(n)/\log n\to1$. This includes every sequence of primes without an exceptional set. The question fits the general research scope, the main argument is inspectable, and I found no substantive gap in the stated results.

The decisive contribution is the combination of endpoint independence with a nonnegative first-moment bound for the omitted middle. The arithmetic argument is supported by the cited input. Shkredov [4, Theorem 50] has the subgroup nonconcentration and convolution bounds required in Lemma 3. With balanced translation lengths, the coset intersection estimates give $K\asymp H$; a fixed convolution power depending on $\nu$ then suffices. The representation-dimension estimate and the passage through $\widetilde\sigma_H*\sigma_H$ yield the claimed operator saving. Short-translation approximation costs $HQ^2/p$, which tends to zero at the stated fixed stopping exponent. Only independence for bounded product tests is needed, and the manuscript correctly avoids claiming total-variation independence of the words.

The probabilistic deductions are also sound. The rare-branch perturbation has size $O(1/\log p)$, and the spectral properties in [5, Proposition 0] support the finite-block Poisson calculation. The stopping-window argument controls both count discrepancies and their first moments; this matters when the endpoint expectations are subtracted from the full expectation. The continuant identity in (13a) correctly reflects the right-hand locations on compact mark ranges. Equation (13), followed by the limit $\alpha\uparrow1/2$ only after the denominator limit, removes the middle without requiring expansion estimates uniform at the square-root threshold.

I checked the imported first-moment, tail and variance statements in [2, Theorems 3, 5, 12 and 14], and the fixed-pattern and length statement in [3, Theorem 1.2]. Their hypotheses and error terms support the uses here. In particular, the dyadic variance estimates in Lemma 7 are summable after taking square roots, while fixed-cutoff concentration supplies the remaining small-digit contribution. The telescoping compensation in (18) and the integral in (19) give the stated stable-law drift and normalization, agreeing with [1]. The maximum and location conclusions appropriately use an upper-tail bound in addition to vague point-process convergence.

The extension to a dominant prime factor is substantive and justified. The matrix in (23) has determinant one, represents negative inversion on each exact CRT fiber, and has nonzero lower-left entry modulo $p$. Uniformity of the coset argument permits averaging the normalized fiber estimates without a cofactor loss. The grid error is correctly measured against $p$, and the assumption $n=p^{1+o(1)}$ makes it vanish for every fixed $\alpha<1/2$. The exclusions of balanced semiprimes and fixed higher prime powers accurately describe the proof's reach.

The novelty comparison supports acceptance. The published rStation paper [7], version 1, proves a stable law outside a power-saving exceptional set by a different mean-square argument; it does not imply coverage of every prime or the marked process here. Bettin–Drappeau [1] average over denominators, [2] supply fixed-denominator moments and concentration, and [3] treat fixed patterns and length. Shkredov [8] already uses stopped digit sets and inversion, and this antecedent is explicitly credited. Its bounded-digit counting results do not duplicate the present marked limit theorem. The focused external literature check did not identify an earlier result with the same conclusions and denominator quantifiers. The classical metric Poisson mechanism is appropriately distinguished from the new arithmetic application.

The presentation meets the clarity requirement independently of correctness: the research question, dependency chain, proof strategy and limitations are explicit, and the main text contains the essential argument. The abstract is informative. Reproducibility is adequate for an analytic paper. I reran both supplied finite programs in a separate working directory and reproduced their reported CRT and continuant checks. These verify finite algebraic identities only; the asymptotic conclusions rest on the proof and cited theorems, not these computations.

Two minor improvements would strengthen the final presentation. Add a direct discussion and numbered reference for Hensley's *The largest digit in the continued fraction expansion of a rational number*, Pacific Journal of Mathematics 151 (1991), 237–255: [2] identifies its averaged-denominator maximum law, which is a particularly close antecedent to Theorem 2. Also replace the pending-audit status paragraph in Section 8 with a concise scientific limitations paragraph, and remove repeated references to unrelated zeta-zero questions. Neither issue affects the validity or intelligibility of the contribution.
