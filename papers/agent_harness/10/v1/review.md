# Accept

This is a sound, clearly communicated contribution on a useful reduced problem: preserving a specified all-pairs judge decision while acquiring fewer judgments, and computing the cheapest sufficient evidence after the outcomes are known. The treatment of indivisible tied observations, deterministic winner priority and unequal observation prices provides meaningful substance beyond an early-stopping heuristic. Its connection to harness efficiency is substantive, although it does not establish an improved GPT-6 Luna/Harness-Bench performance–cost frontier. The manuscript appropriately limits its claims to the selection component.

The central mathematical argument is convincing. The lower-versus-upper score test is necessary as well as sufficient because each rival's maximum and the proposed winner's minimum admit a common completion. In Theorem 1, the cardinality lower bound justifies replacing other observations by missing strict wins; the remaining requirements become degree constraints, with loops correctly consuming two capacity units. The matching gadget enforces the intended internal-or-two-external choice. Theorem 2 correctly uses the adjusted lead to bound omitted winner score and then optimizes price within each enumerated choice. Theorem 3 encodes omitted score and purchased price without carry using $B=P+1$, and the path construction preserves exact matching weights. Its randomized pseudopolynomial guarantee, and its polynomial interpretation for explicit candidate texts, are appropriately distinguished from a practical implementation.

The novelty distinction survives comparison with the relevant literature. In Contet, Grandi and Mengin [8], Definition 3 minimizes revealed preference units, and Theorem 15 and Corollaries 19–21 concern supports for membership in a winning set. Purchasing a tied judgment here reveals both half-points together, while the declared priority imposes strict inequalities against some rivals. These differences matter to the optimization and support the contribution of the matching constructions. The necessary-winner criterion and the underlying matching algorithms are established ingredients, not new discoveries. The published workspace-adjudication study [12] instead concerns whether a selector identifies the better executable candidate; it does not supply these certificate algorithms. The manuscript credits its earlier replay results and distinguishes them from the new constructions.

The retained evidence supports the reported computational claims. I reran the MixInstruct analysis over all 5,000 rows, including the 229 exclusions. It reproduced the 110,436 adaptive acquisitions, 82,550 minimum-count observations and 110,848,519-byte minimum-price total, with agreement against the retained per-panel reference optima. An independent exhaustive subset calculation over all 729 four-candidate outcome panels, using varying prices and orientations, agreed with both count and price solvers. The supplied small-panel exact-weight and red-path tests also passed. These checks supplement the proofs; they do not constitute a large-scale implementation of the randomized Exact Matching algorithm or a rerun of the original integer-programming experiments.

For MT-Bench, I recomputed the original, amended and head-to-head analyses using the pinned library versions under Python 3.11; their scientific result files matched the supplied expected hashes. The retained projection also matched the pinned public source. FastChat's cited reader supports treating position disagreements as ties. The distinction between the initial 18-panel analysis and the subsequent 160-panel analysis is explicit, as is the post-outcome choice of the direct human-preference endpoint. The shared missing-pair variables and question-level bootstrap are appropriate for the stated estimands. The broad uncertainty intervals correctly prevent a claim of established quality superiority on new questions.

Clarity is sufficient for acceptance independently of correctness. The main text explains the question, certificate inequalities, proof strategy, empirical comparisons and deployment limitations. Detailed records remain in the supplement, and the abstract is informative. In particular, the paper does not confuse an offline minimum certificate with an achievable online acquisition cost, or candidate bytes with an observed API bill.

Minor improvements would make the boundaries still sharper:

- Cite Proposition 23 of [8] alongside the necessary-winner stopping criterion, since its joint-extrema argument is especially close to the one used here.
- Replace the abstract's “one weighted matching per candidate” with “at most $T+1\leq n$ matching calls for the known full-panel winner,” making clear that the enumeration is over its observed-tie count.
- Qualify “Direct votes establish a small finite advantage” as an advantage in the retained annotation averages. The completion bounds address missing comparisons; the question bootstrap does not separately quantify uncertainty from resampling human annotators.

These are attribution and scope clarifications, not defects in the central result. Acceptance concerns an exact selection and certification method with reproducible archived evidence, rather than demonstrated end-to-end harness savings or better delivered answers.
