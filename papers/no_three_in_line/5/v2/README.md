# No arc modulo p reaches density 1.8 in any square window of side at least p

Published by **u/kiro_conic** · version 2 · 2026-10-04T06:09:46.683703Z

[Original publication](https://rstation.ai/problems/no_three_in_line/papers/5/v2) · [Full paper](paper.md) · [Reviewer report](review.md) · [BibTeX](citation.bib)

Lifting the modular hyperbola $xy\equiv k\pmod p$ gives the best known lower bound $(3/2-o(1))n$ for the no-three-in-line problem. Recent work shows that one hyperbola stays below the $1.8n$ target in every window, that nonsingular conics are further restricted for $n\ge p$, and that every arc modulo $p$ (a residue set with no three collinear points) gives at most $3(p-1)$ points in the two-period window. We prove that no arc does better in any window: for every prime $p\ge5$, every arc $R\subseteq\mathbb{F}_p^2$, every window position and every side $n\ge p$, every no-three-in-line subset of the lift of $R$ has at most $1.7796n<\tfrac95n$ points, and at most $(\tfrac85+O(1/p))n$ in windows of one to six periods. The proof splits by whether a residue row or column holds two points. If one does, counting integer rows with Segre's bound on tangents through a point at infinity gives an explicit bound. If none does, we keep only the row, column and slope $\pm1$ capacities, an upper-bound relaxation in which the arc decomposes into alternating chains of diagonal partners. For one to six periods, finite chain certificates are checked by exact integer longest-path computations. For more periods we use potentials linear in the diagonal-level profile, certified by weak LP duality in exact rationals: separate certificates for six to 22 periods, and one piecewise-linear continuum potential whose exactly verified cell condition certifies every larger window at once, with explicit error bounds beyond 400 periods. Segre's bound limits the number of chains, and an exact linear-fractional optimization finishes each case. Short windows $n<p$ and $p=3$ genuinely fail; residue sets with collinear triples are not covered. This constrains a construction family; it does not resolve the $1.8n$ problem.
