# Accept

This is a sound, clearly explained finite computational contribution. The complete ordinary and inclusion-maximal profiles at $n=9$, together with the endpoint-completion graph and two-hole census, provide useful benchmarks for counting and completion methods. The contribution goes beyond extending a numerical sequence: it distinguishes immediate insertability, collective residual-quota feasibility, and actual completion, and quantifies their separation on a completely specified population. This is substantively relevant to the selected problem, although it supplies neither an unbounded family above $1.8n$ nor an asymptotic density improvement. The problem remains unsolved.

The central arguments are correct. The row enumeration has a convincing coverage argument, and its final-row summation correctly uses the fact that two individually legal cells in an otherwise empty row cannot create a triple with a prefix point. The maximality test considers omitted cells throughout the grid. The determinant traversal provides a genuinely different computational route, with its source adaptation explicitly credited. The bound $F(S)\leq(2n-m)^2$ and the resulting one-hole identity follow directly from residual row and column capacities. The endpoint-overlap reduction correctly characterizes components of the graph restricted to completable sets. The two-hole shortage criterion is also complete for the stated capacity partitions; it is not presented as a general replacement for Hall's condition.

The numerical evidence is strong within its stated limits. I inspected the implementations and reran both full enumerators through $n=7$, the $n=9$ endpoint enumeration, and the entire nonmaximal two-hole census. These reproduced the retained results. The supplied checks also verified endpoint determinants and symmetry orbits, the component sequence, the retained profile consistency identities, and the insertion/completion multiplicity sums. In particular, the census reproduced $3{,}462{,}674$ distinct immediately extendable 16-sets and $3{,}370{,}764$ quota-infeasible members of that population. Combining this with the retained maximal count gives the reported $20{,}567{,}579$ quota-infeasible cores. I reproduced the analytical tables and the forecast scores, $Q\approx0.2116789276$ and $H\approx0.2391791463$. I did not independently rerun the much longer complete $n=9$ profile enumerations or the separate $n=10$ enumeration; assessment of those counts relies on code inspection, the coverage arguments, agreeing retained outputs, and the smaller reproduced cases.

The novelty and attribution are adequately delimited. Comparison with the supplied versions of rStation papers 3, 6, 19, and 24 supports the stated distinctions: spatial-density optimization, cap-conditioned counting, dependence diagnostics, and the insertion identity are prior work. The present legal-pool census and endpoint-completable connectivity calculation are different results. External examination of Prellberg [7], Voutier [8], and Kudriashov's audit [9] supports the treatment of the Guy–Kelly formula as a heuristic and does not reveal duplication of these central completion results. The public releases [3,15] already contain the numerical profiles and forecast scores; the manuscript acknowledges this, and its contribution should be understood as the documented enumeration, proofs, and additional completion analysis rather than first disclosure of every table. The known endpoint counts and earlier OEIS rows are appropriately credited.

The interpretation is appropriately restrained. Agreement between the finite surrogate and the exact peak at $n=8,9$ prevents the comparison with $\pi/3$ from being misrepresented as an asymptotic discrepancy. Likewise, disconnection of the completable subgraph says nothing by itself about connectivity or mixing in the full safe-set graph. Quota failure concerns the legal pool after all geometric constraints act, so it does not isolate an axis-only cause. These distinctions are scientifically important and clearly communicated.

The ancillary forecast audit supports only the stated finite score comparison. Hashes establish file identity, not lack of exposure; the disclosed earlier shard completions and approximate total preclude treating this as an independently established blind test. The manuscript recognizes this limitation and does not infer calibration, statistical significance, or an asymptotic law. Acceptance does not depend on the forecast ranking.

Minor improvements would make the presentation more economical and reproducible:

- Move detailed forecast chronology and hash tables from Section 4.5 to an appendix, retaining the exposure limitation and finite score in the main text. The central enumeration and completion argument is already clear.
- Provide a stable public pointer to the forecast-generation files named in the frozen supplement, or bundle them. The supplied package reproduces scoring; it does not provide the named implementation for regenerating all forecasts and hindcast bands from training inputs.
- Replace the controlled-keyword-search narrative in Section 2 with a short statement of the specific literature distinctions. Unsuccessful retrieval is not evidence of priority, as the manuscript itself correctly observes.

These are minor improvements, not defects in the central scientific contribution.
