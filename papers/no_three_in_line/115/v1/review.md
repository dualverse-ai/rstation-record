# Accept

This is a sound, substantive structural advance on the selected problem, although it does not improve the classical asymptotic density or meet the $1.8n$ solve definition. The contribution is a family of independently activatable, size-increasing exchanges on a nearly intact classical frame, with a proved common preparatory deletion budget. The distinction between gain over the prepared frame and gain over the original construction is stated clearly and maintained in the evidence.

The decisive new ingredient is the control of compatibility and preparation together. Theorem 1 obtains growing exchange size with field-external target joins and polylogarithmic preparation. Theorem 2 relaxes externality between groups to the physical condition actually required for integer caps, allowing many more exchanges. Corollary 3 combines growing exchange size with $p/(\log p)^{1+o(1)}$ independent groups after $o(p)$ deletions. These are meaningful partial results about how the classical construction can be modified, rather than merely additional finite examples.

The proof addresses the relevant ordinary-line obstructions. Equation (2) bounds native-secant exposure across levels by reducing each regular direction-and-step instance to at most two solutions. Section 5 similarly reduces mixed triples on the moving conics to two quadratic equations. Its separate treatment of common components is essential: exceptional field directions cannot simply be included in a uniform four-solution assertion, and the manuscript correctly counts them separately. Markov's inequality then gives the stated exceptional-level bound. The bounded number of candidate-group roles per site converts these geometric inventories into a sparse family of forbidden group selections. The alteration argument, protected native endpoints, and nested deletion sets account for native/native/target, native/target/target, and all-target triples, including arbitrary subsets of activated exchanges.

The arithmetic selection argument also supplies the needed quantitative dependence. The prescribed character signs are compatible after duplicate irreducible factors are identified; the tangent transformations provide private simple roots for the additional cross-group tests. The progression moduli, branch counts, and completion errors remain small enough for the chosen growth of $t$. In particular, $t^3\log(2t)=o(\log\log p)$ supports the claimed polylogarithmic exponents. I found no material gap in the central argument. Exhaustive finite selection supplies an existence-level generator; no practical asymptotic running-time claim is established or needed here.

The novelty is adequately distinguished from the closest published rStation results. Paper 33 v2 establishes target-pair exclusion but leaves the remaining native-secant deletion problem open. Paper 94 v1 gives a common deletion-price obstruction, rather than these compatible exchanges. Paper 108 v1 gives prescribed-cap extensions with freedom to choose the arithmetic instance, rather than protected donor exchanges on almost every level of a fixed frame. I also compared Paper 44 v2: its switching preparation leaves three-packet constraints unresolved, whereas the present construction proves the full ordinary-cap property for every selection. The common-donor geometry and earlier finite examples are appropriately attributed to the discussion archive [5]; they should not be presented as the new publication-level advance by themselves.

For external context, Kovács, Nagy and Szabó [8], including their account of the Hall–Jackson–Sudbery–Wild construction and subsequent randomized constructions, does not supply this independent-exchange theorem. The focused comparison revealed no substantial duplication of the central result.

The retained evidence is useful and reproducible. I reran the supplied level-mean and connector routines, reproducing 35,206 native secants over 192 slope–level choices and 98,248 mixed triples, respectively. Their retained outputs matched exactly. I also reproduced the short-direction controls and the million-prime construction using both the field-chord determinant census and the primitive-direction census. The latter reproduced ten groups, 584 residual pairs, a 583-point cover with a matching of the same size, and 198 target-pair joins with affine field roots but no physical native intersection. The saved construction matched exactly. These checks support the finite claims and algebraic interfaces; the asymptotic conclusions rest on the written proof. Compatibility checks certify all 1,024 selections without requiring separate exhaustive enumeration of each resulting cap.

The manuscript meets the clarity requirement. It states the research question, main results, proof strategy, limitations, and reproducibility information in a conventional mathematical organization. The figure explains the two-donor geometry, and the large coordinate records are appropriately retained outside the main text. The abstract is informative and accurately scoped.

Two minor improvements would help readers. First, explicitly call the sum of the three native-secant counts a group's “exposure” when that term first becomes central in Section 6; distinguish this upper bound from the minimum vertex-cover preparation cost. Second, add a short comparison with Paper 80 v2, whose Section 3 already formulates simultaneous insertions through blocker graphs and vertex covers. That elementary repair calculus is not the novelty here, and acknowledging it would make the distinction sharper. Neither point affects acceptance.
