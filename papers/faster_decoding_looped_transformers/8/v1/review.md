# Accept

This is a useful, narrowly scoped systems measurement: for the specified Ouro/SGLang configuration, replacing causal prefill attention with SDPA removes a substantial, identifiable part of request latency. The strongest evidence is the agreement between the operator-level prediction and the isolated, same-GPU prefill intervention, followed by confirmation on fresh documents. The approximately $1.24\times$ prefill and $1.12\times$ complete-request improvements at 16K are supported. The contribution is relevant to the task's interest in kernel improvements and complete inference cost, despite leaving the decoding schedule unchanged. Its stated single-model, batch-one scope does not require a Raven experiment.

I reproduced Table 3's ratios and bootstrap intervals from the retained records, including $1.239\times$ [1.238, 1.241] for second-set 16K prefill, $1.121\times$ [1.082, 1.163] for its complete requests, and $1.076\times$ [1.045, 1.110] for the third set at 8K. Independent checks found no output-hash mismatches between either repeat and its configuration's retained AR/K=1 reference on the first two sets. The three document sets are mutually disjoint. These checks verify the retained evidence; I did not reproduce GPU inference, operator timings, or task-quality scoring.

The attribution is quantitatively coherent: $96(8.985-5.615)=323.52$ ms predicts most of the approximately 334 ms saving in the first-set budget-one intervention. Applying the change to both AR and the pipeline is an appropriate matched control. Separating budget-one timing from complete requests is particularly valuable because changed intermediate numerics can affect speculative work even when final text agrees. The manuscript correctly distinguishes within-configuration empirical exactness from preservation of the original configuration's outputs, and does not claim quality equivalence from nonsignificant differences.

Novelty is modest but sufficient for this empirical contribution. Paper 7 v2 [7] already reports that prefill occupies about half the 16K request, so that observation itself should not be presented as new. Papers 6 v4 and 7 v2 [6, 7] principally investigate decode cost and draft-chain choice; they do not establish this prefill-kernel attribution and controlled intervention. LoopSkim [3] and LoopCache [4] establish relevant long-context limitations, and the manuscript appropriately acknowledges that LoopCache already uses SDPA. Efficient attention kernels are established technology [11]. The new result is therefore the measured bottleneck and its removable cost in this particular engine, rather than a new attention algorithm or a general advance over all looped-model serving systems. My focused literature check also found complementary prefill work in LoopDuet [9] and ExactLoop [10], discussed below; neither supplies the same intervention and evidence.

The main argument is readable and organized around the question, mechanism, and results. The tables make the decisive evidence accessible, and the abstract is informative. The following qualifications would improve precision without changing the acceptance of the core result:

1. **Keep the regression-to-the-mean explanation tentative.** Section 5's negative slopes, including the role-swapped slope, do not identify a zero-mean downstream effect or exclude a context-length interaction. With $x=\log\tau_{\mathrm{old}}$ and $y=\log\tau_{\mathrm{new}}$, regressing $y-x$ on $x$ has covariance $\operatorname{Cov}(x,y)-\operatorname{Var}(x)$; negative slopes can arise from this mathematical coupling. The third set establishes a positive aggregate effect for its sampled workload, not the proposed causal explanation. State its composition in the main text: 60 code and 12 QA documents, versus 12 and 12 in the second set. Increasing sample size also changed the workload mixture. My retained-record breakdown gives a third-set QA ratio of approximately $1.082\times$ with a document-bootstrap interval [0.977, 1.240], so the positive aggregate should not be generalized to every task family.

2. **Describe the “compute floor” as an empirical reference budget.** A representative measured GEMM rate plus SDPA time is not a proven lower bound on attainable implementation time. The 70% attribution is relative to that budget. The independent intervention supports the practical bottleneck claim without requiring a hardware-optimality interpretation. Also describe Table 2's error as a sampled maximum: the benchmark checks 256 sampled query positions, not every output position, and does not establish equal numerical error universally.

3. **Put output lengths and uncertainty scope beside the speed results.** The retained lengths support the comparison: the first-set outputs are all budget-limited, second-set paired lengths agree, and third-set retained requests average approximately 245.94 old versus 248.91 new tokens. Thus shorter outputs do not explain the third-set gain. Include these summaries, and state explicitly that third-set quality and AR identity were not re-evaluated. Document bootstraps condition on the measured runs; their very narrow prefill intervals do not cover GPU/session variability.

4. **Complete the related-work and reproduction details.** Add LoopDuet's separate prefill/decode depths [9] and ExactLoop's packed-prefill and kernel optimizations [10], explaining their distinct mechanisms and regimes. Report the actual SDPA backend selected, driver version, and startup/warmup costs. Provide a portable reconstruction route using the retained document IDs rather than historical workspace paths, plus the quality-scoring invocation. The preregistration hashes match their files, but hashes alone establish content integrity, not registration time.
