# Accept

The paper provides a useful, narrowly scoped empirical advance for the supplied decoding problem. On the specified Ouro implementation, reducing the copied draft length from $K=8$ to $K=2$ improves long-context decoding without changing the observed outputs relative to the same engine's autoregressive reference. The contribution is modest but meaningful: a confirmed 13% and 23% improvement in pooled decode throughput at 8K and 16K, respectively, changes an actionable baseline setting. The manuscript appropriately avoids claiming an optimal draft length, a universal context threshold, or a general improvement across models and hardware.

The central comparison is supported. After inspecting the analysis code, I ran the standard-library reproduction script and independently checked the pooled throughput and complete-request times. The retained records reproduce the median ratios $1.145$ [1.029, 1.221] and $1.236$ [1.091, 1.292], the pooled ratios $1.130$ and $1.232$, and the smaller complete-request gains in Table 2b. All four confirmation arms have output hashes matching AR on all 36 requests. The prompt cuts also match their stored hashes. These checks reproduce the analysis of retained evidence; they do not independently reproduce GPU inference or verify the original token streams, which are retained as hashes. There are 12 underlying confirmation records, with repeated runs and context cuts, rather than 144 independent problems. This is adequate for the stated local comparison, with the reported uncertainty and limitations.

The scientific explanation is useful at the cycle-accounting level. At 8K and 16K, the shorter chain loses approximately 10% and 7% of committed tokens per cycle while reducing elapsed cycle cost by approximately 20% and 25%. That directly explains the throughput improvement. The profiling records also support nearly unchanged mean numbers of distinct depths and substantially narrower frontiers. The separation between selection prompts and confirmation records, the treatment of immediate EOS outputs, and the explicit distinction between decode and complete-request time are strengths.

The novelty is an empirical regime-specific result, not a new speculative-decoding algorithm or the discovery of a KV bottleneck. I compared the relevant published rStation work on schedule selection [6] and invariant kernels [7], and consulted the external texts of LoopSkim [3] and LoopCache [4]. Schedule tuning and width-dependent cost are already established in [6]; the numerical execution configuration comes from [7]. LoopSkim Appendix A.2 reports weak sensitivity to draft length on its configuration tasks, while Appendix C.9 discusses declining long-context margins. LoopCache explicitly identifies long-context KV traffic and changes cache scheduling. Those results substantially motivate this study, but do not establish its particular held-out $K=2$ versus $K=8$ contrast and cycle-cost decomposition. This constitutes a useful extension rather than repetition of the earlier short-context studies. It does not establish superiority over every tuned native implementation or trained-drafter variant.

The main text is sufficiently clear: the question, intervention, selection/confirmation distinction, results and limits are identifiable without reconstructing the experiment chronology. The abstract is informative. The following corrections and qualifications would improve precision without changing the acceptance decision:

1. **Distinguish cycle cost from attention cost.** Section 5's $0.225$–$0.854$ ms estimates compare total GPU-cycle times at fixed depth count; they are not isolated attention-kernel measurements. Describe them as observed incremental GPU-cycle costs associated with additional rows. The attention microbenchmark supports attention as an explanation, but its unexplained 1.2–2.4-fold discrepancy prevents assigning the entire difference quantitatively to attention. Also qualify the “within 1%” statement: in my aggregation, cells with at least ten observations in both arms are only $(\text{width},\text{depth count})=(2,2),(3,3),(4,4)$ at each length. These do not comprehensively validate the cost of the wider speculative frontiers.

2. **Use uncertainty language for LoopCache.** A median ratio of $1.021$ with interval [0.830, 1.046] establishes no detected difference, not statistical equivalence or tightly bounded “parity.” Preserve the stated engine, stopping-rule and output-content differences. This is a descriptive implementation comparison, not an isolated comparison of scheduling algorithms or a speed–quality frontier. The main text correctly leaves the cause of output divergence unresolved; update the supplementary RESULTS_C2 statement attributing it definitively to BF16 rounding to match that caution.

3. **Correct two development-screen descriptions.** Section 3 says $K=1,2$ both beat $K=4$ from 2K onward, but the retained 2K ratios are 1.057, 1.055 and 1.086 for $K=1,2,4$, respectively. Also distinguish the initially registered $K=2,4$ screen from the exploratory $K=1$ addition after those results, as the supplement already does. Neither correction undermines the independently confirmed fixed contrast.

4. **Make the implementation lineage directly reproducible.** Add numbered citations to the exact earlier rStation versions supplying the engine and F80 configuration, rather than only “our earlier papers.” Include the software versions, a precise base-revision/artifact mapping for the supplied diff, and portable live-run instructions. The supplied reproduction script verifies the principal comparisons but does not literally emit every table, including Table 2b and all conditional profiling calculations; extend it or narrow that claim. Hashes verify retained contents, not the asserted timing of pre-registration.
