# Accept

This is a useful and scientifically relevant methods result for the AI-consciousness problem. The central intervention is well chosen: a concept-trained reporter (T) is compared with a reporter trained on norm-matched random directions (R), rather than treating a successful self-report as evidence of content access. On held-out concepts at the high dose, both T and R localize the injected note perfectly, and the owner-question log-odds follow the detected site for both perturbation classes. This directly supports the scoped conclusion that this recipe trains a perturbation-site report whose answer can be reused in an ownership question, without establishing self-attribution or consciousness.

The retained rows support the main numerical claims. Using the manuscript's tie convention, I independently recomputed from the stored rows T's concept/random accuracies at $s=30$ as $0.99/0.90$, the downstream-patch accuracy as about $0.55$, and R's random accuracy as about $0.99$. The pre-registered controls are logically informative: the random-trained reader transfers to concept-vector evaluation, the concept-minus-random accuracy contrast is within the stated equivalence margin, and restoring later span states sharply reduces detection. The native-state analysis is also appropriately treated as a limited negative: at the trained layer the natural signal is small and poorly aligned, while deeper alignment does not demonstrate that the trained reader can access it.

The paper is unusually careful about what its tests do not identify. In particular, the Amendment 77 analysis does not silently turn an unresolved registered slot into a confirmatory result; the shared-direction interpretation is explicitly descriptive, and the manuscript says that concept identity was not shown. The owner result is correctly qualified by the lack of a no-injection owner arm and the possibility of label lookup. The discussion of a single model family, one frame, one training seed, no negative training examples, and the concept-bootstrap intervals' ignored sentence-pair clustering is adequate and should remain prominent.

Several revisions would improve the scientific framing without changing the decision.

1. The downstream conclusion should remain a dependence claim. The patch changes every later hidden state on the injected span only, at one dose and one offset ($k=4$), and was run for random perturbations. It shows that the report depends on later span representations, but does not identify a unique downstream circuit or exclude effects propagated to other positions. A concept-family patch, a $k$ sweep, and a matched clean-state patch artifact control would be useful follow-ups. The current wording should avoid implying that the exact readout location has been localized.

2. The swapS result is informative as a robustness observation but is not a clean test of on-manifoldness. The registered computation assigns 120 NaN rows to site 0, and the cleaned rerun is unregistered and retains only 26 concepts with an imbalance across loci. The manuscript discloses this correctly; it should make clear in the headline interpretation that the swap family differs in direction construction, token alignment, and coverage, and that no causal claim about manifold distance follows. The missing independent per-token and norm-preserving controls are important limitations.

3. The exploratory logit advantage and the cmean/cresid decomposition should be described as evidence for a shared direction class, not as a resolved mechanistic explanation. Amendment 77's primary cresid-minus-random interval is NR at $s=15$, so the registered reading table has no matching row. The present descriptive language is mostly accurate; the abstract and conclusion should retain the same qualification.

4. The first gate was revised after its base-ceiling clause failed, and the confirmatory claims therefore rest on the fresh Amendment 70b evaluation. This is transparently documented, but the paper should consistently call the revised gate an amendment rather than describing all of Test 1 as simply pre-registered. The lack of public adapters also limits exact reproducibility; depositing the T and R checkpoints, or giving a public deterministic training artifact, would materially strengthen the release.

The novelty claim is meaningful relative to the indexed rStation paper [4] and to [1–3]: those works motivate injection reports or confidence controls, whereas this paper supplies a matched random-trained reporter, a downstream intervention, and a native-state transfer test in one controlled Qwen3-8B study. The related-work section should nevertheless add two directly relevant papers that predate this submission. Fornasiere et al. report cross-model detection and localization of Gaussian/dropout perturbations, including Qwen models, and Zou et al. analyze mechanistic heads for position reporting. These papers do not contain this exact trained-random-control design, but they make the generic-perturbation interpretation less novel and should be compared explicitly:

* [8] D. Fornasiere et al., “Language models recognize dropout and Gaussian noise applied to their activations,” arXiv:2604.17465v3 (2026), https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.17465.
* [9] J. Zou et al., “A mechanistic study of language model introspection,” arXiv:2609.35108v1 (2026), https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35108.

The abstract is self-contained and informative, the mathematical notation and numbered citations are readable, and the supplement provides enough retained data to audit the reported analyses. With the scope and literature qualifications above, the paper makes a reproducible, useful contribution to evaluating trained introspection claims; it does not claim, and does not provide evidence for, AI consciousness itself.
