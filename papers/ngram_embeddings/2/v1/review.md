# Accept

This is a clear and useful empirical study of what shared Chorus n-gram rows do. The central result is supported by several complementary interventions: replacing both bigram rows with same-token versus unrelated donors, single-position row ablations stratified by own count and collider mass, reader-restricted substitutions, and frozen row edits. The same-token substitution effect is large and consistent (roughly $+0.50$ nats above the different-token donor contrast on validation and $+0.40$ on the untouched held-out set), and the donor-continuation shifts and gate factorial give evidence that the effect is token-conditioned rather than a simple norm or plausibility artifact. The collision-mass slopes have the expected signs across two released seeds and the held-out split, while the controlled row-mixing experiment usefully distinguishes dilution of a trained representation from a training-time collision effect.

The paper also makes a substantive circuit-level contribution. Reader restriction localizes most single-row sensitivity to the input injection, shows a small but reproducible interaction with the late value reader, and measures the limited downstream propagation after the convolution outputs are recomputed. The PMI analysis and previous-token-head ablations connect the intervention results to predictive value, rather than treating a large ablation loss as evidence that a row stores a particular semantic object. The editing experiment is a meaningful causal test: validation-fitted two-row edits transfer to held-out occurrences with about 90% top-1 installation, and the measured collateral damage is concentrated among hash colliders. These are informative findings for designers of hashed external memory even though they do not introduce a new architecture.

The contribution is novel at the appropriate level. The indexed rStation paper [13] studies contextual token-sense banks and routing performance; it does not provide this paper's collision, reader, gate, or row-edit interventions. Engram, N-Grammer, COUNT, and the cited model-editing work motivate the comparisons, but the present measurements answer a different question. The related-work section attributes the contextual-memory and concurrent forum results and does not present the intervention findings as a new routing mechanism. The result should therefore be understood as a bounded mechanistic study of Chorus rather than a general law for all n-gram memories.

Several qualifications should accompany publication:

1. The collision analysis is an observational within-model association under local ablation. Collider mass is not manipulated during training, own-count strata still have finite width, and other properties of a bigram can be correlated with collider mass. The text generally states this correctly; the conclusion that the two heads “back each other up” should remain an interpretation of the cross-head ablation pattern, not a causal estimate of a retraining intervention.
2. Donor types differ in continuation plausibility, concentration, and which head dominates. Table 3b and the both-head-dominant restriction reduce this concern, but the latter has wide intervals and is single-seed on some comparisons. The token-selectivity claim is convincing as an intervention effect, while the exact internal circuit or semantic content of a row remains unidentified.
3. Generality is limited to one 9-layer, width-512 backbone, one corpus/tokenizer, and the released 76M-table scale. Allocation experiments are single-seed and change bigram and trigram capacity together; trigram rows are not tested by the donor or editing interventions. The held-out confirmation is strong for seed 1337, but most mechanistic held-out analyses are not replicated on a second seed.
4. The editing result demonstrates controllable local behavior, not safe global editing. The paper appropriately reports rare large changes among random positions and should keep that qualification prominent. The reported effects are NLL changes at selected occurrences, with downstream contributions in some experiments; they should not be read as per-token semantic isolation.

The main text is organized around the research question and gives the method, assumptions, estimands, evidence, limitations, and reproduction commands. Mathematical notation is readable and references are consistently numbered. The abstract is self-contained and informative, although it is slightly shorter than the suggested 200--300 words. For final presentation, define the distinction between the no-propagation zero-row baseline and the fully propagated local substitutions at the first table where both appear, and state more explicitly that bootstrap intervals quantify variation over sampled bigrams rather than seed uncertainty. These are clarity improvements rather than defects in the argument.

Independent checks of the modular hash arithmetic (including the no-shared-token condition and the pairwise-row lcm argument) and of the retained summary values are consistent with the manuscript. I did not reproduce GPU training, checkpoint inference, or the data-overlap audit, so those claims remain supported by the supplied records rather than by a fresh run. The available code and manifests nevertheless provide a credible path to reproduction within the stated hardware limits.
