# Nine colors after augmenting the first-generation pentagon Hajós joins

## 1. Two ways to augment a nine-critical construction

Write $Q=K_6\vee C_5$, the join of six pairwise adjacent *hubs* to a five-vertex *rim*. To form a Hajós join, take two copies, delete a directed edge $xy_i$ in each, identify the two copies of $x$, and add the edge $y_1y_2$. Call $x$ the root, the $y_i$ the tips, and $y_1y_2$ the bridge. Each parent contributes ten private vertices. Up to parent symmetries there are four directed edge types, according as the two ends are hubs or rim vertices. There are ten unordered pairs of types. The resulting graphs have 21 vertices and 99 edges.

**Theorem 1.** Every biplanar spanning supergraph of any of these ten constructors has chromatic number nine. Also, if two intact copies of $Q$ meet at exactly one vertex and cover a 21-vertex biplanar graph, the union with arbitrary extra edges has chromatic number nine.

A graph is biplanar if its edges partition into two planar layers. These layers may use different placements of their vertices. Biplanarity is required of the final graph, and its decomposition can change when edges are added. The numerical motivation is a published completion of one constructor in which two triangulations give 114 edges on the same 21 vertices [2]. In this example a deleted edge of one parent has been restored. Theorem 1 allows both restorations, chords on either rim, and arbitrary private edges across the two sides.

The same labeled example admits a second result when new vertices are added. Let $F$ denote its original 99-edge seed and let $T$ be the explicit 121-edge graph specified in Section 4.

**Theorem 2.** For $F\subseteq H\subseteq T$, adding any two new vertices to $H$ gives chromatic number nine whenever the result is biplanar. If three added vertices form a triangle, every biplanar extension with at least 106 old edges has chromatic number nine. Two new vertices of degree nine on the triangle suffice at any degree of the third and any old edge count in the interval.

The classical construction and its use in producing nine-critical thickness-two graphs are due to Boutin, Gethner and Sulanke [1]. A precoloring theorem for these ten individual constructors requires a prescribed set to be cofacial in both layers and keeps their edge sets fixed [3]. A two-vertex theorem for a different labeled source assumes two independent new vertices with disjoint neighborhoods in its core [4]. Here the first result covers all completions at fixed order, and the second allows the two new neighborhoods to overlap and their vertices to be adjacent. We draw on the order-twenty nine-color theorem [5], the seven-clique obstruction [6], the twelve-vertex catalogue [7], and the classical nonbiplanarity of $K_9$ [8]. Our conclusions do not require an independence-number bound or a prescribed drawing. The preliminary proofs and labeled certificates appeared in discussion 86 [9].

## 2. Matching the private colors

The intact $Q$ needs nine colors. In eight colors a deleted edge's two ends must coincide, since otherwise its restoration is proper. This forces both tips to take $x$'s color in any eight-coloring of the join, contrary to the bridge. An intact copy gives the same lower bound by itself. We prove the upper bound in Theorem 1. If any vertex has degree at most eight, apply [5] after deleting it and extend greedily. Assume throughout Sections 2 and 3 that every vertex has degree at least nine. Euler's bound is 114 edges.

First leave both deleted edges absent. Color each parent with eight colors, with its tip and root alike. This is possible even with the added rim chords. For a deleted hub edge, merge its ends. The complement on the rim is a subgraph of a five-cycle and has two disjoint edges. Otherwise its edges all meet a single vertex (a pairwise intersecting family of edges is a star or a triangle), and four rim vertices form a clique. Together with five mutually adjacent hubs these give $K_9$. The two nonedge pairs and a singleton color the rim. For a deleted spoke, merge its ends. The four other rim vertices contain a spanning four-vertex path and are complete to all six hubs. A triangle there would yield $K_9$, so their path coloring survives any added edge. For a deleted rim edge, the surviving rim path has five vertices. An added edge of even path distance would join the two ends, which is the missing marked edge, or create a triangle. Hence the alternating coloring survives. In each case all eight colors occur on private vertices. Name the tip's class 0 independently on each side.

Let $m$ be the number of edges between the private sets, including the bridge, and $d$ the number of added rim chords. Then $m+d\le16$. Use the private classes on the first side as eight rows and those on the second side as eight columns. Each cross edge forbids one cell. If the seven nonzero columns can be matched to distinct rows in allowed cells, give them the corresponding row colors. Give the root and column 0 a ninth color and use the original eight colors on the private rows. This is a proper coloring. The roles can be reversed.

If both matchings fail, Hall's theorem gives a rectangle of $i$ nonzero columns against $9-i$ rows, and a rectangle of $j$ nonzero rows against $9-j$ columns. The bridge is outside both. The possible sizes are $i,j\in\{1,2,7\}$. Including the bridge, the union needs at least

$$
1+i(9-i)+j(9-j)-\min(i,9-j)\min(9-i,j)
$$

edges. In the order $1,2,7$ this gives the matrix with rows $(16,21,16)$, $(21,25,15)$ and $(16,15,25)$.

**Without chords.** For a parent with a deleted hub edge or spoke, two nonzero private classes are disjoint rim pairs, and each of their four vertices needs a cross edge to reach degree nine. For a deleted rim edge the private class 0 is the tip and the middle path vertex. Their degrees seven and eight within the parent require at least three cross edges at that class. Two size-one witnesses use all sixteen edges, one per occupied cell. Then row 0 has only two edges and every nonzero row except the full row has only one. Either parent type on the row side contradicts its required incidences.

The matrix leaves a size-seven witness. Orient it as seven nonzero columns against two rows $r,s$. Reserve its fourteen edges, one per cell, and the bridge. At most one more edge exists. Column 0 has at most two contacts, so the column parent has two nonzero rim pairs. At least one of them receives exactly the two reserved edges. Each of its members needs a contact, so one meets $r$ and the other meets $s$. Give each the other's row color. Keep column 0 and the root on the ninth color. The six remaining nonzero columns can be matched to the other six rows while avoiding at most one extra edge.

**With chords.** Now $m\le15$. The matrix forces $m=15$, $d=1$, and a two-by-seven rectangle of fourteen distinct contacts, with the bridge outside. Indeed the two Hall rectangles each have area fourteen, and hence must coincide. One parent is chord-free. If it had a deleted rim edge, its class 0 would require three contacts. In this common rectangle all selected rows and columns are nonzero, so only the bridge meets class 0. Thus the chord-free side has two nonzero rim pairs. If it is on the seven-class side, split one pair as above. If it is on the two-class side, both pairs must be the selected rows, since every member requires a cross edge. Keep the eight private colors on the column side and give the root and row 0 a ninth color. Color one selected pair with private column color 0. The contacts of the other pair partition the seven nonzero column colors into two nonempty sets, so give each member a color from the other's set. The other five rows take the five unused column colors. This treats all cases with both deletions retained.

## 3. Restorations and two intact parents

An intact parent permits no rim chord, since a rim triangle and its six hubs would give $K_9$. With $x$ alone on the ninth color it has eight private classes. For a hub root use five singleton private hubs and rim classes of sizes $2,2,1$, with any chosen rim singleton. For a rim root use six singleton hubs and the two alternating pairs on the remaining four-vertex path.

**One restoration.** Here $m+d\le15$. Choose the intact side as the rows so that every individual opposite vertex misses some row. To see this when $x$ is a hub, at most one opposite vertex can have eight private contacts. If it hits every private hub it must miss a rim vertex, which we choose as singleton. Contact with all ten private vertices would give a twelve-vertex subgraph with at least sixty edges and at least five universal hubs, ruled out by [7]. When $x$ is on the rim, an opposite vertex seeing all six hubs and any rim vertex $t$ would give the seven-clique of the hubs and $t$ three common neighbors, the opposite vertex and both rim neighbors of $t$. This is forbidden by [6].

Use the eight-coloring of the still-deleted parent from Section 2 and reserve color nine for $x$ and column 0. A failed matching of the seven nonzero columns forces $i=1,2$ or 7. For $i=2$ or 7 the fourteen contacts and the bridge consume the entire budget. There are no rim chords, and the two necessary pair columns exist because class 0 has only the bridge. If $i=7$, split a pair as in Section 2. If $i=2$, both pair columns must be the selected columns. Give one pair the single row color outside the rectangle. In the other pair each member can take a color seen only by the other, since its seven contacts are distinct and both members require one. The other five nonzero columns take five unused colors.

For $i=1$ the full column is a pair. Its two members have nonempty disjoint sets of missed row colors. Give each a color it misses. After accounting for the eight contacts of this column and the bridge, there are at most six edges on the remaining six columns. At equality, each of the eight row colors contacts exactly one of the pair. Choose a row hit by one of those six remaining edges as the split color for the member that misses it. After both split colors have been used, at most five forbidden cells remain on six rows and six columns. Hall gives a matching.

**Two intact copies.** Now $m\le14$, with or without a bridge. On each hub-rooted side the five rim vertices each have positive cross degree. Choose a rim vertex of maximum cross degree as singleton. If a remaining pair has total weight at least eight, positivity and the total bound fourteen force the five weights to be $4,4,4,1,1$. Some two heavy vertices are adjacent. Make the third heavy vertex the singleton, so the adjacent two are in different pairs. All pair rows on a hub-rooted side now have weight at most seven.

For an eight-by-eight perfect matching a Hall rectangle of area at most fourteen is a full row, a full column or a two-by-seven rectangle in either direction. A private singleton is adjacent to $x$ and cannot see all opposite private colors. Such contact would meet all six opposite hubs and at least three vertices of its rim, counting the root where appropriate. Some two of those three rim vertices are adjacent, giving a $K_9$ with the outside vertex and the hubs. A full row must therefore be an alternating pair on a rim-rooted side. Move its member nonadjacent to $x$ to the ninth color with $x$. The other member is adjacent to $x$ and misses a color. A full row could not have coexisted with another obstruction within fourteen cells, and the move only removes forbidden cells. This also treats a full column by symmetry.

A two-by-seven rectangle consumes all fourteen contacts. Its two rows cover every private rim vertex on their side, since each needs a cross edge. A hub-rooted side has five such vertices, so the two rows are the alternating pairs on a rim-rooted side. Move either pair's internal path vertex to the ninth color. That vertex has a contact, and every cell contained exactly one contact. All rows and columns were incomplete and fewer than fourteen forbidden cells now remain. A perfect matching colors the graph. This proves Theorem 1.

## 4. Additional vertices on a labeled template

For the rest of the paper the left hubs are $0,\ldots,5$ with cyclic rim $6,7,8,9,10$. The right hubs are $11,\ldots,16$ with cyclic rim $0,17,18,19,20$. The seed $F$ deletes $01$ and $0\,17$ and has bridge $1\,17$. Define $T$ by adding to the 114-edge graph of [2] the pairs $(3,11),(4,11),(4,14),(5,12),(6,17),(8,15),(10,14)$. The supplement supplies both source graph6 strings. Only the actual extension of $H$ is assumed biplanar.

**Local incidence lemma.** Suppose $z$ is external to $Q-e$ and sees ten of its eleven vertices. Among the marked edge types, biplanarity is possible exactly when $e$ is a spoke and $z$ misses the hub of that spoke. Retain the 49 parent edges and ten spokes at $z$. In the complement, the rim nonedges form a five-cycle $C$. The two additional complement edges are $e$ and the edge from $z$ to its sole miss. The only 59-edge catalogue entry whose complement contains a five-cycle is row 9, a five-cycle with a pendant two-edge path and five isolated vertices [7, Table 1]. A marked hub edge stays in a separate component from $C$. A marked rim edge makes a chord of $C$. For a marked spoke its hub must be the miss to give the required path, and then the catalogue supplies a realization. In particular any external vertex in our labeled interval meets each parent at most nine times, by taking any ten of its incidences.

**Reserving two colors.** Let $R=\{0,11,\ldots,20\}$ and $A=\{1,\ldots,10\}$. For each of the 49 mandatory right-parent edges $e$ there is a word on $T$ whose only monochromatic edge in eight colors is $e$. With two nonadjacent new vertices missing the same $v\in R$, choose $e$ incident with $v$ and give $v$ and both new vertices the ninth color. This also proves the one-new-vertex conclusion for every biplanar subgraph of the cone over $T$, even if the seed is dropped. If a mandatory right edge is missing use its word directly. Otherwise the new vertex must miss a right vertex, since seeing all eleven would give sixty edges with a universal vertex on twelve vertices [7].

For new vertices $p,q$ let $X,Y$ be their respective missed sets in $R$. A seven-color word on $T-(U\cup\{a,s\})$, with $U\subseteq X$, $a\in A$, $s\in Y\setminus U$ and $U\cup\{a\}$ independent, implies the following. If $p$ misses $a$, one fresh color goes to $U\cup\{p,a\}$ and the other to $\{q,s\}$. Thus a counterexample must have the edge $pa$. Each of 505 supplied seven-color words gives such implications in the applicable orientations. Count distinct forced neighbors $a$ at each of $p,q$. With $h$ the indicator of $pq$ and $c=22-|X|-|Y|$, the thirteen vertices $R,p,q$ impose $c+h\le17$. The full graph allows at most $27-h-c$ incidences into $A$. The parent lemma also applies on the left. On the right, a vertex that sees all six hubs misses the three internal vertices of the mandatory rim path by [6]. Two adjacent vertices seeing those six hubs cannot have one also seeing both path endpoints, by the same seven-clique obstruction.

There are 2,014 possible individual missed sets after the individual right-parent tests. With $p,q$ nonadjacent it suffices to examine the 67,749 unordered disjoint pairs. The two Euler tests leave two pairs, which require respectively eleven and ten left-parent neighbors at one new vertex. For adjacent $p,q$, the 2,029,105 unordered pairs reduce to 82,627 after the Euler tests and three after the left-parent bound. In each of the three both vertices see the six right hubs and one sees both path endpoints. This proves the two-vertex assertion. Appendix B gives the entire counting rule and executable replay.

**The new triangle.** A new vertex of degree at most eight extends greedily from the two-vertex theorem. Suppose two new vertices $p,q$ have degree nine, so both have seven old neighbors, and call the third vertex $z$. It misses at least two right-parent vertices. Use nine-color words of $T$ in which one of these misses occupies a color alone, and give that color to $z$. Failure requires both seven-sets to avoid that old singleton, use seven different colors each, and use the same seven colors. For each possible pair of misses, enumerate seven-sets with these properties in every supplied word rooted at either miss. Forbidden cliques and forced neighbors of $z$ reduce 1,228 records to eight unordered pairs of seven-sets. Five involve consecutive left rim vertices. There is a reserved color class for the old singleton $0$ and for every private cross nonedge of $T$ in each of these five cases. If $z$ sees $0$, Euler on the left parent with the three new vertices forces at least five missed private left vertices. The two missed private right vertices have at most three common private left neighbors in $T$. Some reserved pair is therefore missed at both ends. The other three cases use the same argument on restricted sets or force the absence of $0\,17$ by a nine-clique. Appendix C gives their lists and words.

For new degrees $(9,10,10)$ the old neighborhoods have sizes $(7,8,8)$. In a fixed old nine-coloring the low vertex has at least two usable colors, and each high vertex has at least one. Suppose a high list has at least two colors. Every pair of lists then has union of size at least two. If the high list and the low list together have at least three colors, Hall extends the triangle. Thus each high set must use at least seven colors, and whenever it uses exactly seven the low set must use those same seven. Apply this necessary condition to 57 explicit words. Of 203,490 eight-sets, 586 survive. Five are always rainbow and the other 581 give 604 compatible low–high pairs. Applying the two forbidden subgraph tests on the seed plus the prescribed incidences leaves 119 triples in all, including fourteen with one always-rainbow high set. In every triple the triangle is complete to $\{0,12,13,15,16,17\}$, where $0\,17$ is the sole nonmandatory old edge. Its restoration would give a nine-clique. The supplement supplies a proper nine-color word on $T-0\,17$ plus each prescribed triangle. Appendix C details the tests on the five always-rainbow sets and the final enumeration.

The sum of the three new degrees is at most $135-e(H)$ by Euler on 24 vertices. Once degrees below nine, two degrees nine, and $(9,10,10)$ are excluded, the least remaining sum is thirty. This proves Theorem 2. At 105 old edges, the only profiles still possible for a ten-chromatic candidate are $(9,10,11)$ and $(10,10,10)$, and both layers must be triangulations.

## 5. Evidence and scope

Theorem 1 is a hand argument conditional only on the cited small-order and forbidden-subgraph results. The finite part of Theorem 2 uses direct comparisons of digits on specified edges and enumeration of explicit finite sets. Appendix A gives the parent-incidence classification and an explicit labeling in the exceptional drawing. Appendix B covers the two-vertex checks, and Appendix C the triangle. Run the two Python commands in the supplement README with assertions enabled. Each checks positive witnesses directly. The triangle's 119 final colorings require only endpoint comparisons once the neighborhoods have been derived.

The ten constructors in Theorem 1 concern the original 21 vertices. The hypotheses on $F,H,T$ in Theorem 2 apply to one labeled constructor. New private contacts outside $T$, further iterations of the Hajós operation, and further triangle degree profiles remain possible directions for a construction. The Earth–Moon problem remains open.
