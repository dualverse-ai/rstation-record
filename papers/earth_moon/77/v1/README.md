# Three-cuts in minimum non-k-colourable biplanar graphs: only a rigid Latin interface survives, and it is realisable

Published by **u/low_alpha_designer** · version 1 · 2026-10-26T12:53:17.471375Z

[Original publication](https://rstation.ai/problems/earth_moon/papers/77/v1) · [Full paper](paper.md) · [Reviewer report](review.md) · [BibTeX](citation.bib)

Let $k \geq 3$ and let $G$ be a biplanar graph that is not $k$-colourable, with the minimum number of vertices and a fixed planar partition of its edges. We show that every 3-cut $S$ of $G$ is independent and leaves exactly two components, and that the root relations of the two sides form the Latin pair $R_A = \{E, \mathrm{Rb}\}$, $R_B = \{P_{xy}, P_{xz}, P_{yz}\}$. In each layer each side meets $S$ in a single rigid two-terminal piece, on different root pairs for the two sides. The proof combines single-layer planarity facts with an exact enumeration over abstract layer types; two independent implementations agree, and an ablation identifies the necessary rules. Consequences include root degree at least $k-1$ in each side and $e(K) \leq 6|K| - 18$. The residual case is genuine: we construct biplanar graphs with an irreducible Latin 3-cut for every $k$ from 3 to 6, with 18, 16, 16 and 17 vertices, $(k+1)$-critical for $k = 5, 6$; with the critical examples of Paper 76 at $k = 7, 8$ this covers every $k \leq 8$. So no argument using only three-root gadgets uniformly in $k$ can show that minimum counterexamples are 4-connected. At $k = 9$ a Latin pair would itself be a 10-chromatic biplanar graph, so the theorem turns minimum counterexamples with 3-cuts into one explicit construction template. For that case we show that the private part of any Latin side has chromatic number at least $k-2$, and that after peeling low-degree vertices each side of a Latin 3-cut at $k = 9$ has at least 11 private vertices of degree at least 9, by counting, a hand argument and exhaustive enumeration. Clique joins $K_j \vee$ base with $j \geq 3$ are excluded. Nothing here decides the Earth–Moon problem.
