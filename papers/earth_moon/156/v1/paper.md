# Preserving prescribed colors in a biplanar completion with interchangeable triangulations

## 1. Main result

The Earth–Moon problem asks whether a finite simple graph consisting of two planar layers can require ten colors [1]. A graph is biplanar if its edge set has a disjoint partition into two planar spanning subgraphs. The drawings of these layers can place the vertices differently. A sphere triangulation here is a finite simple graph with a drawing on the sphere whose complementary faces are triangles. Distance and unqualified degree refer to the union of both layers.

**Theorem 1.** Fix a biplanar graph $G$ with a specified partition and an integer $r\geq0$. There are a sphere triangulation $T$ and a fixed-point-free involution $\tau$ of its vertex set such that $T$ and $\tau T$ have disjoint edges. The union $H$ induces the two given layers on a designated copy of $G$. For each palette with $k\geq4$ names, every proper coloring of $G$ extends to a proper coloring of the same $H$ constant on the pairs $\{v,\tau v\}$. The minimum union degree is eleven, and each layer has minimum degree at least five. Within distance $r$ of either of the two designated copies of $V(G)$, the degree in each layer is at least six.

Consequently a ten-chromatic biplanar graph exists if and only if some sphere triangulation and some fixed-point-free involution give a ten-chromatic union as above. Given an example requiring more than ten colors, delete vertices until the chromatic number first reaches ten. The theorem preserves that number by extension and induced containment. The involution can be fixed in advance by giving its orbits consecutive pairs of labels. This reduces the two planarity requirements of a construction search to a single triangulation and a condition on its edges. A proof that such unions always admit ordinary nine-colorings would resolve the original question.

For comparison, a classical planar two-split replaces each vertex of an abstract graph by at most two representatives and realizes each required adjacency on some pair of representatives. Heawood already realized twelve mutually adjacent empires of size two [2]. Eppstein et al. discuss this construction and the extremal triangulation property [3]. Our theorem begins with a specified biplanar graph, retains both induced layers and preserves every prescription from the stated palettes. In Section 6 we exhibit ordinary seven-colorings where identifying each partner pair gives a twelve-clique. For a positive nine-chromatic control we use the 17-vertex core of Paper 155 [4].

## 2. Completing one layer while retaining prescribed colors

Pad the old vertex set $V$ to order at least four with isolated vertices. Complete each planar layer independently to a simple plane triangulation on $V$. Such an augmentation follows by taking an edge-maximal simple planar supergraph on the same vertices. Mark the old edges introduced in each augmentation. In a simple sphere triangulation of order at least four the two triangular faces incident with an edge have different third vertices. Indeed, if both face boundaries were the same triangle, those two disks would exhaust the sphere. Delete a marked edge. Its two former faces now bound a disk with four distinct vertices $x_0,\ldots,x_3$ in cyclic order.

Triangulate this disk as follows, with indices modulo four. Introduce a four-cycle on $y_0,\ldots,y_3$. Join $y_i$ to $x_i$ and $x_{i+1}$. Inside that cycle put adjacent vertices $s,t$, joining $s$ to $y_0,y_1,y_2$ and $t$ to $y_0,y_2,y_3$. The strip contributes eight triangles. The inner disk has four triangles incident with its four boundary edges and two on the edge $st$.

For any names assigned to the $x_i$, choose four palette names including all names used there. If the four boundary names are distinct, give $y_i$ the name on $x_{i+2}$, then use the names on $y_3,y_1$ for $s,t$ respectively. Otherwise give $y_0,y_2$ the same unused name $q$. For $i=1,3$ choose for $y_i$ any name outside the names on $x_i,x_{i+1}$ and $q$. Each of $s,t$ now has at least two available names, so choose distinct ones. All nineteen added edges are proper, even if consecutive boundary vertices have the same name.

Perform this replacement at each marked edge in order, keeping the other old edges until their own turn. Use a disjoint set $A$ of fresh vertices in red and $B$ in blue. Given a proper prescription on $G$, color the padding arbitrarily and then color the disks in their insertion order. Only temporary old edges can violate properness at an intermediate stage. They are all gone at the end. This yields two triangulations on $V\cup A$ and $V\cup B$ and compatible colorings whose intersection is the fixed prescription on $V$.

## 3. Independent face prescriptions on a common vertex set

We construct a pair of edge-disjoint triangulations on a fresh set $F$ supporting $h$ mutually independent requests for ordered colors on faces. Specify a layer for each request and take $m=128h$ rows, $h\geq2$. Row $i$ is a triangle with vertices $(i,t)$, $0\leq t\leq2$. Between consecutive rows in linear order join all pairs with unequal second coordinates. Each strip is the six lateral triangles of an octahedron. The two end triangles cap the sphere. Obtain the blue triangulation by relabeling $(i,t)$ as $(i+3t\bmod m,t)$. A red edge has cyclic row distance at most one. Its blue image has row difference $3(t'-t)+\epsilon$ modulo $m$, where $t'\ne t$ and $|\epsilon|\leq1$. Its cyclic row distance is between two and seven. Thus the layers are disjoint, and $t$ properly three-colors the union.

Request $j$, starting at $j=0$, uses the red face $(128j,0),(128j,1),(128j+1,2)$ or its blue image. Its rows lie in $[128j,128j+7]$ modulo $m$. The closest two such intervals have cyclic row distance at least 121. Changing row by at most seven per edge gives graph distance at least eighteen between any two selected faces.

Initially color the three coordinates with three fixed distinct palette names. Any ordered target triple of distinct names is reached from that initial triple in at most six changes of one coordinate, always to a currently unused name. Fix the coordinates in order. If another coordinate holds the desired name, move its occupant to a spare name first. Previously fixed coordinates already have their final distinct names. Write $P_0,\ldots,P_\ell$ for the resulting sequence. A vertex at distance $d$ from the selected triangle takes its own coordinate's entry of $P_{\max(0,\ell-d)}$. Adjacent vertices have different coordinates and distances differing by at most one. Entries in different coordinates remain distinct across consecutive triples since the new name was unused. The coloring changes only within distance five. No two such supports meet or have an edge between them. This prescribes all selected triples independently, including for $k>4$.

For every $b\in B$ request a red face of $F$ and insert $b$ in that face, joining it to the three corners. Similarly request a blue face for every $a\in A$ and insert $a$ there. Request one further face in each layer for attachment. In red join any face on $V\cup A$ to the red attachment face by an octahedral annulus, retaining both boundary triangles. In blue join a face on $V\cup B$ to the blue attachment face in the same way. More explicitly, label the three corners of each end $0,1,2$ and join across all unequal indices. Two facial disks removed from distinct spheres and joined by this annulus give a sphere triangulation. Either triangle may be indexed in any fixed order since we may orient its sphere when gluing.

Every layer now spans $V\cup A\cup B\cup F$. A common edge avoiding $F$ would have both endpoints in $(V\cup A)\cap(V\cup B)=V$, where the given partition is disjoint. An edge inside $F$ is a scaffold edge, and the two sets there are disjoint. Every edge crossing into $F$ in a given layer enters a corner of a request in that layer. All request faces have disjoint vertex sets. This covers every edge, even when a replacement vertex is also used as a corner of an attachment.

Fix the coloring of $V\cup A\cup B$ from Section 2. On each insertion face prescribe three different names avoiding the name of its center. On each attachment face prescribe the same three distinct names as on the opposite end in corresponding index order. Every cross edge then has different names on its ends. The simultaneous facial prescription colors the scaffold. This entire construction is independent of the given prescription. Two more requests, one in each layer, may be kept as intact faces $A'=(a_0,a_1,a_2)$ and $C'=(c_0,c_1,c_2)$. Prescribe the same three names to them in their indicated order.

Three colors cannot give this universal extension property even for an edgeless input on three vertices. In a properly three-colored triangulation, a word on a face determines the third name across each of its edges. The dual graph is connected, so all proper three-colorings agree up to a global permutation. Two old vertices can be prescribed either equal or different in the edgeless graph. A single triangulation cannot realize both.

## 4. Exchanging the two layers

Make two copies of the pair $X=(R,B)$ of Section 3, indexed by $0,1$. Exchange the copies by the involution $\tau$. Let $T$ consist of $R^0$, $B^1$ and the six edges $a_i^0c_j^1$ for $i\ne j$. The reserved faces and these edges form the same octahedral annulus, giving a sphere triangulation. Use $\tau T$ as the other layer. Within either copy disjointness was already proved. The two sets of cross edges are supported on $A'^0,C'^1$ and $C'^0,A'^1$, respectively. The sets of corners of the two reserved faces are disjoint. Thus the cross edges have disjoint supports. Copy the coloring of $X$ to both copies. Its prescribed equality of the two ordered facial triples makes every cross edge proper, and the coloring is invariant under $\tau$.

## 5. Placing all degree-five vertices outside a prescribed neighborhood

We first describe a pair with two independently prescribable facial triples. Set $a=100+7r$, $m=6a$. Make $m$ six-cycles with vertices $(j,i)$, $0\leq j<m$, $i$ modulo six. Between successive rings join $(j,i)$ to $(j+1,i)$ and $(j+1,i+1)$. Cap each end with a distinct vertex adjacent to its six ring vertices. The ring strips and caps triangulate the sphere. The twelve end-ring vertices have degree five. All other vertices, including both caps, have degree six. Put $t=i+j\bmod3$ on the rings and a fourth name on the caps. This is proper because ring edges change the residue by one and inter-ring edges by one or two.

For the second layer keep the cap labels and send $(j,i)$ to $(j+3a+3t\bmod m,i)$. The shift preserves residues and is a permutation on each fixed $i,t$. Red ring edges have cyclic row span at most one, and blue ring edges have cyclic row span between two and seven by the same difference argument as in Section 3. The blue cap-neighbor rows are $3a-1,3a,3a+2,3a+3,3a+5,3a+6$. These avoid the red end rows $0,m-1$. Thus the layers share no edges and no vertex has degree five in both. All vertices where a layer degree is five belong to the displayed sets of cap-neighbor rows.

Use the red face $(a,0),(a,1),(a+1,1)$ and its image in blue. Their rows lie in $[a,a+1]$ and $[4a,4a+7]$. Either interval has cyclic distance at least $a-6$ from the cap-neighbor rows. Until reaching such a row, a path cannot visit a cap and advances at most seven rows per edge. Every fresh vertex with a layer degree of five is consequently at distance at least $\lceil(a-6)/7\rceil=r+14$ from the selected faces. The two sets of selected rows have cyclic separation at least $3a-7$, and a path between faces through a cap uses at least $r+15$ edges to and from that cap on each side. The two distance-five recoloring regions of Section 3 are thus separated with no edges between them and avoid all cap neighborhoods. Using that same argument on the three ring residues gives independent ordered triples on the two selected faces for all palettes of at least four names.

To raise the degrees of an old vertex $v$, select an incident face $v,b,c$ in each layer. Take a fresh copy of the last pair with selected corners $i,j,k$ in the corresponding layer. Join the two faces by $vi,vj,vk,bk,ck,ci$. The six triangles are $vij,vjk,vbk,bck,cki,cvi$. Their boundary components are the two old triangles, so these edges triangulate an annulus, increasing the degree of $v$ by three in that layer. In each layer prescribe at $i$ the name on $b$, at $j$ the name on $c$, and at $k$ a palette name unused on $v,b,c$. These three distinct names obey the six cross edges. The fresh faces have disjoint vertex sets, which excludes cross-layer edge coincidences even when the old faces share corners.

Let $S$ be the entire starting graph from Section 4. Process the orbits of its vertices once in a fixed order. If either layer degree at $v$ is below six, carry out an attachment at $v$ and its image at $\tau v$ on an additional copy with layers exchanged. The two required old faces in one layer are distinct: $v$ and $\tau v$ cannot be adjacent, since their edge would be fixed by $\tau$ and shared by the layers. Copy the first extension to the exchanged attachment. This preserves the involution and the invariant coloring. At each center both layer degrees were at least three and both rise by three. Every vertex of $S$ finishes with both layer degrees at least six. Every fresh vertex began with degree pair $(5,6),(6,5)$ or $(6,6)$, and later degrees only increase.

At its attachment, every vertex with fresh layer degree five is farther than $r$ from $S$. After a path's last entry into the copy it must travel from a selected corner to a degree-five vertex. Subsequent attachments preserve old distances: the two old ends of an excursion into a fresh copy lie in the closed neighborhood of its center, and returning through that center takes at most two edges. In particular the asserted distance holds in the finished graph. The graph is finite because each starting vertex centers at most one attachment. Euler's bound gives average union degree $12-24/|V(H)|$, so some vertex attains the lower bound eleven. This proves Theorem 1.

## 6. Checked examples and scope

A labeled 43-vertex triangulation pair containing the nine-chromatic parent of [4] already has two disjoint faces with matching three-color words in its stored nine-coloring. Doubling on the red face $(9,39,0)$ and blue face $(6,15,22)$ gives an invariant nine-coloring on 86 vertices. The union has 504 edges and each layer has 168 triangular faces. The induced core gives the matching lower bound. This particular example precedes the degree-raising step and has minimum union degree seven.

On two orbits of size two, a representative edge of $T$ forces its opposite edge in $\tau T$. Edge disjointness requires that $T$ never occupy opposite cells of the $2$-by-$2$ table between the orbits and never join partners. Thus the union between a pair of orbits is empty, a matching, or the complete bipartite graph on those two pairs. An orbit-constant proper coloring is exactly an ordinary coloring of the orbit graph. Heawood's planar two-split of the twelve-clique gives one representative edge per unordered pair on 24 vertices [2,3]. Indeed selecting one such edge for every pair gives 66 edges, attaining the planar bound. This forces a triangulation. Its partner image is edge-disjoint. For ordinary coloring, either representative may choose its own name.

Our two labeled examples with complete orbit graphs have parameters
$$
(|V(H)|,\, |E(T)|,\,\chi(H/\tau),\,\chi(H))=(20,54,10,7),\quad(24,66,12,7).
$$
At 20 vertices a seven-clique is $2,5,6,9,11,14,16$. At 24 vertices there are exactly 36 independent four-sets and no independent five-set. A six-coloring would partition all 24 vertices into six independent four-sets. Take the least remaining vertex, branch over every available four-set containing it, and recurse on the uncovered vertices. Every partition would survive its own branch at each step. None does, in 105 distinct subproblems. The supplement supplies a seven-color word and a checked rotation in both cases.

The theorem characterizes a class in which the unrestricted problem can be studied. Extracting a vertex-critical subgraph can lose the layer exchange and the degree properties. A large chromatic number of the orbit graph likewise gives no corresponding lower bound for ordinary colorings. The examples give strict gaps of three and five respectively. The existence of an ordinary ten-chromatic biplanar graph remains open.

## 7. Reproduction

The supplement supplies the labeled edge lists, rotations, color words, three replay scripts and expected outputs. The standard-library four-isolate replay constructs both full layers, orients all faces, checks connectivity of the dual and every vertex link, and checks all final edges for each of the 15 equality patterns of four input names. The result has 28,492 vertices, 85,470 edges per layer, 56,980 triangular faces per layer, and zero shared edges. The disk alone admits 1,920 extensions summed over 256 boundary words. The scaffold replay with NetworkX checks the 3,602-vertex pair at $r=0$, including the 24 vertices of degree eleven, the face distance 52 and the distance 26 to each cap. It checks all 24 ordered target triples at each face with disjoint nonadjacent supports, covering all 576 simultaneous choices. The involution replay uses only the standard library and verifies both sphere rotations, the partner map, the selected-face doubling and the orbit examples, including the exhaustive six-color obstruction. Commands and dependency versions are in the supplement.
