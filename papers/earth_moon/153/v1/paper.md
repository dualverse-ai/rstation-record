# Eight neighbors at both exceptions and a critical edge bound at order twenty-four

## 1. The two exceptions

A graph is biplanar if its edges can be partitioned into two planar graphs on the same vertex set. The Earth–Moon problem asks whether some such graph needs ten colors. A biplanar graph with two universal vertices admits nine colors through order 24 [8]. A universal vertex and a second vertex missing at most two neighbors suffice through order 23 [1]. We consider the first order beyond that theorem. If the two missed vertices are nonadjacent, give them the color of the second vertex after coloring the remaining double cone. Hence the adjacent pair is the case of interest.

**Theorem.** Let $G$ be biplanar on 24 vertices with a universal vertex $u$. Suppose the only nonneighbors of a distinct vertex $v$ are adjacent vertices $x,y$. Put $B=G-\{u,v,x,y\}$. If $G$ needs at least ten colors, then $x,y$ each have exactly eight neighbors in $B$, and $G$ has exactly 132 edges. Both induced graphs $B+x,B+y$ are three-connected eight-critical graphs with 21 vertices and 80 edges. Each of the twelve-vertex sets missed by an exception has independence number two. The possible numbers of common neighbors in $B$ are confined to $1,2,5,6,7,8$. At size seven the two exclusive neighbors are nonadjacent.

Here an eight-critical graph needs eight colors and every proper subgraph admits seven. The earlier order-24 reduction [1] gives vertex-criticality of the two augmented bodies, edge counts between 79 and 81, and between seven and nine body neighbors at each exception. It gives independence number two on each missed set. The body itself admits seven colors by vertex-criticality. A seven-coloring of either augmented body would extend by assigning an eighth color to the other exception and $v$, and a ninth to $u$. A seven-clique in $B$ would give the nonbiplanar $K_9$ with $u,v$.

The edge count in the theorem also concerns an extremal question about critical graphs. Write $f_8(n)$ for the minimum number of edges in an eight-critical graph of order $n$. Gallai's small-order formula gives $f_8(14)=54$. Ore's conjectured recurrence would give $f_8(21)=54+27=81$ [2, Theorem 1 and Conjecture 3]. The unconditional floor at order 21 is 80. A counterexample in our geometric class would therefore give $f_8(21)=80$ and violate this instance of the recurrence. The proof below uses only unconditional bounds.

Suppose $G$ requires at least ten colors. Write $d_z=|N_B(z)|$ and $\delta=132-e(G)$. The edges outside $B$ are the 23 at $u$, the 20 remaining edges at $v$, the edge $xy$ and the $d_x+d_y$ exceptional spokes. Therefore
$$
e(B+x)=88-d_y-\delta,\qquad e(B+y)=88-d_x-\delta.
$$
Every eight-critical subgraph of either augmented body spans its 21 vertices, by [1, Theorem 2]. Eight-Ore graphs have order one modulo seven, since an Ore composition adds the two orders and subtracts one. The stronger inequality in Theorem 6 of Kostochka and Yancey [2] therefore gives at least $\lceil(54\cdot21-26)/14\rceil=80$ edges. This applies even to an augmented body containing a seven-clique through its exception. In particular $d_x+\delta,d_y+\delta\le8$.

## 2. A degree-seven exception

Fix $x$ with $d_x=7$ and put $S=N_B(x)$ and $M=B-S$. By [1, Theorem 2], $\alpha(M)=2$. Its thirteen vertices span at least 41 edges by [3]. First suppose both exceptional degrees are seven. For the two remaining distinct neighborhoods, the intersection has size zero or one. Let $R$ consist of the common nonneighbors and let $A$ consist of the common neighbors. If $p,q\in R$ are nonadjacent, $J=B-\{p,q\}$ needs seven colors. With six colors on $J$, the classes $\{p,q,x\},\{v,y\},\{u\}$ would finish a nine-coloring. Put $D=\sum_{w\in J}\max(0,6-d_J(w))$. Each remaining vertex outside $A$ has degree at least seven in $B$ and sees $p$ or $q$ by the appropriate missed-set independence bound. Summing deficits gives $e(J)+D\le e(B)-18+|A|$. An eighteen-vertex seven-chromatic graph without a seven-clique has $e(J)+D\ge58$ by the critical-order calculation in Supplement 136. It follows that $|R|\ge8+\delta$. Equality is excluded in Supplement 138. At equality a six-regular nine-vertex component survives each pair deletion. The common nonneighbors then form two anticomplete cliques, and their forced attachments violate this component's degrees. In particular a set of seven or eight common nonneighbors is impossible, since it contains a nonedge.

At intersection size one there are seven common nonneighbors. At size zero the six common nonneighbors form a clique by the same inequality. The two exclusive seven-sets have at least 41 edges each when combined with this clique. Counting their missed pairs against the clique forces one exclusive pair to cover the six-clique with six contacts and to miss the opposite seven-set. Give the pair the color absent from the six-clique. The five remaining vertices have at most seven outside contacts altogether, and Hall's condition assigns them five distinct colors from the other six (Supplement 137). Thus two degree-seven exceptions with distinct neighborhoods admit nine colors.

Next suppose $S$ contains no six-clique. Each independent set in $B$ has at most two vertices outside $S$. If $\alpha(B)\ge4$, take any maximum independent set $I$. After deleting $I$ from $B+x$, at most five neighbors of $x$ survive. Every seven-critical subgraph left by this deletion is therefore inside the ordinary body, where it has a biplanar double cone. The critical-order and list-extension arguments at the 81-edge cap in [5] force every such maximum set to miss a copy of $K_4\vee C_5$. Counting the contacts to one or two copies contradicts maximality. Supplement 141 gives the orders and the checks on where the double-cone hypothesis enters.

At $\alpha(B)\le3$, the rooted certificate of [6] applies after checking its five-clique hypotheses. A five-clique inside $M$ with three common body neighbors would give the forbidden $K_7\vee I_3$ on adding $u,v$ [7]. A five-clique in $S$ with two common neighbors in $M$ requires a separate list extension. Its common neighbors are nonadjacent, and the degree bounds leave at most three edges from the five-clique to vertices other than its two chosen common neighbors and the exception (Supplements 139–140). Finally, if $\{x\}$ and four vertices of $S$ have three common neighbors, those three are independent. All thirteen vertices of $M$ see at least one of them. Prescribing a singleton in a matching coloring of $M$ lets the three use at most two names. The four other members of $S$ have at most two edges into $M$ and can use four different names. Supplement 142 proves the extension and checks the remaining fixed-clique conditions in [6]. This settles all six-clique-free $S$, including equal degree-seven neighborhoods, since a common six-clique with $u,x,y$ would induce $K_9$.

It remains to have $S=Q\cup\{p\}$ with $Q$ a six-clique and $d_y=8$. Here $e(B+x)\le80$. Let $L$ be the vertices of $Q$ missed by $p$, let $\ell=|L|$, and let $h_i=d_{B+x}(i)-7$ for $i\in S$. The number of contacts into $M$ at $p$ is $\ell+h_p$, and at $i\in Q$ it is $\mathbf1_{i\in L}+h_i$. The count of the seven spokes, the $21-\ell$ internal edges and the contacts gives $\ell+\sum_Sh_i=e(B+x)-28-e(M)\le11$. Group $p$ with the leaf $a\in L$ having the most contacts, leaving five singletons in $Q$. Match the thirteen vertices of $M$ into six nonedge pairs and one singleton when the contact budget calls for it. The matching theorem in [3] and its 32-edge refinement in Supplement 143 allow a singleton missed by the group. When an exterior row has six or more contacts, prescribing a singleton or a matched pair instead provides distinct names for this row and the group. Each prescription is made in its own coloring.

In the remaining case the five rows see at most five of the six paired colors each. A failure of Hall on $r=2,3,4,5$ rows would need at least $r(7-r)=10,12,12,10$ contacts. There are at most ten. The only contact profiles permitting failure are $(5,5,0,0,0)$ and $(2,2,2,2,2)$. In the former, the two rows with five contacts each have two choices and the group sees at most two colors. In the latter every one of the six groups sees at most two vertices. For Hall to fail in every matching coloring all six must contact the same two vertices, as can be tested by prescribing a singleton in the difference of any two contact sets. A nonadjacent contact pair can be prescribed as one matching pair. An adjacent pair would form a seven-clique with the five singleton rows. This finishes the degree-seven case. Supplement 143 gives the list sizes and the matching argument at the boundary.

## 3. Disjoint neighborhoods and the full edge count

The remaining exceptional degrees are eight and $\delta=0$. Suppose first their neighborhoods $P,Q$ are disjoint. The four common nonneighbors $R$ form a clique by Supplement 136. At least one side, say $P$, has no six-clique by [4, Corollary 3], applied to disjoint six-cliques augmented by $u,v,x$ and $u,v,y$. Each member of $R$ misses cliques of sizes at most five in $P$ and six in $Q$. Its body degree is at least eight. All other body degrees are at least seven, which accounts for all $2e(B)=144$ incidences. Equality holds throughout. Each member of $R$ misses a five-clique in $P$. All eight vertices of $P$ have degree seven in $B+y$ and induce a Gallai forest. A vertex outside a fixed missed five-clique sees at most one of its vertices, since two contacts would extend the clique to size six in its Gallai block. The remaining three vertices must be a triangle by independence number two on $P\cup R$. The missed five-clique is unique. Thus $R$ sees the entire triangle, producing a seven-clique in $B$. This excludes disjoint neighborhoods.

Both augmented bodies now attain the critical edge floor. Each is itself eight-critical. When the intersection has size $k$, its $4+k$ common nonneighbors exclude $k=3,4$ by the pair-deletion bound above. At $k=7$, delete the edge from $x$ to its single exclusive neighbor $p$. The resulting seven-coloring uses a color at $x$ absent from the common neighbors. In every seven-coloring of $B$, both original neighborhoods must use all seven names. Hence $p$ and the vertex exclusive to $y$ have the same color in this particular coloring. They are nonadjacent.

Finally, a separating pair in a 21-vertex eight-critical graph would force equal colors on the pair on one side and different colors on the other in all seven-colorings. Add an edge to the first side and identify the two vertices on the second. The resulting vertex-critical graphs have orders summing to 22 and at most 81 edges in total. Gallai's exact small-order formula [2, Theorem 1] gives edge sums at least 82, 89 and 90 on the three possible pairs of orders $(8,14),(10,12),(11,11)$, respectively. Supplement 147 verifies the inheritance of vertex-criticality. This proves the theorem. The full edge count forces both planar layers to attain $3\cdot24-6$ edges.

## 4. Verification and scope

The supplement includes the pair-deletion, Hall and list-extension proofs and a reading guide identifying the previous inputs. The independent-set argument uses the published tables from [5]. The implication at independence at most three uses the published partition certificate of [6] after checking its individual hypotheses in Supplements 139–142. We use that certificate as published. The arithmetic checks in Supplement 144 cover the two limiting Hall profiles and the labeled six-clique count in an earlier overlap argument. The density and connectivity arguments in Supplements 146 and 147 use the explicit inequalities in [2].

Whether the two extremal augmented bodies can occur on the same twenty-vertex body with a biplanar realization remains open. The asserted restrictions concern a universal vertex and a second vertex with the specified adjacent nonneighbors. The chromatic number of arbitrary biplanar graphs remains open.
