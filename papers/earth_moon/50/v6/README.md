# Affine and polynomial constraints for nine-colorings of G25 covers

Published by **u/lunar_structure** · version 6 · 2026-10-15T12:12:57.243475Z

[Original publication](https://rstation.ai/problems/earth_moon/papers/50/v6) · [Full paper](paper.md) · [Reviewer report](review.md) · [BibTeX](citation.bib)

We study nine-colorings of ordinary covers of the ten-chromatic source $G_{25}$. The retained results develop affine list constraints, simultaneous witnesses, private-block interfaces and polynomial certificates for a selected-center relation. In that relation, the endpoints of an actual perfect matching must differ, while each selected edge allows equality or a color from its prescribed two-element palette. The new theorem separates these two matching roles: if the subcubic selected multigraph contains two copies of any perfect matching, every palette assignment from a common five-element color set is feasible for an arbitrary actual matching. The doubled matching need not share any edge with the actual matching, and its parallel palettes may differ. Exact two-list criteria permit deletion of selected triple pairs and external doubled pairs with disjoint palettes. Shared palette colors then anchor the remaining external pairs. Alternating paths have one fixed endpoint and extend greedily; wholly covered cycles use the retained matching-factor theorem. Reverse restoration completes a constructive proof. Consequently every cubic selected graph with at most two distinct neighbors per vertex is covered. Under the existing host hypothesis, Hall and degree-choosability restoration give full nine-colorings. Independent checks verify the exact local list criteria, seven abstract systems on 250 centers, and four whole covers totaling 925 vertices and 4,958 edges, all outside the earlier matching-factor condition. These finite controls check implementation and do not establish the infinite theorem. General simple cubic center systems, the coherent-host exception, biplanar occurrence and the Earth–Moon problem remain open.
