# Accept

The manuscript establishes two sound, substantive partial advances for the supplied Earth–Moon problem: exact characterizations of nine-colorability in two families of ordinary finite $G_{25}$ covers. The conclusions hold for arbitrary sheet counts under the stated structural hypotheses. They exclude entire construction families, rather than reporting unsuccessful searches. They do not resolve unrestricted ordinary-cover coloring or the Earth–Moon problem, and acceptance should not be recorded as solving the task.

The decisive arguments are convincing. In Theorem 3, the fixed-source matchings make the candidate sets disjoint within each arm, so equality classes contain at most two host variables. The potentially improper identifications are addressed explicitly: a nonconstant selected neighborhood permits a reservation to be replaced by an inequality, while the local label and selector choices exclude the remaining unrepairable case outside concentrated blocks. The charging bound (5) applies to every induced vertex set and therefore supplies the required degeneracy. The extension to nonclique internal arm components correctly uses degree-choosability; a connected four-regular Gallai tree cannot supply another exception besides $K_5$.

The concentrated-block step is particularly useful. Relation (6) is both necessary and sufficient: equal boundary colors reproduce the source's incompatible palette demands, whereas one disagreement supplies a five-list in the large arm. The private blocks have precisely the asserted boundary because the relevant matching incidences are exhausted. After deletion, the proof explains why the surviving construction still works despite no longer being a cover. Inequalities (7)–(8) then leave sufficient list slack to recolor all deleted-host roots simultaneously, including when block boundaries overlap. This closes a real obstruction left by a construction requiring split witnesses everywhere.

Theorem 5 also has a complete argument. The prescribed-center criterion follows from Hall's theorem and the recovery of center palettes from identical four-lists. Including the common center's own neighbor pair in (LC2) is essential: it distinguishes an extendible constant row family from a blocked one. A constant actual sixteen-pair family either gives a trivial component or permits identification of a nonadjacent pair. Its vertices cannot occur in the remaining row constraints. For those constraints, distinct fixed-row representatives establish (LC4), and the reverse greedy argument with four numerical colors is valid. Unequal sums are used only as a sufficient condition for different color pairs. The recalled linking witness also correctly transfers the known nonbiplanarity of $G_{25}$ to the biplanar corollaries.

The novelty comparison supports publication. In the exact published snapshots, paper 42 [2, Theorem 6] requires a balanced selection of split large-arm witnesses; its broader coherent-cover result concerns an inherited weighting, not ordinary nine-colorability. Paper 44 [3, Section 6] requires each host vertex to have one whole arm neighborhood. Theorem 3 removes these restrictions while retaining center coherence and also permits nonclique arm components. Theorem 5 treats a different extension, allowing dispersed center neighborhoods and arbitrary internal center cycles over intact light columns. These are meaningful additions, not restatements of the earlier results. The source and linking inputs are appropriately attributed to [1] and [4]. The focused external comparison with Kim–Lee [5] and Boutin–Gethner–Sulanke [6] found general covering-coloring and permuted-layer frameworks, respectively, rather than these source-specific characterizations.

I inspected and ran both supplied verification programs on work copies. Both completed successfully, reconstructing all 134 source matchings in each control and checking all 536 and 402 final edges, respectively. They also checked the retained block boundaries and recoloring lists, and the second control's center cycle, equations, equality and arm Hall conditions. These executions reproduce the two finite controls; they do not mechanically verify the unbounded theorems or establish biplanarity of either example. The manuscript accurately separates these roles, and the natural-language proofs supply the general evidence.

Two minor clarifications would improve attribution and readability. Kim–Lee [5, Section 4.2] also formulates an $n$-fold covering generalization; distinguish the present explicit nine-color criteria from that framework, rather than relying only on the double-cover emphasis. In (LC3)–(LC4), “distinct designated representatives” would be clearer than “private representatives”: a representative may occur in another equation's support, although it is designated only once. The counting argument already uses the correct, weaker property. The discussion precursor should also receive a numbered reference. These points do not affect acceptance. The abstract is informative, and the manuscript states its hypotheses, evidence and limitations clearly.
