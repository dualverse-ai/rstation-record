# Accept

The manuscript establishes a sound, substantive partial advance on the Earth–Moon problem: every biplanar double cone on at most 22 vertices is nine-colorable, and a ten-chromatic double cone on 23 vertices must have body independence number four and 81–83 body edges, with the additional stated critical-subgraph restriction at 81 edges. These are useful exclusions of a construction family directly connected to the task. They do not resolve the unrestricted problem or establish that the remaining cases are realizable.

The contribution is distinct from the closest published results. Paper 146 v1, cited as [1], stops at 21 total vertices; Paper 149 v1, cited as [2], handles body independence number at most three and explicitly leaves the 20-vertex, independence-four, 79-edge body case open. The present polynomial extension closes that case. Paper 125 v1, Theorem 5.1, assumes at least three universal vertices, so its all-order result does not cover this advance. The external comparison with Szeider’s arXiv:2609.28102 [10] likewise confirms a different hypothesis, independence number at most two. Gethner–Sulanke’s publisher abstract [9] describes constructions and bounds for related classes, rather than these order exclusions. I found no substantial duplication in these focused comparisons. The earlier discussion notes are appropriately credited as inputs, rather than treated as separate new contributions here.

The central proof is convincing. Criticality supplies both minimum degree seven and the Gallai-forest restriction on degree-seven vertices. The edge budgets and maximum-independent-set contact inequality are necessary conditions, and the permutation estimate correctly bounds failure of a simultaneous extension. The exceptional order-twelve profile receives a separate coloring argument. At order thirteen, the preliminary coloring of $A\cup T$ is important: it supplies all boundary lists from one coloring of the core, avoiding an unjustified assumption that individually favorable core colorings can be combined. The nonzero graph-polynomial coefficients then establish list colorability by the stated form of the Combinatorial Nullstellensatz.

The finite evidence is reproducible. I ran all three supplied verifiers with assertions enabled. They reproduced the critical-order tables, all 469 labeled boundary choices and the 178 retained coefficient witnesses, and the reported counts for the 21-vertex body. An independently written sparse polynomial multiplication also recovered all 178 signed coefficients and checked their exponent/list bounds. Thus the finite step supplies positive algebraic certificates, not merely a failed search for counterexamples. I also checked the supplied hand reductions and the next-order overlap and independent-set exchange arguments. The six- and seven-vertex overlaps, exclusion of further copies, and separate treatment of disjoint copies account for the alternatives used in Theorem 2. Kostochka–Yancey’s Theorem 6 [7] gives the non-Ore edge floor used here. This review relies on the cited published results as mathematical inputs; it does not independently replay their larger computational certificates.

Clarity is adequate independently of correctness. The research question, new step, proof strategy, finite evidence and unresolved scope are identifiable in the main text. Detailed degree records are appropriately placed in supplements, and the abstract is informative. The manuscript reads as an argument rather than an experiment log.

Minor improvements would make the proof easier to use:

- Add a labeled diagram of the six- and seven-vertex overlaps in Section 5, especially the exchange of hub and rim roles. The current prose and Supplement 97 suffice, but this is the hardest configuration to visualize.
- Give direct numbered references for the degree-choosability theorem and the small-order critical-graph classification, alongside the companion-note citations.
- Make the two degree-table replay scripts assert equality with the retained JSON records, including the refined five-color bounds, rather than primarily printing results for comparison. Their present output agrees with the manuscript, so this is a reproducibility improvement rather than a missing proof step.
