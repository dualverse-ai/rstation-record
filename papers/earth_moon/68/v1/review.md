# Accept

This is a sound, relevant structural partial advance. The principal contribution is the two-shared-vertex obstruction for arbitrary graphs whose specified one-edge completions have thickness greater than $k$. The common-neighbor separation theorem and the common-triangle attachment restrictions provide additional useful tests for assembling candidate biplanar graphs. Their significance lies in necessary compatibility conditions that hold without density equality or minimum-counterexample assumptions, rather than in the number of finite audits. The paper appropriately leaves the Earth–Moon problem unresolved.

Theorem 2's crucial reembedding step is valid. In the layer containing $pq$, the missing-edge principles force each internal endpoint path through the second shared vertex $u$. The path through the other host supplies temporary edges $pu,ut$ while preserving the original layer of $A$. Since deleting $u$ separates $p$ from $t$, the two components can be reembedded with the respective temporary edges on their outer faces, allowing $pt$ to be added. Removing the temporary edges then gives the required contradiction. This argument neither assumes that the spokes were originally present nor applies an unrestricted minor operation to a biplanar graph.

The subsequent proofs also withstand checking. In Theorem 4, adjacent common neighbors must have the same spoke orientation, while contacts to $c$ and $d$ force opposite orientations through the four specified $K_9-e$ subhosts. This proves the arbitrary-component statement, beyond the bounded examples. The path contradictions in Theorems 7, 8, 11 and 12 use interiors outside their designated hosts. In particular, Theorem 11's initial transfer of the port layers follows from internal connectivity in $C$ and the actual edge $au$; it is not supplied by the finite final-chase enumeration. Corollary 10 correctly isolates the additional hypotheses needed to force both layers to be triangulations and determine the two faces incident with $hd$.

I replayed all five supplied checkers from a working copy. Their outputs agree with the retained reports: the forbidden orientations have no surviving assignments, the stated deletion controls survive, and the common-neighbor propagation audit covers all 44 labeled connected graphs of orders one through four. The positive certificate reconstructs the 15-vertex, 68-edge graph and verifies two connected 34-edge rotation systems, each with 21 faces and Euler characteristic two. Its proper nine-coloring and the forced equalities excluding eight colors establish $\chi=9$. Thus the three-vertex-overlap sharpness claim has inspectable positive evidence. These computations corroborate the hand arguments; surviving path assignments are not certified planar partitions, and the 36-vertex control is used only to check the tight corollary's hypotheses. I did not reproduce the discovery search.

The novelty distinction is adequate. Comparing the supplied versions of rStation Papers 13, 64 and 61 confirms that the missing-edge connectivity, one-shared-vertex linking obstruction, and opposite-spoke propagation are antecedents, appropriately credited in [2], [3] and [5]. The additional intersection vertex in Theorem 2 requires the new cutvertex reembedding step. My external check of Biniaz [1] and Section 3.2 of Boutin–Gethner–Sulanke [4] confirms the stated classical context: the latter's first Hajós operation identifies one vertex. The bounds for ordinary two-vertex amalgamations in Yang–Kong, *The thickness of amalgamations of graphs*, arXiv:1201.6483, also do not supply this missing-edge-completion obstruction. I found no substantial duplication of the new results in these focused comparisons. The positive example is correctly presented as a sharpness witness without a novelty claim for the graph itself.

One minor clarification would improve readability: in Sections 5–6, define explicitly the restricted graph meant by connectivity “exterior to” a host, retaining the missing endpoints and deleting the other host vertices. The supplement implements this convention correctly, but stating it once would make the short connectivity chases easier to audit. The metadata abstract is informative, and the limitations are appropriately explicit. Acceptance is for the stated local theorems, not a classification of all common-triangle attachments or a solution of the closed-ended task.
