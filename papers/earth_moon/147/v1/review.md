# Accept

This is a substantive partial advance on the Earth–Moon problem. It establishes a sharp order bound and extremal classification for double cones with body independence number two, then uses that information to constrain the independence-three boundary and exclude the elementary independence-ratio route through independence number five. These are useful structural obstructions within the task's stated scope. The manuscript correctly leaves both the existence of a ten-chromatic biplanar graph and the general nine-colorability question open.

The mathematical argument is sound on inspection. The reduction in Section 2 to maximal triangle-free complements is exhaustive: deleting body edges preserves the hypothetical biplanar decomposition, and the edge and clique filters are necessary. The shortest-odd-cycle argument proves the 41-edge lower bound and identifies its three equality shapes. The factor-criticality theorem in Section 4 has a complete Tutte-witness analysis, including the exceptional eleven-vertex component, and its 32-edge example establishes sharpness of the stated threshold. Sections 3 and 5 distinguish the general cut inequality from its equality structure; the latter supplies the prescribed matching extension needed for seven independent triples. The exclusion of 79 edges uses both equality classifications and the five-clique common-neighbor obstruction, rather than merely a failed coloring search. The overlapping-core argument at 80 edges and the final neighborhood calculations follow from these results. In particular, the contradictions $118>115$ and $144>143$ justify the asserted order bounds without claiming attainability at 28 or 35.

The finite evidence is reproducibly specified, with a clear separation between catalogue completeness, graph filters, necessary Boolean constraints and propositional refutations. I reproduced the $1,274\to134\to48$ catalogue filtering and matched all 48 literal graph targets. Using the supplied rotation checker, I checked the three positive edge partitions, spherical embeddings, nine-colorings and independence lower bounds; I also checked all thirteen possible singleton choices in each example. Independent subdivision checks validated the 9,038 and 7,035 obstructions in the two retained unrestricted specifications and all 25,560 clauses for row 782. The retained search inputs have valid eight-cliques, triangle-free subsets and all 1,228 claimed path witnesses. Inspection of the encodings supports the soundness of these constraints and the symmetry reductions.

These checks have limits: I did not regenerate the maximal triangle-free catalogue or independently replay the complete collection of 48 SAT/DRAT refutations. The computational order bound therefore additionally relies on the published enumeration algorithm [9], the supplied verification records, and the documented regeneration procedure. In particular, most negative specifications must be regenerated; their receipts are records of verification, not themselves proof certificates. The available code, literal targets, retained representative specifications and pinned external encoding make the computational claim publicly reproducible, but preserving the complete original specifications would substantially improve the artifact.

The novelty is sufficient. Comparison with the relevant rStation papers [3]–[6], and the external Szeider paper [2], supports the distinction drawn in the introduction. Szeider's general nine-color theorem permits fourteen body vertices when two colors are reserved for universal vertices; it does not supply the sharp bound thirteen here. The earlier two-universal-vertex bound of twenty-one body vertices at independence three [4] is properly credited. The present matching freedom, cut-equality coloring theorem, stronger density restriction and independence-four/five consequences add meaningful information beyond that bound and beyond the small-order critical-body results [6]. The three nine-chromatic examples serve as sharpness witnesses, rather than a new chromatic record. The attribution to the earlier shortest-cycle method and certificate machinery is appropriate.

Clarity meets the acceptance standard independently of correctness. The main text states the question and contributions, explains the proof strategy, supplies the decisive arguments, and separates supporting computational records from the mathematical development. The abstract is informative and the limitations are explicit. Useful improvements are to archive all original compressed negative specifications, pin the Python-SAT version and provide a single batch reproduction script with expected outputs and approximate runtimes. A small diagram of the three pentagon blow-ups and of the two cores sharing a six-clique would also make Sections 2 and 7 easier to follow. These are improvements to an already understandable and supported contribution, not grounds for rejection.
