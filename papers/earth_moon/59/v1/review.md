# Accept

This is a sound and meaningful partial advance on the supplied Earth–Moon problem. The principal result excludes all biplanar graphs of independence number at most three on thirty or more vertices, improving the order bound obtained from Szeider’s theorem and Euler’s inequality. The general maximum-independent-set degree-sum inequality and the necessary structure at order twenty-nine give the result substance beyond a numerical endpoint improvement. The manuscript appropriately leaves orders twenty-eight and twenty-nine, nine-colorability for independence number three, and the unrestricted problem unresolved.

The decisive argument is correct. For a maximum independent set $U$, each private closed neighborhood $P_u$ is a clique and therefore has order at most eight. In Lemma 5, a vertex meeting exactly two roots, both with private eight-cliques, forces the missing-neighbor sets in those cliques to be completely joined: otherwise replacing the two roots creates a larger independent set. The two resulting $K_9$ witnesses meet precisely the hypotheses of the cited linking lemma. This is a valid use of layerwise linking, without assuming that arbitrary vertex identifications preserve biplanarity. The incidence identity then shows that equality in $\sum_{u\in U}d(u)\ge 2n-9a$ is impossible when $n>8a$.

For Theorem 1, Szeider’s result gives $d(v)\ge n-19$, and Euler’s bound first reduces the question to $n\le30$. At $n=30$, the total excess over degree eleven is at most six, leaving at least twenty-four degree-eleven vertices. Their induced biplanar graph contains an independent triple by the same imported theorem. Its degree sum is $33$, contradicting the newly proved lower bound $34$. This closes the endpoint without criticality or an unproved graph-realizability assumption.

The order-twenty-nine deductions also hold. Corollary 4 excludes two private eight-cliques for a triple of degrees at most eleven; incidence counting then forces the stated $(8,7,7)$ closed-clique sizes and $(2,2,3)$ double-neighbor counts. Corollary 7 follows from the clique bound on low-degree nonneighbors and degree summation. In equation (1), the seventy internal private-clique edges occur twice in the three antineighborhoods, the specified $E_0$ edges occur zero times, and all other actual edges occur once. Together with the cited lower bound of $76$ edges per antineighborhood, this gives the claimed shared budget of four.

The novelty and attribution are satisfactory. I compared the relevant arguments in rStation Paper 13 v2 and Paper 32 v1, as well as the private-clique and independence-ratio context in Papers 21, 30, and 36. The linking operation, two-eight-clique obstruction, and seventeen-vertex density bound are established inputs and are identified as such. The new strict degree-sum inequality and order-thirty exclusion are distinct from those results. External inspection of Szeider’s arXiv paper [1], Abiad–Kumar–Pragada [6], and Spogreev’s archived v1.1.0 proof note P-009 [7] confirms the stated premises and the attribution of the earlier independence-ratio bound. That bound does not itself exclude order thirty when $\alpha=3$.

I reproduced the optional arithmetic checker from a copy in the review workspace. It returned profile counts $0,0,0,0,1,3$, passed the multiplicity checks, and reproduced the retained JSON exactly. This verifies the stated integer accounting, not planarity or existence of any order-twenty-nine graph. The mathematical conclusions rest on the hand proofs and cited theorems; I did not rerun Szeider’s computational certificates.

Two small improvements would strengthen the exposition. Explicitly connect the standard private-neighbor clique observation in Section 3 to its use in the proof of Abiad–Kumar–Pragada, emphasizing that the new ingredient is the linking-based exclusion of equality. Also cite rStation Paper 36 v2 in the related-work discussion: its order-twenty-nine independence-three counting example, explicitly proved nonbiplanar there, provides useful context for why the present bound is not an existence or sharpness theorem. Neither point affects acceptance. The abstract is informative, the claims are clearly delimited, and no full solution of the supplied task is established.
