# Accept

The paper supports a useful, bounded empirical advance: reducing the cost of an established CATT contextual support readout while retaining high measured retrieval quality. Its contribution is the joint quality–cost comparison and representative-sensitivity analysis, rather than a new attribution operator. This is substantively relevant to the task because it addresses the extraction bottleneck left unresolved by the earlier successful neural readouts.

The central distinction is scientifically sound. Stopping execution at the fixed eighth-block boundary preserves the collected representation; replacing ten passages by one changes the score. Inspection of the collectors supports the former argument: both capture the same block output under evaluation without caching, and the prefix collector stops through a narrowly handled exception with hook cleanup. The manuscript does not mistakenly infer score equivalence from equivalent rule content. Its first-passage construction reaches 100% original RR and FB recall and 96.25% changed-RR recall, but appropriately reports the first-minus-full interval of $[-3.75,7.5]$ percentage points as insufficient to establish two-point statistical noninferiority.

The retained evidence supports the reported numerical results. I ran the supplied CPU replays for primary quality, aligned representative choices, recorded costs, independent choices and coverage selection. The combined-cost replay reproduced the six retained score matrices and corresponding reference rankings, together with timing and storage arithmetic. A separate reviewer-written recount reproduced the main adapted/disabled RR recalls and the changed-RR paired interval. An independent comparison of the source texts also confirmed that every RR group's ten passages share the same eight case–answer pairs. These checks reproduce calculations from retained evidence; they do not independently reproduce GPU inference, wall-clock measurements, omitted activation banks or all coverage nearest-neighbor calculations.

The cost comparison is appropriately scoped. The reported loaded-batch medians decrease from 261.236 to 34.198 seconds on RR and from 347.899 to 23.069 seconds on FB. The same-prefix ablation supplies evidence that source reduction contributes savings beyond stopping the backbone early. Charging the full-sized scoring scratch prevents the logical vector reduction from being misrepresented as a comparable memory reduction: useful source-vector bytes approach one tenth of the reference, whereas peak allocated GPU memory remains approximately 92% and 86%. Three paired repetitions support a local implementation comparison, not a population runtime confidence interval or an online-serving claim.

The sensitivity analysis adds useful substance. Eight of ten aligned representatives pass the declared gates, but some choices materially damage changed-RR recall. The exact independent-choice calculation handles corpus-order ties and shared index selection correctly; linearity of expectation does not require independently choosing an index for each query. Its expected 94.02% changed-RR recall does not guarantee the performance of an individual sampled index. Likewise, the coverage experiment supports failure of the specified selector and the looseness of its certificates, rather than a universal impossibility claim. These distinctions are communicated correctly.

Novelty is modest but sufficient for this empirical contribution. The published compact-readout and wording-stress studies [3,2] already establish the operator, representations and strong retrieval results. The present work goes beyond those results by measuring a direct reduction in extraction cost and characterizing the quality consequences of removing whole source passages. The external literature check supports the distinctions drawn from coordinate/residual compression, candidate pruning, within-passage token pooling and predictive early exit [6–9]. Those methods prevent broad algorithmic novelty claims, but do not establish this CATT operating point. The earlier source-conditioned routing paper [12] already uses the source-program groups and first corpus representatives; that inheritance should be cited explicitly, although its behavioral intervention differs from scoring only the representatives here.

The principal limits remain consequential: one exposed target per task, reused facts, imperfect wording substitutions and no new training-run or fresh-fact validation. Full group-max adapted and disabled RR scores both reach 95%, and exact-token matching saturates FB. Consequently, this paper establishes neither a general adaptation advantage nor historical training influence. The manuscript states these limits and retains strong controls, so they bound acceptance rather than contradict its central claim.

The scientific narrative is sufficiently clear for acceptance. The following minor improvements would make reuse easier:

- In “Representation, grouping and evaluation,” state the actual RR text-grouping criterion and FB input-symbol criterion, and cite the inherited grouping construction [12]. “Audited source-text properties” is less informative than the available concrete definition.
- Define “disabled model” explicitly, including which adapter or embedding components are disabled on each task. The distinction matters when interpreting the FB contrast.
- Give the coverage bound explicitly, with its radius definition and assumptions. For normalized query vectors and nonnegative weights summing to one, the intended statement is $0\leq S_{\mathrm{full}}(q,G)-S(q,d_G)\leq r_G$, where every group token has a representative token within Euclidean distance $r_G$. This would make the certificate's meaning easier to follow.
