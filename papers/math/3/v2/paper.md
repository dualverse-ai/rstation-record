## Problem and contribution

Let $q=2^n$ and $G=\mathbb{F}_q^\rho$, viewed as an additive group, and let $\mathbb{F}_2[G]$ be its binary group algebra. An augmentation-one element supported on a line (a one-dimensional $\mathbb{F}_q$-subspace) is a unit, and every such unit is an involution. This paper studies two elementary but distinct questions about multiplication by such line units: (i) when does a random product of line units fix a given coefficient, and (ii) when is the associated multilinear Boolean evaluation constant along the translates of a target line? The point of the paper is that these two questions have exact or sharp answers in several natural regimes, and that the answers differ.

*Unconditional results (all proved in full below).*

- A support-four coefficient $K=\Omega_H$, built from three distinct coplanar lines, is fixed by the product of their line-sum units, and every nonzero coefficient fixed by that product has support at least four (Proposition 3.1).
- Exact survival probabilities: for one uniform line unit, for two independent uniform line units on arbitrary lines in the plane (Theorem 4.2), and for any number of independent uniform units on the three defining lines, including repetitions (Theorem 4.3). For arbitrary families of supporting lines the joint survival probability is $1/|P|$ for an explicit subgroup $P$ of the quotient unit group, hence at least the product of the individual probabilities and unchanged by repeated lines (Proposition 4.4); the two- and three-line laws show that the inequality can be strict.
- For a singleton coefficient and a fixed transverse line-sum unit, a uniform bound $\mathbb{P}[\text{orbit constant}]\le \exp(-\Omega(\sqrt{\mathcal{A}}))$ with $\mathcal{A}=q2^{-(\rho-1)}/s$, valid for all coordinate-full directions, hence vanishing at every fixed density $\rho/n\to\kappa<1$ (Theorem 5.2).
- For a repeated-slope family, the orbit probability equals the empty-active-set probability $(1-2^{-(\rho-1)})^q$ up to an additive error $1/(2q)+e^{-q/72}+2^{1-q/6}$, uniformly in the parameters, giving the critical limit $e^{-\lambda}$ when $q2^{-(\rho-1)}\to\lambda$ (Theorem 6.1).
- Lower bounds from positive association for four-point coefficients: nonvanishing at $\rho=2n+O(1)$ for arbitrary target lines, with the sharper limit $e^{-2^{-c}}$ for every coefficient on a second line of the defining plane and at $\rho=n+O(1)$ when the unit lies on the target line (Theorem 7.2, Corollary 7.3).

*Not claimed.* We do not determine the orbit transition for generic directions at critical density $\kappa=1$, nor the true cross-line transition for four-point coefficients (the scale $\rho=2n$ is only where our lower bound becomes nonvanishing). We do not claim exact survival laws for three general supporting lines. The finite computation in Section 8 checks one finite instance of the counting step; it is not part of any proof.

## Related work and distinction

Normalized units of modular group algebras of $p$-groups are classical objects; in characteristic two for an elementary abelian $2$-group, the identity $B^2=\operatorname{aug}(B)1$ (Lemma 2.1) is standard, and we include its one-line proof only to fix notation. Borello, Willems and Zini [1] relate the dimension and minimum support of ideals in group algebras (an uncertainty principle $d(C)\dim C\ge|G|$) and study Schur (coordinatewise) products of ideals. The ideal $\Omega_H\mathbb{F}_2[G]$ generated by our four-point coefficient has minimum distance $4$ and dimension $|G|/4$, so it is an equality case of their bound [1] (Corollary 2.6), of the form classified in their Theorem 2.10; Proposition 3.1 adds that the strictly larger annihilator $\operatorname{Ann}(D-1)$, of dimension at least $|G|/2$, has the same minimum distance. Our survival laws concern convolution products of random units and an additive quotient that turns the fixed-coefficient equation into a product equation among subalgebras. The orbit lower bounds use the Harris–FKG positive association inequality for product measures [2], [3], for which a short proof of the needed special case is included. The orbit upper bound is a rank bound for a structured random matrix over $\mathbb{F}_2$; Darling, Penrose, Wade and Zabell [4] (Section 2.1) study sparse random $\mathbb{F}_2$ matrices with i.i.d. rows, each sampled uniformly after its weight is specified; the rows of our matrix instead share role variables through field-index collisions, so row independence is unavailable, and Lemma 5.1 extracts columns supported on disjoint sets of role variables. The full-support line model is the one appearing in Reed–Solomon evaluation vectors [5]; this is a motivation only.

## 1. Introduction

Convolution by a line-supported element is a simple operation in a finite
group algebra. Its effect on coefficient supports is algebraic, while its
effect on a multilinear Boolean evaluation depends on the number and
arrangement of the underlying role variables. We determine several exact
survival laws and orbit bounds that make this distinction quantitative.
The central finite object is a four-point additive subgroup meeting three
coplanar lines in two points each. Passing to its quotient turns the
survival equation into a product equation among normalized units.

The algebraic results apply to arbitrary lines. The orbit upper bound
requires directions with no zero coordinates, as in the full-support line
model associated with Reed–Solomon evaluations [5]. To see the
connection explicitly, for distinct evaluation points $\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_\rho$
and a polynomial $f$ with $f(\xi_j)\ne0$, scalar multiplication of the
evaluation vector gives the line
$\{g(f(\xi_1),\ldots,f(\xi_\rho)):g\in\mathbb{F}_q\}$. We prove the orbit bound
for every nonzero-coordinate direction, without assuming a polynomial
degree restriction. No such interpolation interpretation is needed for
the group-algebra statements.

The main results have three forms. Theorems 4.2 and
4.3 count exactly the random units preserving the four-point
coefficient. Theorem 5.2 bounds the probability of a constant
orbit for a singleton and a fixed transverse line-sum unit, uniformly in
the direction. Theorem 7.2 gives lower bounds for the
orbit probability of a four-point coefficient multiplied by a random
line unit. A structured singleton family also admits the exact critical
limit in Theorem 6.1.

Normalized units and ideals in modular group algebras provide the natural
algebraic setting. Related work of Borello, Willems and Zini
[1] connects ideal dimension with minimum support and studies
Schur products of ideals; the remark after
Proposition 3.1 places our four-point coefficient
precisely relative to their uncertainty principle. The survival
equations here use convolution and intersections of subalgebras after an
additive quotient. The probability
lower bound uses the classical positive association of product measures
[2], [3]; a proof of the precise form needed here is included.
The independent-column argument is also elementary, but its applicability
depends on keeping the role labels and affine-index collisions explicit.
Rank deficiency of sparse random matrices over $\mathbb{F}_2$ is studied, for
example, by Darling, Penrose, Wade and Zabell [4] (Section 2.1),
for matrices with i.i.d.\ rows, each row sampled uniformly after its
weight is specified. The rows of the matrices $H_X$ below instead share
role variables through affine-index collisions, so row independence is
unavailable; Lemma 5.1 handles the dependence by extracting
columns supported on disjoint sets of role variables.

## 2. Group algebras, fixed coefficients and orbit events

Throughout, $q=2^n$ and $G=\mathbb{F}_q^\rho$ is regarded as an additive group.
The binary group algebra $\mathbb{F}_2[G]$ has basis $[g]$, $g\in G$, with
$[g][h]=[g+h]$ and identity $1=[0]$. For $B=\sum_g b_g[g]$, write
$\operatorname{aug}(B)=\sum_g b_g\in\mathbb{F}_2$ and $\operatorname{supp}(B)=\{g:b_g=1\}$.
For an additive subgroup $S\le G$, put

$$
\Omega_S=\sum_{s\in S}[s],\qquad
\mathcal{U}(S)=\{B\in\mathbb{F}_2[S]:\operatorname{aug}(B)=1\}.
$$

All algebraic sums and products of coefficients in this paper are over
$\mathbb{F}_2$; probabilities and cardinalities are ordinary real numbers and
integers.

**Lemma 2.1.** 
For every $B\in\mathbb{F}_2[G]$, $B^2=\operatorname{aug}(B)1$. Consequently $\mathcal{U}(S)$ is a
group of order $2^{|S|-1}$, and every element of $\mathcal{U}(S)$ is its own inverse.
For additive subgroups $S,T\le G$,

$$
\Omega_S\Omega_T=|S\cap T|\,\Omega_{S+T}, \tag{1}
$$

where the integer coefficient on the right is reduced modulo two.

*Proof.* 
Commutativity and characteristic two cancel all cross terms in $B^2$;
each remaining basis square is $[2g]=1$. Augmentation is a nonzero linear
functional on the $|S|$-dimensional coefficient space, which gives the
cardinality. Every element of $S+T$ has exactly $|S\cap T|$
representations as $s+t$, proving (1).
 $\square$

A line is a one-dimensional $\mathbb{F}_q$-subspace. We call its direction
*coordinate-full* if all coordinates of a nonzero generator are
nonzero. For a line $L$, its line-sum unit is $1+\Omega_L$; it has
augmentation one and support $L\setminus\{0\}$.

**Definition 2.2.** 
A nonzero coefficient $K\in\mathbb{F}_2[G]$ is *fixed* by a unit $D$ if
$KD=K$. Its survival under a further unit $A$ is the event $KDA=K$.

Let $(X_{j,u}:1\le j\le\rho,\ u\in\mathbb{F}_q)$ be independent uniform bits.
The Boolean phase of $B$ is

$$
Q_B(X)=\sum_{g\in G}b_g\prod_{j=1}^{\rho}X_{j,g_j}\in\mathbb{F}_2. \tag{2}
$$

Translation is $\tau_tB=[t]B$. For a target line $T$, let

$$
\mathcal{C}_T(B)=\{X:Q_{\tau_tB}(X)\text{ is constant as }t\text{ ranges over }T\}.
$$

This event allows both constant values. It is not the algebraic equation
$BA=B$, and no identification between these two conditions will be used.

## 3. A support-minimal four-point fixed coefficient

Let $q\ge4$ and let $W\le G$ be a two-dimensional $\mathbb{F}_q$-subspace.
Take distinct lines $L_1,L_2,L_3\le W$. There are linearly independent
$a,b\in W$ with $a\in L_1$, $b\in L_2$ and $a+b\in L_3$. Define

$$
H=\{0,a,b,a+b\},\qquad K=\Omega_H=(1+[a])(1+[b]),\qquad
D=\prod_{i=1}^{3}(1+\Omega_{L_i}). \tag{3}
$$

**Proposition 3.1.** 
The coefficient $K$ satisfies $KD=K$. Every nonzero coefficient fixed by
$D$ has support at least four, even if its support is allowed outside $W$.

*Proof.* 
Each $H\cap L_i$ has size two, so (1) gives
$K\Omega_{L_i}=0$. Thus $K$ is fixed by each factor of $D$.
For distinct lines in $W$, their subgroup sums multiply to $\Omega_W$;
a product of three such sums vanishes because $|W\cap L_i|=q$ is even.
Consequently

$$
N:=D-1=\Omega_{L_1}+\Omega_{L_2}+\Omega_{L_3}+\Omega_W.
$$

Its support is $W\setminus(L_1\cup L_2\cup L_3)$, of size
$(q-1)(q-2)>0$. A coefficient of odd support is a unit by
Lemma 2.1, and hence cannot annihilate $N$.

A two-point coefficient, after translation, is $1+[h]$ with $h\ne0$.
If $h\notin W$, then $N$ and $[h]N$ have disjoint supports. If $h\in W$
and $[h]N=N$, the union $S=L_1\cup L_2\cup L_3$ is invariant under
translation by $h$. Since $0\in S$, this forces $h\in S$, say $h\in L_1$.
Choose a selected line $L_2\ne L_1$ and an unselected line $M\le W$.
The affine line $h+L_2$ intersects $M$ at a nonzero point: these lines
are not parallel, and $0\notin h+L_2$. That point is outside $S$,
contradicting $h+L_2\subseteq S$. This excludes support two and proves
the asserted minimum.
 $\square$

The proof uses no restriction on coordinates. In particular it applies
whenever the three selected lines are coordinate-full.

The principal ideal $J_H=\Omega_H\mathbb{F}_2[G]$ has a basis of the disjoint
coset sums $\Omega_{g+H}$, so $\dim_{\mathbb{F}_2}J_H=|G|/4$ and every nonzero
element has support a positive multiple of four. Hence $J_H$ attains
equality in the bound $d(C)\dim C\ge|G|$ for ideals of group algebras
[1] (Corollary 2.6), and is of the form classified in
[1] (Theorem 2.10). Proposition 3.1 concerns the
annihilator $C_D=\operatorname{Ann}(D-1)$, which contains $J_H$ because
$KN=0$. Since $D^2=1$ we have $N^2=0$, so the image of multiplication by
$N$ lies in its kernel and $\dim C_D\ge|G|/2>\dim J_H$. The proposition
shows that this strictly larger ideal still has minimum distance four. In the rest of
the paper, the notation $H,K,D,W$ refers to (3).

## 4. Exact survival laws from an additive quotient

Let $\pi:W\to Q=W/H$ be the quotient of additive groups; it is generally
not a quotient of $\mathbb{F}_q$-vector spaces. Its cardinality is $|Q|=q^2/4$.
Extend $\pi$ linearly to $\pi_*:\mathbb{F}_2[W]\to\mathbb{F}_2[Q]$.

**Lemma 4.1.** 
For every $B\in\mathbb{F}_2[W]$,

$$
KB=K\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\pi_*(B)=1. \tag{4}
$$

If $M\le W$ is a line, $h=|H\cap M|$ and $S=\pi(M)$, then
$h\in\{1,2\}$, $|S|=q/h$, and every fiber of
$\mathbb{F}_2[M]\to\mathbb{F}_2[S]$ has cardinality

$$
2^{q-q/h}. \tag{5}
$$

The pushforward of the uniform measure on $\mathcal{U}(M)$ is uniform on $\mathcal{U}(S)$.

*Proof.* 
The translates $K[g]=\Omega_{H+g}$ have disjoint supports for distinct
$H$-cosets. Thus $KB$ records the parity of the coefficients of $B$ in
each such coset, proving (4). The nonzero elements of
$H$ lie on three distinct $\mathbb{F}_q$-lines, so $h$ is one or two. The
coefficient pushforward is surjective and has rank $|S|=q/h$, hence
kernel dimension $q-q/h$. It preserves augmentation. Therefore the
entire fiber above an augmentation-one target lies in the
augmentation-one source, and all these fibers have the size in
(5). Notice that this coefficient-space fiber is larger
than the point-map fiber of size $h$ when $h=2$ and $q>2$.
 $\square$

Since $KD=K$, survival under $DA$ is precisely $KA=K$. For a uniform
$A\in\mathcal{U}(M)$, Lemma 4.1 immediately gives

$$
\mathbb{P}[KA=K]=2^{1-q/|H\cap M|}. \tag{6}
$$

**Theorem 4.2 (Two line units).** 
Let $A_i$ be independent uniform elements of $\mathcal{U}(M_i)$, where
$M_1,M_2\le W$ are lines, and write $h_i=|H\cap M_i|$. If $M_1\ne M_2$,

$$
\mathbb{P}[KA_1A_2=K]
=2^{\,1+4/(h_1h_2)-q/h_1-q/h_2}. \tag{7}
$$

If $M_1=M_2=M$, this probability is $2^{1-q/|H\cap M|}$.

*Proof.* 
Set $S_i=\pi(M_i)$ and $x_i=\pi_*(A_i)$. These are independent uniform
elements of $\mathcal{U}(S_i)$. For distinct lines, $S_1+S_2=Q$ and

$$
|S_1\cap S_2|=\frac{|S_1||S_2|}{|Q|}=\frac4{h_1h_2}.
$$

By Lemma 2.1, $x_1x_2=1$ if and only if $x_1=x_2$.
Their common values are exactly $\mathcal{U}(S_1\cap S_2)$, because the
intersection of two group-basis subalgebras is the subalgebra on the
intersection of their supports. There are $2^{|S_1\cap S_2|-1}$ such
values, out of $2^{|S_1|+|S_2|-2}$ ordered pairs. This proves
(7). For a repeated line there is one admissible second
unit for each first unit.
 $\square$

The excess over the product of the two one-unit probabilities is
$2^{|S_1\cap S_2|-1}$. It equals $8$, $2$ or $1$ when
$(h_1,h_2)$ is respectively $(1,1)$, $(1,2)$ or $(2,2)$, with the
mixed case symmetric. For the three defining lines, Theorem 4.3
gives the analogous excess $d$ over the product $2^{3(1-d)}$ of the three
one-unit probabilities. Independent sampling of units therefore need not
give multiplicative survival probabilities.

**Theorem 4.3 (Units on the three defining lines).** 
Let $A_1,\ldots,A_k$ be independent uniform line units, with $k\ge1$
and each supporting line among $L_1,L_2,L_3$. Let $j$ be the number of
distinct supporting lines and put $d=q/2$. Then

$$
\mathbb{P}\left[K\prod_{i=1}^{k}A_i=K\right]
=\begin{cases}
2^{1-d},&j=1,\\
2^{2-2d},&j=2,\\
d\,2^{-3(d-1)},&j=3.
\end{cases} \tag{8}
$$

The probability depends on the active lines and not on their multiplicities.

*Proof.* 
Using $a,b$ as an $\mathbb{F}_q$-basis identifies
$Q$ with $(\mathbb{F}_q/\mathbb{F}_2)^2$. The three images $S_1,S_2,S_3$ are the
two coordinate axes and the diagonal, each of size $d$.
Suppose $x_i\in\mathcal{U}(S_i)$ and $x_1x_2x_3=1$. Then
$x_1x_2=x_3$, and the support of $x_1x_2$ is the Cartesian product
of the nonempty supports of $x_1$ and $x_2$. There is no cancellation:
every element of $S_1\oplus S_2$ has a unique decomposition into the
two axes. A nonempty Cartesian product contained in the diagonal is
a single point. The admissible triples are consequently

$$
([(u,0)],[(0,u)],[(u,u)]),\qquad u\in\mathbb{F}_q/\mathbb{F}_2.
$$

Their number is $d$, proving the $j=3$ case. The first two cases
follow from (6) and Theorem 4.2.
Finally, the product of $k'\ge1$ independent uniform elements of any finite
group is uniform: each target has exactly $|\mathcal{U}(S_i)|^{k'-1}$ preimages.
Group the factors on each supporting line to obtain the general statement.
 $\square$

The excesses above have a common source, valid for arbitrary
supporting lines.

**Proposition 4.4 (Arbitrary families of supporting lines).** 
Let $M_1,\ldots,M_k\le W$ be lines, not necessarily distinct, and let
$A_i$ be independent uniform elements of $\mathcal{U}(M_i)$. Put
$V_i=\mathcal{U}(\pi(M_i))$ and let $P=V_1\cdots V_k$ be the subgroup of $\mathcal{U}(Q)$
they generate. Then

$$
\mathbb{P}\Bigl[K\prod_{i=1}^kA_i=K\Bigr]=\frac1{|P|}
\ge\prod_{i=1}^k\mathbb{P}[KA_i=K],
$$

and the ratio of the two sides is $|\ker\Phi|$, where
$\Phi:V_1\times\cdots\times V_k\to P$ is multiplication. In particular,
the probability is a power of $2$, it is unchanged when a supporting
line is repeated, and it does not increase when an independent uniform
unit on a further line is added.

*Proof.* 
By Lemma 4.1 the elements $x_i=\pi_*(A_i)$ are independent and
uniform on $V_i$, and survival is the event $x_1\cdots x_k=1$. The group
$\mathcal{U}(Q)$ is abelian, so $\Phi$ is a surjective homomorphism onto $P$ and
all its fibers have size $|\ker\Phi|$. Hence $x_1\cdots x_k$ is uniform on
$P$, which gives the equality. Since $\mathbb{P}[KA_i=K]=1/|V_i|$ and
$|P|\,|\ker\Phi|=\prod_i|V_i|$, the inequality and the ratio follow. The
group $P$ is a $2$-group, it does not change when a factor $V_i$ is
repeated, and it can only grow when a factor is added.
 $\square$

For two distinct lines $|\ker\Phi|=|V_1\cap V_2|=2^{|\pi(M_1)\cap\pi(M_2)|-1}$,
recovering Theorem 4.2; for the three defining lines the ratio is
the factor $d$ in (8). The proposition does not compute $|P|$
for three general supporting lines.

## 5. Exposed columns for a singleton line-sum orbit

In this section the coefficient is the singleton $[0]$, and the unit is
the fixed line sum $U=1+\Omega_L$, not a uniformly sampled line unit.
Let $\rho\ge2$ and take two distinct coordinate-full lines. Independent
coordinate scalings preserve the distribution of the role arrays, so we
may normalize them to

$$
T=\{a\mathbf1:a\in\mathbb{F}_q\},\qquad
L=\{gv:g\in\mathbb{F}_q\},\qquad v\in(\mathbb{F}_q^*)^\rho.
$$

The nonparallel assumption is $v\notin\mathbb{F}_q\mathbf1$. Write
$Y_x=X_{\rho,x}$ and $d_j=v_j/v_\rho$ for $j<\rho$. Let

$$
J=\{j<\rho:d_j\ne1\},\quad s=|J|\ge1,\qquad
I=\{j<\rho:d_j=1\},\quad t=|I|,\quad p=2^{-(\rho-1)},
$$

and let $\nu=|\{d_j:j\in J\}|$ be the number of distinct ratios different
from one, so that $1\le\nu\le s$.
Changing variables from $g$ to $x=a+v_\rho g$ gives

$$
z(a):=Q_{\tau_{a\mathbf1}U}(X)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_q}H_X(a,x)Y_x,
\qquad
H_X(a,x)=\mathbf1_{x\ne a}\prod_{j<\rho}X_{j,(1+d_j)a+d_jx}. \tag{9}
$$

The zero diagonal in this definition is essential.

**Lemma 5.1 (Exact exposed-column probability).** 
Fix $E\subseteq\mathbb{F}_q$ with $|E|=M\le q/2$ and a nonzero relation
$c\in\mathbb{F}_2^E$ with support $C$ of size $m$. There is an exposed set
$F\subseteq\mathbb{F}_q\setminus E$, chosen independently of the role arrays,
with

$$
|F|\ge R_M:=\left\lfloor\frac{q-M}{\nu M(M-1)+1}\right\rfloor, \tag{10}
$$

and, if $E$ is an $\mathbb{F}_2$-linear subspace of $\mathbb{F}_q$,

$$
|F|\ge R_M^{\mathrm{sub}}:=\left\lfloor\frac{q-M}{\nu(M-1)+1}\right\rfloor, \tag{11}
$$

such that

$$
\mathbb{P}_X[(H_X^{\mathsf T}c)|_F=0]
=\left(1-2^{-t}\frac{1-(1-2^{1-s})^m}{2}\right)^{|F|}
\le(1-p)^{|F|}. \tag{12}
$$

*Proof.* 
For $j\in J$, let $\ell_j(a,x)=(1+d_j)a+d_jx$. A collision
$\ell_j(a,x)=\ell_j(b,y)$ in the same role, with $x\ne y$, requires

$$
y=x+d_j^{-1}(1+d_j)(a+b),\qquad a\ne b. \tag{13}
$$

Make a graph on $\mathbb{F}_q$ using every edge in (13) for
$j\in J$ and distinct $a,b\in E$, and remove $E$. The edges in
(13) depend on $j$ only through $d_j$, so roles with equal
ratios contribute the same edges, and the shifts are
$d_j^{-1}(1+d_j)e$ with $e\in (E+E)\setminus\{0\}$. The maximum degree
is therefore at most $\nu|(E+E)\setminus\{0\}|\le\nu M(M-1)$; when $E$ is a
subspace, $(E+E)\setminus\{0\}=E\setminus\{0\}$ and the bound is $\nu(M-1)$.
Greedy selection of a vertex and deletion of its
neighbors produces an independent set satisfying (10), and
(11) in the subspace case. The parameters $s$ and $t$ in
(12) remain the numbers of roles, not of distinct
ratios.

For $x\in F$ and $a\in C$, the products

$$
Q_{a,x}=\prod_{j\in J}X_{j,\ell_j(a,x)}
$$

are independent Bernoulli variables of parameter $2^{-s}$. Within one
column, $a\mapsto\ell_j(a,x)$ is injective for $j\in J$; between
columns, independence follows from the forbidden-edge construction.
Variables in different roles remain distinct even when their field
indices agree. The remaining roles contribute the common factor
$R_x=\prod_{j\in I}X_{j,x}$, a Bernoulli variable of parameter $2^{-t}$,
independent across columns and from all the $Q_{a,x}$. The empty
product is one. Since $F$ avoids $E$,

$$
(H_X^{\mathsf T}c)_x=R_x\sum_{a\in C}Q_{a,x}.
$$

The parity of $m$ independent Bernoulli$(2^{-s})$ variables is one
with probability $[1-(1-2^{1-s})^m]/2\ge2^{-s}$. All exposed column
values are independent, proving both parts of (12).
 $\square$

**Theorem 5.2 (Uniform averaged row-rank bound).** 
With the preceding notation, for every fixed $E$ of size $M\le q/2$,

$$
\mathbb{P}_X[\operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb{F}_2} H_X[E,*]<M]
 \le(2^M-1)(1-p)^{R_M}\le2^M e^{-pR_M}, \tag{14}
$$

$$
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{C}_T(U)]\le2^M e^{-pR_M}+2^{1-M}, \tag{15}
$$

and both bounds hold with $R_M^{\mathrm{sub}}$ in place of $R_M$ when $E$
is an $\mathbb{F}_2$-subspace. Let $\mathcal A=qp/\nu$. If $\mathcal A\ge64$, then

$$
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{C}_T(U)]\le e^{-(1-(\log2)/4)\sqrt{\mathcal A}}
+2e^{-(\log2)\sqrt{\mathcal A}/8}
=\exp\bigl(-\Omega(\sqrt{\mathcal A})\bigr), \tag{16}
$$

uniformly over the allowed directions.
In particular, $2^{n-\rho}/\rho\to\infty$ is sufficient for the orbit
probability to tend to zero, and this includes every fixed density
$\rho/n\to\kappa<1$.

*Proof.* 
Rank deficiency gives a nonzero row relation. Apply
Lemma 5.1 to each of the $2^M-1$ relations and take a
union bound. Conditional on full row rank, the uniform vector $Y$
maps uniformly onto $\mathbb{F}_2^M$, where the two constant vectors have
total probability $2^{1-M}$. This proves (15).

For (16), let $E$ be an $\mathbb{F}_2$-subspace of cardinality
$M=2^{\lfloor\log_2(\sqrt{\mathcal A}/4)\rfloor}$, so that
$\sqrt{\mathcal A}/8<M\le\sqrt{\mathcal A}/4$; in particular $M\ge2$ and,
since $\mathcal A\le q$, $M\le q/2$. As $\nu(M-1)+1\le\nu M$ and $q-M\ge q/2$,

$$
R_M^{\mathrm{sub}}\ge\frac{q}{2\nu M}-1\ge\frac{q}{4\nu M},
$$

where the last step uses $q/(4\nu M)\ge q/(\nu\sqrt{\mathcal A})
=\sqrt{\mathcal A}/p\ge1$. Hence $pR_M^{\mathrm{sub}}\ge\mathcal A/(4M)\ge\sqrt{\mathcal A}$, and

$$
2^M e^{-pR_M^{\mathrm{sub}}}\le e^{-(1-(\log2)/4)\sqrt{\mathcal A}},\qquad
2^{1-M}\le2e^{-(\log2)\sqrt{\mathcal A}/8}.
$$

Finally $\nu\le s\le\rho-1$, so the stated sufficient density condition
forces $\mathcal A\to\infty$.
 $\square$

## 6. An exact critical limit for a repeated-slope family

The uniform upper bound leaves a critical window. A family with repeated
slopes provides a solvable example within that window. Fix
$\gamma\in\mathbb{F}_q^*\setminus\{1\}$ and $\rho\ge3$, and take

$$
T=\mathbb{F}_q(1,\ldots,1),\qquad L=\mathbb{F}_q(1,\ldots,1,\gamma),\qquad
U=1+\Omega_L.
$$

Let $p=2^{-(\rho-1)}$ and define the random active set
$\Lambda=\{h:\prod_{j<\rho}X_{j,h}=1\}$. Its membership indicators are
independent Bernoulli$(p)$ variables. Direct substitution gives

$$
z(a)=\sum_{\substack{h\in\Lambda\\h\ne a}}Y_{a+\gamma(h-a)}. \tag{17}
$$

**Theorem 6.1 (Repeated-slope Poisson limit).** 
Uniformly over all $\rho\ge3$ and all $\gamma\in\mathbb{F}_q^*\setminus\{1\}$,

$$
0\le\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{C}_T(U)]-(1-p)^q
\le \frac{1}{2q}+e^{-q/72}+2^{1-q/6}. \tag{18}
$$

If $n\to\infty$, $\rho\to\infty$ and $qp\to\lambda\in[0,\infty)$,
then

$$
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{C}_T(U)]\longrightarrow e^{-\lambda}. \tag{19}
$$

If $qp\to\infty$, the probability tends to zero.

*Proof.* 
The lower bound is the event $\Lambda=\varnothing$. Let $H_\Lambda$ be the matrix
in (17). Adding the diagonal matrix of $\mathbf1_\Lambda$ gives the matrix
$B_\Lambda(a,x)=\mathbf1_\Lambda(\gamma^{-1}x+(1+\gamma^{-1})a)$. Permuting rows
and columns changes $B_\Lambda$ into the additive convolution matrix for
$t_\Lambda=\sum_{h\in\Lambda}[h]$. Thus

$$
\operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb{F}_2} H_\Lambda\ge\operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb{F}_2}(t_\Lambda\cdot)-|\Lambda|. \tag{20}
$$

Write $\epsilon=|\Lambda|\bmod2$ and $\ell=\sum_{h\in\Lambda}h\in\mathbb{F}_2^n$.
When $\epsilon=1$, Lemma 2.1 gives rank $q$ for
multiplication by $t_\Lambda$. When $\epsilon=0$ and $\ell\ne0$, use

$$
\mathbb{F}_2[\mathbb{F}_2^n]\cong\mathbb{F}_2[u_1,\ldots,u_n]/(u_1^2,\ldots,u_n^2),
\qquad u_i=1+[e_i].
$$

The degree-one part of $t_\Lambda$ is nonzero. An invertible linear
substitution makes it $u_1$; such a substitution is an algebra
automorphism, because the square of every linear form in the $u_i$
vanishes in characteristic two. Multiplication by $t_\Lambda$ is then injective
on the $q/2$-dimensional span of monomials not containing $u_1$:
the lowest nonzero homogeneous part of any element of this span
is carried to its nonzero product with $u_1$. Hence its rank is at
least $q/2$.

On $|\Lambda|\le q/3$ and $(\epsilon,\ell)\ne(0,0)$,
(20) gives $\operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb{F}_2} H_\Lambda\ge q/6$. As above, conditional
orbit constancy has probability at most $2^{1-q/6}$. Since $p\le1/4$,
the standard exponential bound for a sum of independent bounded
variables [6] gives $\mathbb{P}[|\Lambda|>q/3]\le e^{-q/72}$. This bound follows,
for example, from
$\mathbb{E} e^{t(B-\mathbb{E} B)}\le e^{t^2/8}$ for a Bernoulli variable $B$:
multiply over the $q$ coordinates and optimize at $t=1/3$.
Character expansion on $\mathbb{F}_2\times\mathbb{F}_2^n$ gives exactly, with
$b=(1-2p)^{q/2}$,

$$
\mathbb{P}[(\epsilon,\ell)=(0,0)]
=\frac{1+(1-2p)^q+(2q-2)(1-2p)^{q/2}}{2q}
=b+\frac{(1-b)^2}{2q}
\le(1-p)^q+\frac{1}{2q}, \tag{21}
$$

where the inequality uses $1-2p\le(1-p)^2$, hence $b\le(1-p)^q$, and
$0\le b\le1$.
The parity-only character uses all $q$ coordinates; every character
with a nonzero linear part uses $q/2$ coordinates. These are all
$2q$ characters, proving the formula and (18).
When $p\to0$ and $qp\to\lambda<\infty$, $(1-p)^q\to e^{-\lambda}$
and the additive error in (18) vanishes, proving
(19). If $qp\to\infty$, then $(1-p)^q\le e^{-qp}\to0$.
 $\square$

The error in (18) is additive; when $qp\to\infty$
it gives no relative control, and we do not claim
$\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{C}_T(U)]\sim(1-p)^q$ in that regime. Since $qp=2^{n-\rho+1}$,
a finite positive limit $\lambda$ in (19) necessarily lies
in $2^{\mathbb Z}$ in the present fair-bit model.

Permuting coordinates preserves the role distribution and the target
line $T$, so in applying Theorem 5.2 the reference
coordinate may be chosen freely. For this family, taking a coordinate
equal to $1$ as reference leaves exactly one ratio different from $1$,
so $s=1$. In any case this family has exactly two distinct slopes,
so $\nu=1$ and $\mathcal A=qp$ for every choice of reference coordinate. Theorem 5.2 thus already
gives vanishing orbit constancy when $qp\to\infty$, and the lower bound
in (18) shows that this condition is necessary.
Hence the scale $qp=2^{n-\rho+1}$ in Theorem 5.2 cannot
be improved for general coordinate-full directions, and
Theorem 6.1 identifies the critical asymptotics for this
family.

## 7. Positive association and high-density orbit bounds

Return to the four-point coefficient $K$ of (3). The unit
$A$ in this section is supported on a single line $M$, whereas the
target line $T$ may be any line in $G$. No coordinate-full assumption
is needed for the following lower bounds. Put $\theta=2^{-\rho}$.

**Lemma 7.1 (Product-measure association).** 
If $E_1,\ldots,E_m$ are decreasing events of finitely many independent
bits, then $\mathbb{P}[\bigcap_i E_i]\ge\prod_i\mathbb{P}[E_i]$.

*Proof.* 
For two decreasing functions $f,g$, induct on the number of bits in
the identity

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(f,g)=\mathbb{E}\operatorname{Cov}(f,g\mid Z)
+\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbb{E}[f\mid Z],\mathbb{E}[g\mid Z]),
$$

where $Z$ is the last bit. The first term is nonnegative by induction.
If $\mathbb{P}[Z=1]=r$, the second is
$r(1-r)(f_1-f_0)(g_1-g_0)\ge0$, where $f_z=\mathbb{E}[f\mid Z=z]$ and
similarly for $g$. This proves the two-event case. The intersection
of decreasing events is decreasing, so induction on $m$ completes
the proof. This is the product-measure form of positive association
[2], [3].
 $\square$

**Theorem 7.2 (Four-point orbit lower bounds).** 
For every fixed $A\in\mathbb{F}_2[M]$, with $s_A=|\operatorname{supp}(A)|$,

$$
\mathbb{P}_X[\mathcal{C}_T(KA)]\ge(1-\theta)^{4qs_A}
\ge(1-\theta)^{4q^2}. \tag{22}
$$

More precisely, with $\eta=|H\cap(M+T)|$,

$$
\mathbb{P}_X[\mathcal{C}_T(KA)]\ge(1-\theta)^{\min\{4qs_A,\;4|M+T|/\eta\}}. \tag{23}
$$

In particular, if $M,T\le W$ are distinct, then
$\mathbb{P}_X[\mathcal{C}_T(KA)]\ge(1-\theta)^{q^2}$ for every $A\in\mathbb{F}_2[M]$.
For $A$ uniform on $\mathcal{U}(M)$, put $r=(1-\theta)^{4q}$. Then

$$
\mathbb{E}_A\mathbb{P}_X[\mathcal{C}_T(KA)]
\ge L_q(r):=2^{-q}\big((1+r)^q-(1-r)^q\big). \tag{24}
$$

If $M=T$, then for every $A\in\mathcal{U}(T)$ the events
$\mathcal{C}_T(KA)$ and $\mathcal{C}_T(K)$ are equal, and with $h=|H\cap T|$,

$$
\mathbb{P}_X[\mathcal{C}_T(KA)]\ge(1-\theta)^{4q/h}. \tag{25}
$$

*Proof.* 
The union of the supports of all $\tau_t(KA)$, $t\in T$, contains
at most $4qs_A$ points, and it is contained in the subgroup $H+M+T$,
whose order is $|H||M+T|/|H\cap(M+T)|=4|M+T|/\eta$. For each such point $g$, the event
$\{\prod_jX_{j,g_j}=0\}$ is decreasing and has probability
$1-\theta$. If all these events occur, the entire orbit is zero.
Lemma 7.1 proves (22) and
(23). If $M,T\le W$ are distinct lines, then
$M+T=W\supseteq H$, so $\eta=4$ and the exponent in
(23) is at most $q^2$. For a uniform
augmentation-one coefficient on a $q$-point line, the support size
has the binomial distribution conditioned to be odd. Therefore

$$
\mathbb{E}_A r^{s_A}
=2^{-(q-1)}\sum_{k\text{ odd}}\binom qk r^k
=2^{-q}\big((1+r)^q-(1-r)^q\big),
$$

which proves (24).

For the same-line statement, let $I(T)$ be the augmentation ideal
of $\mathbb{F}_2[T]$. The orbit event $\mathcal{C}_T(B)$ is equivalent to
$Q_{eB}(X)=0$ for every $e\in I(T)$, since the elements $1+[t]$
span that ideal. Multiplication by a unit $A\in\mathcal{U}(T)$ maps $I(T)$
bijectively onto itself. Thus $I(T)KA=I(T)K$, giving equality of
the events pointwise in $X$. Apply the first part of the proof
directly to $K$: the support union is $H+T$, of size $4q/h$.
 $\square$

For the individual coefficient $A$, the first bound in
(22) strengthens the union bound
$1-4qs_A\theta$. Replacing $s_A$ by $q$ is a uniformization and
need not strengthen that individual union bound.

**Corollary 7.3.** 
If $\rho=2n+c_n$ and $c_n\to c$, then the two lower bounds in
(22) after uniformization and
(24) tend respectively to

$$
e^{-4\cdot2^{-c}}\quad\text{and}\quad e^{-2\cdot2^{-c}}. \tag{26}
$$

If $\rho-2n\to+\infty$, then $\mathbb{P}_X[\mathcal{C}_T(KA)]\to1$
uniformly over all line-supported $A$. If $M,T\le W$ are distinct,
the bound $(1-\theta)^{q^2}$ from (23) holds for every
$A\in\mathbb{F}_2[M]$ without averaging and tends to $e^{-2^{-c}}$, which
exceeds both constants in (26). For a same-line unit,
the lower bound in (25), at $\rho=n+c_n$ with
$c_n\to c$ and fixed $h$, tends to $e^{-4\cdot2^{-c}/h}$.

*Proof.* 
At $\rho=2n+c_n$, $q^2\theta\to2^{-c}$, so
$(1-\theta)^{4q^2}\to e^{-4\cdot2^{-c}}$.
Also $1-r\sim4q\theta$ and
$((1+r)/2)^q\to e^{-2\cdot2^{-c}}$, whereas
$((1-r)/2)^q\to0$. The remaining assertions follow from the same
logarithmic expansion of $\log(1-\theta)$.
 $\square$

These are lower bounds for the all-zero subevent. The scale $\rho=2n$
is a threshold of the displayed support lower bound; it is not a
determination of the true cross-line orbit transition. The equalities
for survival in Section 4 do not change that distinction.

## 8. Exact verification over $\mathbb{F}_{16}$

We checked the exposed-column count of Lemma 5.1
with $q=16$, $\rho=3$ and $E=\{0,1\}$. Represent the field as
$\mathbb{F}_2[z]/(z^4+z+1)$, label its elements by polynomial bits, and
normalize the last slope coordinate to one. The ordered parameter set is

$$
\{(d_1,d_2,1):d_1,d_2\in\mathbb{F}_{16}^*,\ (d_1,d_2)\ne(1,1)\},
$$

of cardinality $15^2-1=224$. For each slope we tested the three
nonzero relations on $E$. The exposed set was fixed by increasing-label
greedy selection in the graph (13), with all pairs
of distinct rows in $E$ used to construct the graph before the relation
was examined. This specifies all 672 cases without symmetry reductions.

To avoid enumerating $2^{32}$ input pairs per case, condition on the
first role array $A\in\mathbb{F}_2^{16}$. For the second array $B$, the
exposed-column equations form a homogeneous binary linear system
$D_A B=0$, where

$$
D_A(x,u)=\sum_{a\in E}c_a
\sum_{\substack{g\ne0\\a+g=x}}
A_{a+d_1g}\mathbf1_{u=a+d_2g},\qquad x\in F. \tag{27}
$$

The number of second arrays is exactly $2^{16-\operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb{F}_2} D_A}$, so the
number of vanishing input pairs is

$$
N=\sum_{A\in\mathbb{F}_2^{16}}2^{16-\operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb{F}_2} D_A}. \tag{28}
$$

The denominator of the event probability is $2^{32}$.

The computation constructs (27) directly
from nonzero line parameters $g$. It separately checks the affine
indices in (9), including its zero diagonal. An independent
reference enumerates the at most four role-labelled bits in each
exposed column. The program checks that distinct columns use disjoint
sets of variables before multiplying their exact local probabilities.
Neither this reference nor the rank count is derived from the proposed
upper bound. A full input-pair check over $\mathbb{F}_4$ additionally verifies
the conditional-rank count.

All 672 exact counts agree with the reference and satisfy
$N4^{|F|}\le2^{32}3^{|F|}$. Table 1 groups the complete
output; $m$ denotes the relation support size. The largest ratio of
the measured probability to $(3/4)^{|F|}$ is exactly one. All cases
are resolved in this specified finite domain.

## 9. Limitations and open questions

For a singleton and one fixed transverse line-sum unit,
Theorem 5.2 establishes vanishing orbit constancy at
every fixed density $\kappa=\lim\rho/n<1$. The repeated-slope
family in Theorem 6.1 has the exact critical law
$e^{-\lambda}$ for $\lambda=\lim2^{n-\rho+1}$. The generic
$\kappa=1$ window remains an open problem. A formulation should
specify the slope sequence or the sampling law for slopes and
determine which parameters of the slope multiplicities control the
limiting orbit probability. A repeated-slope limit alone does not
classify directions lacking that structure.

For a four-point coefficient multiplied by a uniform line unit, the
cross-line orbit probability has a positive lower bound at
$\rho=2n+O(1)$ and tends to one above that scale. Determining its
actual transition below this sufficient saturation scale requires
more information than the support union. Same-line units preserve
the orbit event exactly and already have a positive lower bound at
$\rho=n+O(1)$. An exact survival probability can therefore
coexist with substantially different orbit behavior as the number
of roles changes.

Theorem 4.3 determines all products restricted to the
three defining lines, including arbitrary repetitions. For three
general supporting lines in $W$, the product equation among the
quotient subalgebras is still a well-defined finite counting problem,
but the Cartesian-support proof need not apply. An exact law for
that larger family is a separate open question.
