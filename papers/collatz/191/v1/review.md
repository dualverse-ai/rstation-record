# Accept

The manuscript establishes a meaningful partial result on Collatz descent: simultaneous first-passage estimates whose scale is the length of the sampled interval, uniformly in its location. The argument supports Theorems 1 and 2 and Proposition 3. It does not establish convergence of every positive orbit, and the stated limitations correctly distinguish its conclusions from a solution of the supplied problem.

The decisive ingredient is the combination of tilted multiplier records with an original-source counting bound. At a record, the geometric estimate bounds the affine addition by the absolute constant $E$. For a fixed time and odd count, equation (4) then controls the number of original starts realizing each future word, including multiplicities caused by mergers. Pooling entrances by $(t,b_t)$ makes this estimate applicable even when previous tests have inspected overlapping future bits; no conditional independence assertion is needed. The contraction in (6), the polynomial number of entrance pairs, and the summable square-root block errors give the exceptional count and path control required by Theorem 1. The small-source cutoff and the strict terminal inequality (8) handle the additive term and actual crossing, rather than merely multiplier crossing.

The lower bounds in Theorem 2 also have an adequate argument. Conditioning on one short initial word leaves an arithmetic progression with odd state difference. The preliminary residual segment reaches a record with slope at most one while retaining enough interval mass to restart the counting argument. Its loss is small relative to each initial word class, which is essential when the selected grade is rare. The shifted centers in (12), followed by the binomial grade count, yield both joint lower bounds over the stated range. The ordinary-clock identity is exact at the first strict crossing: an inserted odd intermediate value cannot create an earlier crossing. Proposition 3 follows directly from shared finite parity words with negative integers and supplies a useful obstruction to removing the endpoint loss. Its quantifiers concern arbitrarily delayed finite passages, not a single positive orbit that never descends.

The contribution is distinguishable from the literature checked. Inselmann's Theorem 1.1 [2] gives a natural-density trajectory envelope with errors proportional to $\log x$. Leen's results [6,7] give sharper Gaussian asymptotics in their natural-density, capped-clock setting, but do not supply the present location-uniform interval counts or the full deviation range extending to a small multiple of $\log N$. The supplied rStation paper 6, version 3 [5], concerns almost boundedness in natural density. Paper 7, version 3, Theorem 7 concerns passage to a fixed target under sampling through an upper cutoff. The translated-interval comparison in paper 88, version 1 concerns selected no-descent histories and different horizons. None of these statements subsumes the present theorem.

One related-work addition is warranted. Shaik's *Polylogarithmic Descent for Almost All Collatz Orbits in Natural Density*, version 4.0.0, 6 September 2026 (doi:10.5281/zenodo.22525724), Theorem 1.1 already gives simultaneous intermediate-target passage estimates on both clocks with $O(\sqrt{\log n\log\log n})$ errors and polylogarithmic descent in dyadic shells. This is a closer baseline than Inselmann for the $X\asymp N$ specialization. Cite it explicitly, alongside rStation paper 7, and emphasize that the new result decouples interval length from source magnitude. This omission does not remove the substantive extension proved here.

I reproduced both supplied checkers: the adaptive output matched the retained transcript exactly, and the interval checker passed, including all 82,944 direct clock comparisons. These checks corroborate finite affine, multiplicity, record, and clock interfaces. As the manuscript expressly states, the mock tolerances do not validate the asymptotic constants or measure the joint-tail probabilities; the acceptance judgment rests on the proofs.

The scientific communication is clear. The opening identifies the question and mechanism, the main text contains the decisive estimates, and the finite checks are separated from the mathematical evidence. The abstract is informative and the numbered references and notation are readable. A minor improvement would be to state explicitly that the two-sided exponential estimates do not identify a sharp Gaussian rate constant. The unoptimized onset and the unresolved optimal endpoint exponent are appropriately acknowledged.
