# Effective denominator growth for two-interval Collatz rotation codings

## 1. The denominator forced by a simple coding

How small can the reduced denominator of a rational Collatz cycle be when
its parity word is generated by a rotation with only two marked intervals?
We give an explicit exponential lower bound, uniform over the rotation,
the marked intervals and the number of odd steps.

The objects are primitive positive rational cycles of the shortcut map,
with parity read modulo two over an odd denominator. Their binary words
have the form $w_j=\mathbf1_B([aj+c]_n)$, where $a$ is invertible modulo
$n$ and $B$ has at most two cyclic components. Equivalently, the word has
a coprime cyclic shift differing from it in at most four bits. This is a
condition on the binary shortcut word, not on the odd-step valuation word.

Let $q$ be the common reduced denominator, $u$ the number of ones, and
$D=2^n-3^u>0$. Put $\alpha=\log_2 3$, $C=u2^n/(3D)$ and
$\ell=\lfloor(n-1)/4\rfloor$. If $t$ is the number of digits in the
canonical continued fraction of $a/n$, our main bound, for $n\ge5$, is
$$
(1+256\alpha)\log_2q>
\ell-\log_2C-108\alpha t+4\alpha-256\alpha\log_2(C+1).
\tag{1}
$$
A classical effective separation estimate for powers of two and three
then gives, for every $n\ge2$ in this family,
$$
q>\frac{2^{n/1643-11}}{n^{16}}.
\tag{2}
$$
The constants are explicit and not optimized. The result controls rational
denominators throughout the stated coding family; it does not assert that
an arbitrary Collatz cycle has such a coding.

The proof couples two constraints on the same rotation. Small continued-
fraction cost bounds the discrepancy of its parity counts and hence its
state height. Large continued-fraction cost forces long repeated blocks.
After clearing the common denominator, exact integer divisibility limits
those repetitions in terms of that same denominator. Eliminating the cost
between the two inequalities removes the large-discrepancy exception.

### Relation to prior work

Armstrong's human-directed research project supplies the main predecessor
mechanisms. Its finite Denjoy–Koksma/Ostrowski argument bounds discrepancies
by variation times continued-fraction cost [1, proof §§1–2]. Its inverse-
continued-fraction argument uses repeated integer blocks to bound that cost,
including a positive-addend formulation for odd cycles of $3x+q$ [2, §§7–10].
We reproduce the latter argument in the shortcut clock; its constants
$16,27,64$ are inherited. We do not claim a new inverse-continued-fraction
method. The change of clock must be proved: odd-step sampling does not
preserve the binary mismatch count, and shortcut blocks can be all-even
or contracting.

The same project's Fibonacci four-arc theorem already combines factor
counts, discrepancy and denominator growth for a specified odd-valuation
template [3, template (T)]. Its general Denjoy–Koksma denominator law
retains a continued-fraction/variation cost that can make its estimate
vacuous [4]. Other results exclude denominator one for substantial
odd-valuation four-mismatch classes [5]. Our proposed contribution is the
shortcut transfer and elimination of the shared continued-fraction cost
for the entire binary two-interval family, giving (1)–(2). The component
arithmetic and symbolic methods retain their prior attribution.

Weaver's finite-descent theorem for rational Collatz dynamics has qualitative
constants and a broad local factor-complexity hypothesis [6, §1]. It does
not state the explicit restricted-family denominator rate (2). The finite
Sturmian approximation results [7, Theorems 1 and 5; 8, Theorem 1] concern
integer approximants to infinite mechanical itineraries; our object is a
closed rational cycle with an arbitrary admissible finite rotation coding.
Mahler's recent complexity–discrepancy cycle bound [9, Theorem 4.1] combines
state-height bounds with parity injectivity under supplied symbolic bounds.
That shared counting mechanism is background here. The additional step is
controlling discrepancy through actual repetitions and eliminating its
continued-fraction parameter while retaining the reduced denominator.

No finite enumeration, convergence verification or external cycle-period
lower bound is needed for (1)–(2). The only quantitative transcendence input
is the classical power separation cited in §3.

## 2. Rational cycles and the coding family

Let $w=(w_0,\ldots,w_{n-1})$ be a primitive nonconstant binary word of
least period $n\ge2$, with $u=\sum_jw_j$ and $D=2^n-3^u>0$.
The shortcut branches on odd-denominator rationals are $T_0(x)=x/2$ and
$T_1(x)=(3x+1)/2$; parity is reduction modulo two in $\mathbb Z_{(2)}$.
Write $\sigma$ for left rotation and $s_j=\sum_{i<j}w_i$.
Here $q$ denotes the reduced rational denominator.

$$
T_w(x)=\frac{3^ux+\rho(w)}{2^n},\quad
\rho(w)=\sum_{j:w_j=1}2^j3^{u-1-s_j},\quad
x_j=\frac{\rho(\sigma^jw)}{D},\quad
q=\operatorname{den}(w)=\frac{D}{\gcd(D,\rho(w))}.
\tag{3}
$$

Let $m=\min_jx_j$, $M=\max_jx_j$, and let $p_w(\ell)$ count length-$\ell$
factors of the complete periodic extension for integer $\ell\ge1$, including
seam-crossing factors.
Set

$$
\alpha=\log_2 3,\quad C=\frac{u2^n}{3D},\quad
F_j=s_j-(u/n)j,\quad F_{j+n}=F_j,\quad
H=\max_jF_j-\min_jF_j.
\tag{4}
$$

The first bound in §5 holds for every word in this common context. The
rotation-coding clauses and the results in §§6–7 additionally assume

$$
w_j=\mathbf1_B([aj+c]_n),\quad 1\le a<n,\quad\gcd(a,n)=1,
\quad B\subset\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z
\text{ has at most two nonempty cyclic components}.
\tag{5}
$$

The counts $(n,u)$ need not be coprime. Use the canonical continued fraction
$a/n=[0;a_1,\ldots,a_t]$ with $a_t\ge2$, and $K=\sum_{i=1}^t a_i$.
Here $t$ counts digits, not odd steps. The coding step is $a$; its unit
mismatch shift is $a^{-1}\pmod n$.

## 3. Imported discrepancy and power separation

**Finite Denjoy–Koksma/Ostrowski inequality [1].**
For a mean-zero function $f$ on $\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z$, define
$\operatorname{Var}(f)=\sum_{r\bmod n}|f(r+1)-f(r)|$.
For the unit rotation $a$ above, all starts $c$ and $0\le N<n$,

$$
\left|\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}f([aj+c]_n)\right|
\le(K-1)\operatorname{Var}(f).
\tag{6}
$$

This is the generic finite mean-zero statement from [1, proof §§1–2]:
proper-convergent blocks cost at most the cyclic variation, and the
Ostrowski digits total at most $K-1$. We import that statement, not the
odd-cycle height formula surrounding it.

**Power separation [10].** Jeffrey C. Lagarias, *Ternary Expansions
of Powers of 2*, arXiv:math/0512006v4 (11 July 2008), Lemma 2.2(1), printed p.10,
equation (2.4), gives for all integers $p$ and $b\ge1$,

$$
\left|\log_3 2-\frac pb\right|\ge\frac1{1200b^{14.3}}.
\tag{7}
$$

The estimate is stated in [10, Lemma 2.2(1), equation (2.4)],
with attribution there to Rhin through Simons–de Weger. It is used only
for the simplified rate and its consequences. With $v=n\log_3 2-u>0$, concavity gives
$1-3^{-v}\ge2v/3$ for $0<v\le1$; for $v\ge1$ use $1-3^{-v}\ge2/3$.
Thus

$$
\frac D{2^n}\ge\frac1{1800n^{13.3}},\qquad
C\le600u n^{13.3}<600n^{14.3}.
\tag{8}
$$

The proof in §6 transfers [2, §§7–10] with the actual positive
addend retained. Neither its earlier externally conditional exclusions
nor their numerical certificates are used.

## 4. Native denominator and coding equivalence

**Proposition 1.** All $x_j$ are positive native rational states, distinct at least period, with
common reduced denominator $q$. Clearing it gives distinct positive integers
$z_j=qx_j$ with branches $z/2$ and $(3z+q)/2$. In particular $q=1$ is precisely
ordinary integer realization. The coding condition (5) is equivalent to

$$
\min_{\substack{1\le h<n\\\gcd(h,n)=1}}
\#\{j:w_j\ne w_{j+h}\}\le4.
\tag{9}
$$

**Proof.** Affine concatenation gives
$2\rho(\sigma w)=3^{w_0}\rho(w)+w_0D$ and the corresponding relation at every
rotation. The first numerator term is odd exactly when the first bit is one;
all later terms are even. Since $\gcd(D,6)=1$, all phases have their indicated
parities and identical numerator gcd with $D$. Thus they are one native closed
orbit with denominator $q$. Equality of two proper phase states would, by
determinism, give a shorter word period.

For a coding step $a$, the shift $h=a^{-1}$ advances coding phase by one.
Its Hamming mismatch count is twice the number of cyclic components of $B$.
Conversely, reading $w_{hk}$ around the residue circle for a unit $h$ gives a
coding set with exactly half that many components, with step $a=h^{-1}$.
Nonconstancy excludes zero mismatches for a unit shift. ∎

**Scope.** These are shortcut mismatches. Sampling odd states instead uses
exponents $e_i\ge1$, expanding by $e_i\mapsto10^{e_i-1}$; mismatch counts do
not transfer unchanged. Repeated presentations must first be reduced to least
period; both numerator and determinant acquire the same repetition factor,
so repeating a presentation cannot improve its reduced denominator.

## 5. Discrepancy-sensitive packing

**Proposition 2.** In the common context,

$$
M<C3^H,\qquad p_w(\ell)<n\ \Longrightarrow\ q>\frac{2^\ell}{C3^H}.
\tag{10}
$$

For (5), additionally

$$
p_w(\ell)\le\min(n,4\ell),\qquad H\le4(K-1).
\tag{11}
$$

**Proof.** Every rotated prefix has centered count at least $-H$. Writing
$\Lambda=2^n/3^u>1$, each term in the numerator sum satisfies
$2^j3^{-s_j}=\Lambda^{j/n}3^{-F_j}<\Lambda3^H$ for $j<n$.
Summing the $u$ terms gives $M<C3^H$. If two distinct phases have the same next
$\ell$ parity bits, the difference of their integer numerators over denominator
$q$ is a nonzero multiple of $2^\ell$: compose the common affine branches and
use coprimality of powers of two and three. This difference is smaller than
$qM$, proving the strict bound.

For (5) each of the $\ell$ letters introduces at most four translated
boundary points around the coding circle, proving the factor bound.
Apply (6) to $f=\mathbf1_B-u/n$, whose cyclic variation is at most four.
The difference of any two centered prefix values is itself a sum over one
cyclic interval of time, so its absolute value is at most $4(K-1)$, with no
additional factor two. ∎

## 6. Integer repetition controls continued-fraction cost

**Proposition 3 (shortcut transfer of [2]).** Under (5), put $L=qm$ and $Y=\log_2(L+q)$. Then

$$
K<27t+64Y,\qquad t\le2\log_2n+1.
\tag{12}
$$

**Proof.** The following is the shortcut-clock transfer, with the common
integer denominator retained throughout.

### Repeated blocks

Clear the common denominator: $z_i=qx_i$ are distinct positive integers, $q$ is odd, and their actual branches are

$$
z_{i+1}=
\begin{cases}
z_i/2,&w_i=0,\\
(3z_i+q)/2,&w_i=1.
\end{cases}
$$

Both branches satisfy

$$
z_{i+1}+q\le\tfrac32(z_i+q). \tag{13}
$$

Suppose a proper binary block of length $Q<n$, with $v$ ones, repeats $N\ge1$ times consecutively. At its cuts put $d_i=z^{(i+1)}-z^{(i)}$. Repeated affine composition gives

$$
d_{N-1}=\frac{3^{v(N-1)}}{2^{Q(N-1)}}d_0.
$$

The numerator and denominator powers are coprime, so $2^{Q(N-1)}\mid d_0$. Primitivity makes $d_0\ne0$: equality after a proper block would give a shorter deterministic cycle. With $\alpha=\log_2 3$ and $Y_0=\log_2(z^{(0)}+q)$,

$$
2^{Q(N-1)}\le|d_0|<(3/2)^Q(z^{(0)}+q),
$$

and therefore

$$
NQ<Y_0+\alpha Q,\qquad
\log_2(z^{(N)}+q)<\alpha Y_0+\alpha(\alpha-1)Q. \tag{14}
$$

For fixed $Q$, write $Z=\log_2(z+q)+\alpha Q$. The repeated segment has length less than its entering $Z$ and satisfies $Z_{\rm out}<\alpha Z_{\rm in}$.

**Discussion.** This is the arithmetic input used by the phase-prefix argument. In its odd clock, positive valuations give at least one denominator factor 2 per arrow. Here every shortcut arrow gives exactly one. The power of 3 counts $v$, not $Q$, but coprimality is all the divisibility step needs. All-zero or contracting blocks are allowed. No growth-sign hypothesis has been silently transferred.

### Coding boundaries

Let $L=\min z_i$ and $Y=\log_2(L+q)$. For every proper rational phase

$$
1\le Q<n,\quad \gcd(p,Q)=1,\quad Qa-pn=\pm j,\quad j\ge1,
$$

we claim

$$
n<16jY+27jQ. \tag{15}
$$

If $jQ\ge n$, this is immediate. Otherwise start the chronological word at its actual minimum and consider its first $\lfloor n/Q\rfloor$ complete $Q$-blocks. Their starting coding phases advance by $\pm j$.

A block at phase $\theta$ has letters $\mathbf1_B(\theta+as)$ for $0\le s<Q$. Each coding boundary supplies one family of $Q$ change points. In a family the points are a uniform $Q$-grid, since $\gcd(p,Q)=1$, perturbed over a range less than $j$. Their cyclic spacing is therefore at least $n/Q-j>0$. There are at most four families.

If a fifth change of actual block pattern occurs, choose one crossed boundary event for each of the first five changes. Assign each event to the half-open interval between successive unwrapped phases, avoiding double counting at a cut. Place discrete coding cuts at half-integers so that integer phases never lie on a cut; this treats either orientation. Two chosen events belong to the same family. If the fifth change occurs after $r_5$ phase steps, then

$$
r_5j\ge n/Q-j,\qquad r_5Q\ge n/j-Q.
$$

The prefix before that fifth change consequently has at most five constant block regimes and length at least $n/j-Q$. If fewer than five changes occur, use all complete blocks. Their length is greater than $n-Q\ge n/j-Q$, and again there are at most five regimes.

Every regime repeats one actual proper $Q$-block. Apply the repeated-segment estimate successively. The total prefix length is less than

$$
(1+\alpha+\alpha^2+\alpha^3+\alpha^4)(Y+\alpha Q).
$$

Since $\alpha<8/5$, the geometric sum is less than 16 and its product with $\alpha$, plus one, is less than 27. Comparison with $n/j-Q$ proves the phase estimate. The actual word, minimum and integer states are retained throughout.

### Proper convergents

Write the canonical continued fraction $a/n=[0;a_1,\ldots,a_t]$, with last digit at least two, and $K=\sum a_i$. For each digit use its preceding convergent $p/Q$, including $0/1$ at the first digit, and put $j=|Qa-pn|$. The continuant split gives

$$
n\ge a_iQj.
$$

Combining with the phase estimate yields $a_i<27+16Y/Q$. The denominators satisfy $Q_{i+2}\ge2Q_i$, so their reciprocal sum is less than four, including the possible two initial denominators 1. Also $t\le2\log_2n+1$. Therefore

$$
K(a/n)<27t+64Y,\qquad t\le2\log_2n+1. \tag{16}
$$

This is the source's inverse-CF mechanism in the exact clock needed here: the unit mismatch shift is $h=a^{-1}$, and its inverse is the same $a$ appearing in the direct discrepancy bound (11).


∎

## 7. Effective denominator growth

**Theorem 4.** Under (5), for $n\ge5$ and
$\ell=\lfloor(n-1)/4\rfloor$,

$$
(1+256\alpha)\log_2q>
\ell-\log_2C-108\alpha t+4\alpha-256\alpha\log_2(C+1).
\tag{17}
$$

Using only the additional power-separation import (7), for all $n\ge2$,

$$
q>\frac{2^{n/1643-11}}{n^{16}}.
\tag{18}
$$

**Proof.**
Put

$$
C=\frac{u2^n}{3D},\qquad z=\log_2q,\qquad
\ell=\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{4}\right\rfloor.
$$

The product identity gives $m=x_{\min}<C$. Indeed,

$$
1-\frac{3^u}{2^n}
=1-\prod_{\text{odd inputs }x_i}\left(1-\frac1{3x_i+1}\right)
\le\sum_{\text{odd inputs }x_i}\frac1{3x_i+1}
\le\frac{u}{3m+1}<\frac{u}{3m}.
$$

Since $L=qm$,

$$
Y=\log_2(L+q)<z+\log_2(C+1). \tag{19}
$$

(10) gives $M<C3^H$, while capacity for $n\ge5$ gives $qM>2^\ell$. Hence

$$
z>\ell-\log_2C-\alpha H. \tag{20}
$$

Use the finite DK bound $H\le4(K-1)$, followed by (12) and the preceding minimum bound:

$$
H<108t+256z+256\log_2(C+1)-4. \tag{21}
$$

Consequently

$$
\boxed{
(1+256\alpha)\log_2q>
\ell-\log_2C-108\alpha t+4\alpha
-256\alpha\log_2(C+1).
} \tag{22}
$$

**Discussion.** This determinant-sensitive inequality combines both continued-fraction regimes. A large $K$ cannot be assigned freely to evade the height estimate: it requires long repetitions, whose integer lattice cost includes the very denominator being bounded.

For (18), the classical estimate gives

$$
C\le600u n^{13.3}<600n^{14.3},\qquad
\log_2C,\ \log_2(C+1)<10+15\log_2n.
$$

Combine $-108\alpha t+4\alpha=-\alpha(108t-4)$ before using $\alpha<8/5$, and use $z\ge0$, $t\le2\log_2n+1$. Then

$$
2053z>5\ell-32523\log_2n-21362
\ge\tfrac54n-32523\log_2n-21367.
$$

Finally,

$$
\frac5{8212}>\frac1{1643},\quad
\frac{32523}{2053}<16,\quad
\frac{21367}{2053}<11
$$

give $z>n/1643-16\log_2n-11$, proving (18). For $n=2,3,4$ its right-hand side is below one and $q\ge1$, so those cases require no empty-factor convention.

∎

### Consequences and scope

**Corollary 5.** The entire primitive entry family has an explicit denominator-growth bound. There is no remaining large-discrepancy exception in this comparison. Moreover $x_{\min}<C<600n^{14.3}$, so (18) implies the separate minimum-scale consequence on this family: along $x_{\min}\to\infty$, necessarily $n\to\infty$, while $\log q=\Omega(n)$ and $\log x_{\min}=O(\log n)$; hence
$\log q/\log x_{\min}\to\infty$.

For an integer cycle in this class, (18) forces

$$
\frac n{1643}<16\log_2n+11.
$$

In particular $n<2^{19}$: at $2^{19}$ the left side exceeds 319 while the right side is 315, and the difference increases thereafter, since
$\log_2(1+1/n)<2/n$ and $n>32\cdot1643$.
This is a scoped finite-period consequence, not a new global Collatz cycle bound or a check of all smaller periods. The theorem's main payload is the denominator rate and its mechanism.


## 8. What the bound resolves

Equation (1) bounds the denominator without a separate restriction on the
continued-fraction digits of the coding rotation. Equation (2) makes the
result exponential in least shortcut period. The same argument explains why
large discrepancy cannot be chosen independently of the denominator: its
continued-fraction cost also forces repeated integer blocks.

The denominator-one consequence is only a necessary finite-period restriction
inside this coding family. We neither classify arbitrary integer cycles nor
check every smaller member. A denominator-cleared $3x+q$ integer orbit is
an ordinary $3x+1$ integer cycle only when $q=1$. Repeating a presentation
cannot strengthen the result, since it leaves the reduced denominator
unchanged and violates the least-period hypothesis.

The restriction to a unit rotation is material. The argument does not give
the same constants for nonunit rotations, more marked intervals, or arbitrary
languages of linear factor complexity. Those extensions are separate questions.
