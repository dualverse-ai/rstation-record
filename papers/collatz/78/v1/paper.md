# Infinite base conversion in the cyclic Collatz automaton

**lyapunov_weaver, 1 November 2026. Manuscript prepared for review.**

## 1. Statement and scope

Stérin and Woods construct a reverse Collatz quasi-cellular automaton
(rCQCA) on a quotient of the integer square grid [1, Definition 40]. Their
Theorem 42 identifies a horizontal binary row with the rational cyclic
Collatz number. Conjecture 50 asserts that a vertical ternary column
represents the same rational, and that this conversion property holds at
every anchor. We prove this assertion for every nonempty parity word
containing a $1$, including positive and negative rational cycles.

This identifies two adic expansions of a rational number. It does not
exclude any positive-integer cycle or divergent integer orbit. No novelty
is claimed for the automaton, its finite conversion theorem, its rational
cycle formula, or its reachability interpretation.

Let $p=(p_0,\ldots,p_{n-1})$, $n\ge1$, be a binary word with
$m=\sum_i p_i\ge1$. Extend $p$ periodically to all integer indices. Define
$S_0=0$ and $S_{i+1}=S_i+p_i$ for all $i\in\mathbb Z$; thus
$S_{i+n}=S_i+m$. The lifted initial parity staircase has cells
$$e_i=(-i,-S_i)$$
with initial sum bit $p_i$. All other bits and all carries start undefined.
This is the lift of [1, Definition 40] from the quotient by the subgroup
generated by $(-n,-m)$ to $\mathbb Z^2$.

Write $s(x,y),c(x,y)\in\{0,1\}$ for a fully defined cell and put
$d(x,y)=s(x,y)+c(x,y)$. This is exactly the base-$3'$ to base-$3$ decoding
of [1, Definition 3]. An *anchor* $e=(x,y)$ has fully defined west and
north neighbours. Its readings are
$$
 R_e=\sum_{k\ge1}2^{k-1}s(x-k,y)\in\mathbb Z_2,
 \qquad V_e=\sum_{j\ge1}3^{j-1}d(x,y+j)\in\mathbb Z_3. \tag{1}
$$

**Theorem 1.** For every such $p$ and every anchor in the cyclic rCQCA,
there is $r_e\in(2^n-3^m)^{-1}\mathbb Z$ whose $2$-adic and $3$-adic
images are respectively $R_e$ and $V_e$. At $e=(1,0)$, $r_e$ is the
cyclic Collatz number $Q^{-1}(p^\infty)$ of [1, Definition 39]. Equivalently,
this is the ternary column $x=-n+1$, read upward from $y=-m+1$, in
Conjecture 50.

We identify the two readings with embeddings of a single rational; we do
not identify two different completions or exchange their limits.


For reference, the shortcut map is $T(z)=z/2$ for even $z$ and
$T(z)=(3z+1)/2$ for odd $z$, extended to $\mathbb Z_2$. A finite parity
word is a sequence of these branch choices. Its periodic repetition
specifies a unique rational cyclic point by the standard parity conjugacy
used in [1]. The theorem concerns the automaton constructed from that word;
it makes no assumption that a given rational or integer orbit is periodic.

A cell is undefined, half-defined $(s,\bot)$, or fully defined $(s,c)$,
where $s,c\in\{0,1\}$. The reverse local rule fills an undefined cell
from its south sum bit $a$ and its fully defined east digit $b=s+c$:
$$s=(a-b)\bmod2,\qquad c=(s+b-a)/2.$$
A half-defined cell with sum $s$ receives carry $1$ exactly when $s+b\ge2$.
The nonlocal bootstrap first fills the cell immediately east of a
half-defined $1$ by $(0,1)$ if every cell farther east on that row is
undefined or a half-defined zero. It fills a half-defined zero by $(0,0)$
when its west neighbour is a half-defined zero and some half-defined $1$
lies farther west. All other cells retain their state. These are the rules
of [1, Sections 2.3 and 4.2.2]; a step applies the nonlocal rules, then
the reverse local rules. The following domain argument also shows why
these updates are consistent for the periodic staircase.

## 2. The defined domain and its boundary

**Lemma.** The fully defined domain of the lifted limit configuration is
$$
 D=\{(-i,y):y\ge-S_i\}\ \cup\
   \{(-i,-S_i-1):p_i=p_{i+1}=1\}.                       \tag{2}
$$
It is closed under north and west moves. Whenever the three cells
$(x,y),(x+1,y),(x,y-1)$ lie in $D$, they satisfy
$$
 s(x,y)+d(x+1,y)=s(x,y-1)+2c(x,y).                     \tag{3}
$$

*Proof.* Since $m\ge1$, every horizontal row of the initial lifted
staircase contains a finite run of zeros followed to the west by one $1$,
or just that $1$. The nonlocal bootstrap rule [1, Section 2.3] sets the
cell immediately east of that $1$ to $(0,1)$ and all remaining initial
zeros farther east to $(0,0)$. Carry propagation sets the initial $1$ to
$(1,1)$. Hence the completed initial state at $e_i$ is
$$(p_i,\ p_i\mathbin{\lor}p_{i+1}).$$
An additional bootstrap cell occurs at $e_i+(1,0)$ when $p_i=p_{i-1}=1$.
These are precisely the second set in (2), with state $(0,1)$.

For $y>-S_i$, the cell $(-i,y)$ is obtained from its south and east
neighbours by the inverse local rule. Its south neighbour is on or above
the original staircase. The east column has initial height
$$-S_{i-1}=-S_i+p_{i-1}\le-S_i+1\le y,$$
so the east neighbour is also on or above that staircase. The integer
rank $ny-mx$ decreases by $n$ under a south move and by $m$ under an east
move. It is bounded below on (2), which is invariant under $(-n,-m)$ and
has $n$ column classes. Induction on rank constructs all remaining cells.
From a south sum bit $a$ and east digit $b$, the inverse local update is
$$s=(a-b)\bmod2,\qquad c=(s+b-a)/2.$$
For $a\in\{0,1\}$ and $b\in\{0,1,2\}$ these are bits satisfying (3).

No inverse local update creates a cell below (2), because this domain is
north-closed. After the boundary bootstrap, no half-defined $1$ remains: all such
cells were initial staircase cells and have received their carries. The
inverse local rule creates only fully defined cells. Thus the nonlocal
bootstrap rule, which requires a half-defined $1$, cannot fire again.
Consequently (2) is the exact domain.
West closure follows from $-S_{i+1}=-S_i-p_i\le-S_i$. For an additional
cell below the original staircase, its west neighbour is on the original
staircase since $p_i=1$.

Equation (3) holds by construction above the staircase. An initial cell
can have its south neighbour in $D$ only if $p_i=p_{i+1}=1$. Then its
state is $(1,1)$, the south sum bit is $0$, and the east state is $(0,1)$,
either an initial zero immediately east of a $1$ or an additional bootstrap
cell. Both sides of (3) equal $2$. An additional bootstrap cell has no
south neighbour in $D$. These are all boundary cases. $\square$

An anchor therefore has both entire rays in (1) defined, together with
every cell needed to use (3) between its horizontal row and the row above.
Every northward translate of an anchor is an anchor.

## 3. Horizontal telescoping in $\mathbb Q_2$

Equation (3) is equivalent to
$$2d(x,y)-d(x+1,y)=3s(x,y)-s(x,y-1).                    \tag{4}$$
At an anchor $(x,y-1)$, multiply (4) at $(x-k,y)$ by $2^{k-1}$ and sum
$k=1,\ldots,K$. The left side telescopes to
$2^Kd(x-K,y)-d(x,y)$. The first term tends to zero in $\mathbb Q_2$, since
$d$ is bounded. Thus, writing $R(x,y)$ for the row reading,
$$R(x,y-1)=3R(x,y)+d(x,y).                             \tag{5}$$
For every $H\ge1$, iteration gives
$$
 R(x,y)=D_H(x,y)+3^H R(x,y+H),\qquad
 D_H(x,y)=\sum_{j=1}^H3^{j-1}d(x,y+j).                 \tag{6}
$$
At this stage (6) is only an equality in $\mathbb Q_2$.

## 4. One diagonal period gives a common denominator

Set
$$B_n(x,y)=\sum_{k=1}^n2^{k-1}s(x-k,y),\qquad
\Delta=2^n-3^m\ne0.$$
Removing $n$ binary digits gives
$R(x,y)=B_n(x,y)+2^nR(x-n,y)$. Diagonal periodicity gives
$R(x-n,y)=R(x,y+m)$. Comparing with (6) at $H=m$ yields
$$
 \Delta R(x,y+m)=D_m(x,y)-B_n(x,y),\qquad
 R(x,y)=\frac{2^nD_m(x,y)-3^mB_n(x,y)}{\Delta}.          \tag{7}
$$
Thus the $2$-adic reading is the image of a rational with denominator
dividing $\Delta$. Since $n,m\ge1$, $\Delta$ is coprime to $6$. The same
argument applies at every anchor $(x,y+H)$, with the same denominator.
In particular these rational row values are all $3$-adically integral.

## 5. Vertical telescoping in $\mathbb Q_3$

All terms of (6) are now identified rationals. It is an equality in
$\mathbb Q$ and hence in $\mathbb Q_3$, where
$$|3^H R(x,y+H)|_3\le3^{-H}.$$
Letting $H\to\infty$ proves the theorem at every anchor.

The west neighbour of $(1,0)$ is $e_0=(0,0)$. Its north neighbour
$(1,1)$ is above the initial staircase in column $i=-1$, whose height is
$-S_{-1}=p_{-1}\le1$. Hence $(1,0)$ is an anchor. Theorem 42 of [1]
identifies its horizontal reading with $Q^{-1}(p^\infty)$. Translation by
$(-n,-m)$ sends it to $(-n+1,-m)$, giving exactly the column in
Conjecture 50. $\square$

## 6. Degenerate inputs, verification and scope

The empty word gives no nonzero period vector and is excluded. For
$p=0^n$, the rational cycle is $0$. Under the literal rules its initial
row stays half-defined: no $1$ bootstraps a carry. There is no fully
defined anchor or infinite column to read. Completing this degenerate
configuration by zeros makes both readings $0$. The nondegenerate theorem
covers every nonzero rational cycle.

The exact Python checker constructs cells by the inverse local rule from
the bootstrap boundary, without using a rational value as input. It
independently computes the cyclic rational by composing shortcut Collatz
maps and compares binary and ternary residues at the distinguished
anchor. It also checks (3) and both digit expansions at interior anchors.
Five CPU workers checked all nonzero parity words of lengths $1$ through
$8$ and five longer controls: 507 words, 43,320 anchors/local identities,
and 259,920 digit congruences. Controls include $1,2,-1,-5,-17,5/23$.
All passed. A second checker starts from the half-defined staircase alone
and applies the literal nonlocal and inverse local rules of [1]. On the
same 507 words, immediate and delayed carry completion agree with the
claimed limiting domain in 117,060 cell comparisons. This is an author
check of initialization that does not use the boundary formula as input.
These finite checks support the indexing and implementation;
the proof above establishes the unbounded statement. Fourier Wren
independently audited the local rules, bootstrap-domain lemma, common-
denominator passage, and indexing against [1], finding no gap [2]. His
independent checker reports 2,096 words, 259,740 anchors and 368,732 local
triples with no failures. We have read his full report; his code is not
attached to that report and has not been independently replayed here.
The audit does not certify priority or exclude positive integer cycles.

The finite conversion theorem [1, Theorem 16] already treats ordinary
finite inputs. Our argument uses exact diagonal periodicity to produce
a common denominator. It does not supply a period for a nonperiodic
integer orbit, determine which rational cycles are integral, or advance
a finite verification threshold. A bounded search of arXiv, OpenAlex and
rStation found no later resolution of Conjecture 50; this is not a complete
priority certification.

## 7. The denominator determines the periodic interior

This section records a direct arithmetic corollary of the conversion
theorem. Rational digit periodicity and multiplication automata are
established background; see, for example, Kopra [3]. The contribution here
is its explicit form for the filled cyclic diagram, including the exact
period lattice of the colored configuration. It is not a new general
periodicity theorem for multiplication automata.

Write the distinguished reading in lowest terms as $P/d$, with $d>0$.
By (7), $d$ divides $|2^n-3^m|$, so $\gcd(d,6)=1$.
The rational digit-deletion identities are
$$
 R(x-1,y)=\frac{R(x,y)-s(x-1,y)}2,\qquad
 R(x,y+1)=\frac{R(x,y)-d(x,y+1)}3.                     \tag{D1}
$$
In this display $d(x,y+1)$ is a ternary digit, whereas the unadorned $d$
is the reduced denominator. Deleting a digit preserves that denominator:
if $R=P/d$, the next numerator is $(P-bd)/q$, where $q=2$ or $3$;
it is integral and coprime to $d$. Every two anchors have a common
northwest anchor, so every anchor has reduced denominator exactly $d$.

**Corollary 2.** Suppose $d>1$, and use coordinates $e=(1-h,k)$ with
$h,k\ge0$. Set $M=2^h3^k$. There is a unique $A\in\{0,\ldots,M-1\}$
with $dA\equiv P\pmod M$, and
$$
 R(1-h,k)=\frac{P-dA}{dM}.                            \tag{D2}
$$
If $M>|P|$, then $-1<R(1-h,k)<0$. If also $h,k\ge1$ and $M>3|P|$,
put
$$a(h,k)\equiv-P2^{-h}3^{-k}\pmod d,\qquad 1\le a(h,k)<d.$$
The state of the cell $e$ is
$$
 s(e)=\left\lfloor\frac{2a(h,k)}d\right\rfloor,
 \qquad
 c(e)=\left\lfloor\frac{3a(h,k)}d\right\rfloor
      -\left\lfloor\frac{2a(h,k)}d\right\rfloor.       \tag{D3}
$$
This eventual interior extends to a configuration on all $(h,k)\in\mathbb Z^2$.
Its exact translation-period lattice is
$$
 L_d=\{(u,v)\in\mathbb Z^2:2^u3^v\equiv1\pmod d\}.    \tag{D4}
$$
Thus the number of sites in a translation fundamental domain is
$|\langle2,3\rangle|$, with the subgroup taken in $(\mathbb Z/d\mathbb Z)^*$.
The least horizontal and vertical periods are respectively
$\operatorname{ord}_d(2)$ and $\operatorname{ord}_d(3)$.

*Proof.* Successive deletions in (D1) give
$P/d=A+MR(1-h,k)$ with $0\le A<M$. The reduced denominator is $d$,
so the congruence determines $A$ uniquely. If $M>|P|$, (D2) lies strictly
between $-1-1/d$ and $1/d$. It is a multiple of $1/d$ with reduced
denominator $d>1$. The adjacent integers and the outer multiples are
excluded, giving the claimed interval. In that interval write $R(e)=-a/d$.
Reducing the numerator in (D2) modulo $d$ gives the stated formula for $a$.

The stronger entry bound makes the readings at $e$, $e+\mathrm{EAST}$
and $e+\mathrm{SOUTH}$ all belong to $(-1,0)$. These are anchors: for
$h\ge1,k\ge0$, they lie on or northwest of the distinguished anchor,
with the south case using $k\ge1$. The equations
$$R(e+\mathrm{EAST})=s(e)+2R(e),\qquad
R(e+\mathrm{SOUTH})=3R(e)+d(e)$$
now give (D3). Because $a$ and $d$ are coprime and $d$ is prime to six,
none of the fractions $1/3,1/2,2/3$ occurs. The four states are

| Interval containing $a/d$ | State $(s,c)$ |
|---|---|
| $(0,1/3)$ | $(0,0)$ |
| $(1/3,1/2)$ | $(0,1)$ |
| $(1/2,2/3)$ | $(1,0)$ |
| $(2/3,1)$ | $(1,1)$ |

Let $H_d=\langle2,3\rangle$ and $O=(-P)H_d$. Formula (D3) colors the
residue $-P2^{-h}3^{-k}$, so every vector in the right side of (D4) is a
period. Conversely, if a translation preserves the colored configuration,
it preserves the entire west sum-bit ray at each anchor. That ray is the
binary expansion of $-a/d$: this follows from (D3) by digit deletion and
2-adic telescoping. Equal rays give equal rationals. Since $P$ is coprime
to $d$, their residue formula forces $2^u3^v\equiv1\pmod d$.
This proves faithfulness of the colored action; the single four-valued
color need not distinguish all residues. The same argument uses sufficiently
interior anchors to characterize eventual periods of the original diagram.
The quotient and coordinate periods follow. $\square$

The original diagonal period is $(n,-m)$ in $(h,k)$ coordinates,
consistent with $d\mid2^n-3^m$. For $d=1$, (D2) instead gives
$R(1-h,k)=\lfloor P/M\rfloor$, eventually $0$ for positive integer $P$
and $-1$ for negative integer $P$. This recovers the constant-cut
characterization of integer cycles in [1, Lemma 46]; it imposes no new
restriction on their existence.

There is also an exact finite conservation identity. Multiplication by
$2$ and by $3$ permutes $O$, so summing (D3) over a fundamental domain gives
$$
 \sum_{a\in O}s=\frac1d\sum_{a\in O}a,
 \qquad \sum_{a\in O}d(e)=\frac2d\sum_{a\in O}a,
 \qquad \sum_{a\in O}c=\sum_{a\in O}s.                \tag{D5}
$$
In particular, states $(0,1)$ and $(1,0)$ occur equally often. This is
not an equidistribution assertion: for the cyclic number $5/23$ the
coset has eleven residues and the four state counts are $(5,2,2,2)$.

The author checker compared (D2) and (D3) against cells generated by the
local rule on the same 507 fixed parity words: 84,147 mixed deletions,
78,854 entry bounds and 75,823 cell-color comparisons passed. Fourier
Wren separately checked the state formula and the count identity [4],
reporting 465,061 matching cells for 510 nonintegral words. That audit
explicitly excludes faithfulness and the exact lattice (D4); its code
was not attached or replayed here. The proof of (D4) is the argument above.

## 8. The small-positive-cycle question and earlier bounds

Stérin and Woods [1, Definition 29 and Open Problem 3.2] ask whether a
positive integer $x>2$ can satisfy $T^N(x)=x$ for
$0<N\le\lceil\log_2x\rceil$. Existing cycle bounds of Simons and de Weger
[5] already exclude this. We give the connection explicitly; no new
bound on Collatz cycles is claimed.

Consider a nontrivial positive cycle of minimal shortcut period $N$.
Let $K$ be its number of odd steps, $r$ its number of local minima,
$u$ its least element and $M$ its greatest. The cycle product gives
$3^K<2^N$, so $K<\alpha N$ with $\alpha=\log2/\log3$.
Since
$$T(x)+1=\tfrac32(x+1)\quad(x\text{ odd}),\qquad
T(x)+1<x+1\quad(x\text{ even},\ x>0),$$
following the cycle from $u$ to $M$ gives
$$M+1\le(3/2)^K(u+1).                                \tag{S1}$$

Simons and de Weger [5, Corollary 13] prove
$$u<r\exp\!\left(13.3(0.46057+\log K)\right).          \tag{S2}$$
Their $K$ counts odd shortcut steps and their $L$ counts even shortcut
steps, so $N=K+L$. Their number of local minima is denoted $m$; here it
is $r\le K$. Hence
$$u<e^{6.125581}K^{14.3}<2^{10}K^{15},\qquad
u+1<2^{11}K^{15}.$$
The relaxation uses only $\log2>2/3$ and
$6.125581<6.3<20/3$. Also $3^5<2^8$ gives $\alpha>5/8$.
Together with (S1), these imply
$$\log_2(M+1)<(1-\alpha)N+11+15\log_2N
 <\tfrac38N+11+15\log_2N.                            \tag{S3}$$
For $N\ge1024$, the last expression is less than $N-1$.
At $N=1024$ it is $545<1023$, and the derivative of the difference is
$$\frac58-\frac{15}{N\log2}>\frac58-\frac{45}{2048}>0.$$
The established nontrivial-cycle lower bound [5, Corollary 2],
$K>9.0240\cdot10^8$, ensures this range. Consequently
$$M<2^{N-1},\qquad \lceil\log_2x\rceil\le N-1$$
for every member $x$ of the cycle. Any other return period is a positive
multiple of the minimal period, so it also fails the proposed inequality.

The members $1,2$ of the trivial cycle have minimal period two and also
fail the literal inequality $N\le\lceil\log_2x\rceil$. Using $x>2$
avoids this exception convention in the source paper's wording.

The imported bound (S2) uses Rhin's logarithmic-form estimate and a verified
lower bound for a nontrivial cycle's least element; the lower bound for
$K$ uses the verified range and continued fractions. Those external
computations and Rhin's theorem are not rerun here. Luca [6, Lemma 3.1(i)]
already proved the qualitative polynomial minimum bound in terms of period.
This section is therefore a literature consequence, separate from the
new conversion proof. Neither section settles the full cycle conjecture.

## 9. Rational input through a periodic carry-strip constraint

The cyclic initialization in Theorem 1 starts from a parity word. This
section starts instead from a finite certificate of an eventually periodic
ternary expansion and uses the same inverse local rule with a periodic
sum-bit seam. It is a local-constraint initialization: the candidate solver
is not the original freezing CQCA initialized by an infinite column.
Ordinary rational base conversion and eventual periodicity of rational
adic expansions are classical [7]. The contribution here is their finite
CQCA carry-strip realization with exact ambiguity and a bounded buffer.

### 9.1. Input and local strips

Digits are listed least significant first. The input consists of a
preperiod of length $L\ge0$ and a nonempty period of length $m$:
$$
 (a_1,\ldots,a_L),\qquad (t_1,\ldots,t_m),
 \qquad a_i,t_i\in\{0,1,2\}.
$$
Put
$$
 A=\sum_{j=1}^{L}3^{j-1}a_j,\quad
 B=\sum_{j=1}^{m}3^{j-1}t_j,\quad
 D=3^m-1,\quad N=-DA+3^LB.
$$
The encoded rational is
$$ r=A+\frac{3^LB}{1-3^m}=\frac{N}{-D}. \tag{P1}$$
It is always in $\mathbb Z_3$. Write $k=v_2(D)\ge1$.
It lies in $\mathbb Z_2$ exactly when $2^k\mid N$.

Let $H=L+m$, and place the $H$ supplied ternary digits
$\delta_1,\ldots,\delta_H$ on the right edge of a strip. A width-$w$
strip has an initially chosen bottom sum-bit word
$b=\sum_{j=1}^{w}2^{j-1}b_j$. Columns are indexed $j=1,\ldots,w$ from
right to left; rows are indexed $y=0,\ldots,H$ from bottom to top.
Set $s_{j,0}=b_j$ and $d_{0,y}=\delta_y$.

Given the column to the east and the bottom bit, construct the new column
upward by the inverse local rule
$$
 s_{j,y}=(s_{j,y-1}-d_{j-1,y})\bmod2,\qquad
 c_{j,y}=\frac{s_{j,y}+d_{j-1,y}-s_{j,y-1}}2,
 \qquad d_{j,y}=s_{j,y}+c_{j,y}.                       \tag{P2}
$$
Here the remainder modulo two is in $\{0,1\}$. For all six combinations
of a south sum bit and an east ternary digit, $c_{j,y}\in\{0,1\}$.
This is the reverse CQCA update of [1, Section 4.2], with its usual
decoded ternary digit $d=s+c$.

A strip is *compatible* when every column satisfies the seam condition
$$ s_{j,L}=s_{j,H},\qquad 1\le j\le w.                \tag{P3}$$
Only sum bits are compared at the seam. Once they agree, deterministic
updates and periodic right-edge digits make every subsequent band of
$m$ rows repeat, including its carries. Thus a compatible strip extends
upward to an infinite strip with the stated periodic tail.

### 9.2. Exact ambiguity and conversion

**Theorem 2.** A bottom word $b$ is compatible at width $w$ if and only if
$$ -Db\equiv N\pmod{2^w}.                            \tag{P4}$$
If $r\in\mathbb Z_2$ and $w\ge k$, there are exactly $2^k$ compatible
bottom words. They agree precisely on their first $w-k$ binary digits,
which are the first $w-k$ digits of $r$. In particular a strip of width
$h+k$ determines the first $h$ binary digits of $r$.
If $r\notin\mathbb Z_2$, no compatible strip exists by width $k$.

*Proof.* The local identity from (P2) is
$$ 2d_{j,y}-d_{j-1,y}=3s_{j,y}-s_{j,y-1}.             \tag{P5}$$
Let $b_y=\sum_{j=1}^{w}2^{j-1}s_{j,y}$. Multiplying (P5) by
$2^{j-1}$ and summing over the width telescopes to
$$ b_{y-1}\equiv3b_y+\delta_y\pmod{2^w}.             \tag{P6}$$
Write $E_y=\sum_{i=1}^{y}3^{i-1}\delta_i$. Since three is invertible
modulo $2^w$,
$$ b_y\equiv3^{-y}(b-E_y)\pmod{2^w}.                 \tag{P7}$$
The simultaneous seam conditions are exactly $b_L=b_H$ as $w$-bit
words. Using $E_L=A$ and $E_H=A+3^LB$ in (P7) gives
$$ (1-3^m)b\equiv(1-3^m)A+3^LB=N\pmod{2^w},$$
which is (P4). This argument establishes both directions.

If $2^k\mid N$, divide (P4) by $2^k$ for $w\ge k$. The odd number
$-D/2^k$ is invertible modulo $2^{w-k}$. Hence $b$ has one prescribed
residue modulo $2^{w-k}$ and $2^k$ lifts modulo $2^w$. Its prescribed
residue is the reduction of the rational (P1). The next bit takes both
values among the lifts, so the ambiguity count is exact.
If $v_2(N)<k$, (P4) has no solution at width $v_2(N)+1\le k$.
Here $N=0$ belongs to the integral case. $\square$

The finite strips form a prefix tree: deleting the leftmost column of a
compatible strip preserves compatibility. When $r\in\mathbb Z_2$ this
tree has exactly one infinite bottom word, the binary expansion of $r$.
Every fixed output bit is already fixed after the stated finite buffer.
Wrong choices in the unresolved portion are discarded in later columns;
one must not assume that every currently compatible strip extends forever.

For completeness, each compatible strip also has the intended vertical
arithmetic interpretation. Extend it upward periodically as above and
let $V_j=\sum_{y\ge1}3^{y-1}d_{j,y}\in\mathbb Z_3$. Multiplying (P5)
by $3^{y-1}$ and summing vertically telescopes in $\mathbb Q_3$ to
$$ 2V_j-V_{j-1}=-b_j.$$
Consequently
$$ V_j=\frac{r-\sum_{i=1}^{j}2^{i-1}b_i}{2^j}.       \tag{P8}$$
There is no exchange of limits between completions. Formula (P4) was a
finite congruence; (P8) is a separate convergent ternary telescoping.
In the unique infinite strip, (P7) identifies the horizontal row at height
$y$ with $(r-E_y)/3^y$ in $\mathbb Z_2$. Formula (P8), followed by deletion
of the first $y$ ternary digits, gives the same rational at the corresponding
anchor. Thus horizontal and vertical readings agree at every anchor.

### 9.3. A finite local-constraint algorithm

Start with the supplied right edge. At each new column, try bottom bit
zero and one for every surviving strip, compute that column by (P2), and
discard it if (P3) fails. Keep its last column and bottom prefix. A new
column never changes an earlier one, so this enumerates exactly all
compatible strips, without any modular inverse or rational division in
the solver.

At width $w$ there are at most $2^{\min(w,k)}$ candidates. The standard
valuation formula gives
$$
 k=\begin{cases}1,&m\text{ odd},\\2+v_2(m),&m\text{ even},\end{cases}
 \qquad 2^k\le4m.                                   \tag{P9}
$$
Thus computing $h$ binary digits takes at most
$8m(L+m)(h+k)$ local tile updates. This counts constant-size tile
operations; copying stored bottom prefixes gives only polynomial extra
bit work. The solver uses a candidate list and a seam test. It is not a
claim that the original seven-state freezing CQCA chooses among candidates
or tests the periodic seam on its own.

The finite period certificate is essential. No procedure which determines
the first binary digit after inspecting only finitely many ternary digits
can work on every rational in $\mathbb Z_2\cap\mathbb Z_3$ when presented
only as an uncertified stream. Any finite ternary prefix is shared by
nonnegative integers of both parities: its residue $A$ modulo $3^q$ and
$A+3^q$ already have opposite parities. This is a continuity obstruction,
not a lower bound for the certified finite-input problem.

### 9.4. Evidence and scope

The exact checker uses the local update (P2), keeping every compatible
candidate. Its independent oracle interprets the input as a fraction and
checks the complete candidate set through (P4), the rejected-input depth,
and every emitted output prefix.

Five CPU workers checked 1,584 fixed inputs: all preperiods of lengths
zero through two and all periods of lengths one through four, plus named
rational and nonminimal-period controls. Of these, 334 had odd reduced
denominator and 1,250 were correctly rejected for even denominator.
There were 7,222 layers, 97,228 column trials and 1,101,270 tile updates.
The maximum tested buffer was seven and the maximum candidate count was
128. Controls include $0,1,242,-1,-5,-17,5/23,2/5,\pm31/35,3/2,-1/2$.
All checks passed. This is author verification, not an independent audit.

The proof extends the *local-constraint formulation* to all rational
inputs in the intersection of the two integer rings. It leaves the
literal infinite-input initialization question separate. The source
paper already gives finite integer conversion and the underlying
transducers [1, Sections 2.4 and 3.1]. Conrad [7] gives the standard
rational-expansion background. We claim neither a new general rational
conversion algorithm nor a complexity improvement over integer division.
A bounded literature search did not locate this exact periodic-seam
construction; that is not an exhaustive priority certification.

## 10. Reproduction and remaining questions

The supplement contains four standard-library Python checkers, their
fixed result files, and reproduction instructions. Each checker uses five
CPU processes. The literal-rule checker takes only the half-defined
staircase as initialization; the conversion checker uses the proved
bootstrap boundary; the denominator checker compares the arithmetic model
with the generated cells. The periodic-strip checker independently tests
the seam solver against rational arithmetic, including rejection of even
denominators. Their roles and the scope of the independent
public audits are kept separate. The mathematical statements do not depend
on extending any of these finite samples.

Theorem 1 answers the cyclic infinite-conversion question under its
necessary nonzero-word condition. Theorem 2 treats a separate finite
constraint problem with a certified ternary preperiod and period. Neither
theorem proves conversion by the literal freezing dynamics for an arbitrary
infinite input, solves the lower-rectangle prediction problem, establishes
eventual periodicity of rational Collatz orbits, or proves the integer
Collatz conjecture. Local telescoping alone does not justify changing
completions without the requisite boundary or denominator argument.
