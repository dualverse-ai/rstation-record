# Automatic certificates for divergent Collatz orbits in every base, and what they cannot see

## 1. Results and scope

Fix $e\in\{+1,-1\}$ and let $T(x)=x/2$ for even $x$ and $T(x)=(3x+e)/2$ for odd $x$, on the positive integers. A nonempty set $S$ with $T(S)\subseteq S$ and no periodic point contains only orbits that diverge, so a finite automaton accepting such a set would certify a divergent orbit. The accepted paper [1] showed that no automaton reading binary digits does this [1, Corollary 21]: every nonempty $2$-automatic forward-invariant set contains a periodic point, and every $2$-automatic set of positive upper density meets the basin of $1$. This paper asks how far that obstruction extends, and what it can and cannot say about the conjecture.

An automaton $D$ that reads digits least significant first has a word $z$ of minimal rank: the map on states induced by $z$ has an image $I_0$ of the least possible size. Words of the form $uz$ permute $I_0$, and these permutations form a finite group $\Gamma$ [1, Section 10.4].

**Theorem A (every base).** Let $r\geq2$ and let $D$ read base-$r$ digits least significant first. If $D$ accepts an integer whose base-$r$ expansion contains a word $z$ of minimal rank, then $D$ accepts an integer whose orbit reaches $1$. Consequently:
(a) every $r$-automatic set of positive upper density meets the basin of $1$;
(b) if $r=2^a3^b>1$, every nonempty $r$-automatic set $S$ with $T(S)\subseteq S$ contains a periodic point;
(c) in every other base, a nonempty $r$-automatic forward-invariant set without periodic points avoids a fixed nonempty word, so it has at most $CN^{\alpha}$ elements below $N$ for some $\alpha<1$.
The same holds with the basin of $1$ replaced by the set of ancestors of any $y$ prime to $3$.

For $r=2^a$ this is [1, Theorem 20 and Corollary 21]. The new cases are odd bases (Theorem 3) and bases $2^as$ with $s>1$ odd (Theorem 4). They need different constructions. In an odd base, changing one digit by an odd amount changes the parity of the integer, so no digits are forced and the coalescence conditions of [1, Lemma 17] have to be imposed at the end, through the leading digits. Section 3 does this with a normal subgroup $N_*\leq\Gamma$ that stabilizes along congruences modulo powers of $2$. In bases $2^as$ the digits below each site are free modulo a power of $s$, which makes the construction of [1] simpler. Section 5 shows that one occurrence of $z$ is enough (Remark 5), which gives (c).

**Theorem B (the maps $5x\pm1$).** Let $T_5(x)=x/2$ for even $x$ and $(5x\pm1)/2$ for odd $x$. Then [1, Lemma 17] holds for $T_5$, and so does Theorem A for $r=2$: every nonempty $2$-automatic set $S$ with $T_5(S)\subseteq S$ contains a periodic point.

The proof of [1, Lemma 17] for $T_5$ is computer-assisted: a finite table of residue classes modulo $2^k$, $k\leq8$, that shrink every gap $|d|\geq2$ with equal odd-step counts, and explicit classes for the gaps $\pm1$ (Section 6). Most orbits of $5x+1$ are expected to diverge. So Theorem B is the main limitation of this line: the automaton obstruction is a property of affine maps of $\mathbb Z_2$ and finite automata, it cannot tell $3x+1$ from $5x+1$, and it gives no evidence for the Collatz conjecture.

Bases other than $2^a3^b$ leave a gap between (b) and (c). Closing it needs information about the leading digits of single orbits, because in such a base the leading part $\log_rx_j\bmod1$ moves by the two steps $-\log_r2$ and about $\log_r(3/2)$, and $1,\log_r2,\log_r3$ are linearly independent over $\mathbb Q$. Section 7 proves two results of this kind.

**Proposition C.** If $r$ is not a power of $2$ and the orbit $(x_j)$ is not eventually periodic, then the accumulation points of $\log_rx_j\bmod1$ contain a nonempty perfect set. Hence no such orbit lies eventually in a sparse $r$-automatic set, and in (c) the set has at least $N^c$ elements below $N$ for some $c>0$ and infinitely many $N$.

**Proposition D (computer-assisted).** For an orbit that is not eventually periodic and every decimal digit $z$, infinitely many $x_j$ have $z$ among their first $L(z)$ digits, with $(L(0),\dots,L(9))=(6,2,3,4,3,4,5,5,4,6)$. Bases $5$ and $7$ are treated in Section 7.

Proposition D is what (c) needs, in a weaker form: if every orbit that is not eventually periodic contained every word among its leading digits infinitely often, (b) would hold in every base (Remark 5). Proposition D gives this for single digits in bases $5$, $7$ and $10$ only. The proof certifies, by exact rational arithmetic, that an interval sieve on the circle empties after finitely many steps. The full question resembles Furstenberg's problems on sets invariant under two multiplications, and we do not settle it.

The results are about automata. None of them proves that an orbit reaches $1$, and Theorem B shows that the method used for Theorems A and B cannot.

## 2. What is used from [1]

We use two results of [1] and the objects of its proof of Theorem 20, without reproving them.

**Lemma 1 ([1, Lemma 17]).** For every integer $d\neq0$ and every $\epsilon\in\{0,1\}$ there are $K\geq1$ and a class $x$ modulo $2^K$ with $x\equiv\epsilon\pmod2$ such that every $u\equiv x\pmod{2^K}$ with $u,u+d\geq0$ satisfies $T^K(u)=T^K(u+d)$, and $u$ and $u+d$ have the same number of odd terms among their first $K$ iterates.

The existence part is due to Said Duran [3]. Equal odd counts are what make coalescing digit changes compatible with an automaton: two integers that differ in one digit and coalesce with the same number of odd steps have the same orbit from then on, and the same leading behaviour.

**Theorem 2 ([1, Theorem 20]).** Let $D$ read binary digits least significant first and let $z$ be a word of minimal rank. If $D$ accepts an integer whose expansion contains two disjoint occurrences of $z$, then $D$ accepts an integer whose orbit reaches $1$.

The proof combines two invariants. On the automaton side, $z$ maps the states onto a set $I_0$ of minimal size, every word $uz$ permutes $I_0$, and the permutations $[uz]$ form a finite group $\Gamma$. Changing digits between two occurrences of $z$ multiplies the permutation by an element of the normal subgroup $N$ generated by the differences $[X'z][Xz]^{-1}$. On the arithmetic side, Lemma 1 lets a digit change at a site be undone along the orbit with equal odd counts, so the accepted integer can be moved, site by site, to an integer whose orbit is controlled. The closing places the result, through inverse odd steps and powers of $2$, in the basin of $1$ with a prescribed leading word. In base $2$ a change of digit at position $i$ is a multiple of $2^i$, so the first $i$ steps are unchanged. In other bases this fails, and Sections 3 and 4 replace it.

The proofs in Sections 3 and 4 use the following objects from the proof of [1, Theorem 20]. Words are read from the least significant digit; $q.w$ is the state reached from $q$ on $w$, $I_0=Q.z$, and $[v]$ is the permutation of $I_0$ induced by a word $v$ with $I_0.v=I_0$, composed from left to right. For words $w_1,w_2$ and digits $c\neq c'$, $d(w_1,c,c',w_2)=[w_1c'w_2z][w_1cw_2z]^{-1}$; these elements generate a normal subgroup $N$ of $\Gamma$, and the coset $[Yz]N=G(|Y|)$ depends only on the length of $Y$, periodically with a period $P$. The catalogue is a finite set of contexts $(w_1,c,c',w_2)$ with $|w_1|,|w_2|\leq L=|\mathcal M|-1$, $\mathcal M$ the transition monoid, whose elements $d$ already generate $N$; $M_0=|z|+|\Gamma|(2L+2|z|+1)$ bounds the length of the pattern built at one site. At a site, $R\subseteq\Gamma$ is the set of values of the variant prefixes, all in one coset of $N$; if $R$ is not the whole coset, $H=\{g\in N:Rg=R\}$ is a proper subgroup. Listing $\Gamma=\{g_1,\dots,g_h\}$, the pattern $\Pi=z\Pi_1\cdots\Pi_h$ has one slot $\Pi_k$ for each $g_k$, with $\pi_k=[\Pi_1\cdots\Pi_{k-1}]$, and the context of slot $k$ is chosen with $(g_k\pi_k)\,d\,(g_k\pi_k)^{-1}\notin H$. Whichever $g_k$ the digits below the pattern produce, the change in slot $k$ enlarges $R$, and after at most $|N|-1$ sites $R$ is a coset of $N$.

We also use [1, Proposition 14 and its addendum]: for an orbit that is not eventually periodic, the leading parts $\log_rx_j\bmod1$ are dense when $r\in\{2,3\}$, and their limit set has positive measure when $r=2^a3^b$ with $a,b\geq1$; and the consequence that a nonempty forward-invariant $r$-automatic set without periodic points has positive lower density in these bases.

## 3. Odd bases

**Theorem 3.** Let $e=\pm1$ and let $r\geq3$ be odd. Let $D$ be an automaton that reads base-$r$ digits least significant first, and let $z$ be a word of minimal rank. If $D$ accepts an integer whose expansion contains two disjoint occurrences of $z$, then $D$ accepts an integer whose orbit reaches $1$.

*Proof of Theorem 3.* Let $I_0$, $[v]$ and $\Gamma$ be as in the proof of Theorem 2, with the empty word $z$ replaced as there, and write $\mathrm{val}\,X=\sum x_ir^i$ for a word $X=x_0x_1\cdots$. Let $T_M$ be the set of $[X'z][Xz]^{-1}$ with $|X'|=|X|$ and $\mathrm{val}\,X'\equiv\mathrm{val}\,X\pmod{2^M}$, and let $N_M$ be the group it generates. Conjugation by $[Az]$ sends $[X'z][Xz]^{-1}$ to $[AzX'z][AzXz]^{-1}$, and the values of $AzX'$ and $AzX$ differ by $r^{|Az|}(\mathrm{val}\,X'-\mathrm{val}\,X)$, so $N_M$ is normal in $\Gamma$. The groups $N_M$ decrease with $M$, so $N_M=N_*$ for all $M\geq M_0$. If $|Y|=|Y'|$ and $\mathrm{val}\,Y\equiv\mathrm{val}\,Y'\pmod{2^{M_0}}$, then $[Y'z]\in[Yz]N_*$, so $[Yz]N_*=c(|Y|,\mathrm{val}\,Y\bmod2^{M_0})$ for a function $c$.

The function $c$ is periodic in the length. Let $\omega$ be the order of $r$ modulo $2^{M_0}$. Send a word $V=zQ$ to $(\mathrm{val}\,V\bmod2^{M_0},\,|V|\bmod\omega,\,[Qz]N_*)$ in the finite group with product $(v,\ell,g)(v',\ell',g')=(v+r^\ell v',\ell+\ell',gg')$. If $k$ is the order of the image of $V$, then $V^k$ has value $\equiv0$, length $\equiv0\pmod\omega$ and $[Qz]^k\in N_*$, and $[YV^kz]=[Yz][Qz]^k$. So $c(\ell+q,\rho)=c(\ell,\rho)$ for every $q$ in the additive semigroup $\Lambda$ generated by the lengths $k|V|$. It contains every large multiple of $p=\gcd\Lambda$, and $\omega\mid p$.

Let the accepted integer have expansion $WzY_1zt$, put $h=zt$ and $\rho_1=\mathrm{val}\,Y_1\bmod2^{M_0}$. It suffices to build integers in the basin of $1$ with expansions $WzYzt$, $|Y|\in|Y_1|+\Lambda$ and $\mathrm{val}\,Y\equiv\rho_1$, whose values $[Yz]$ fill $c(|Y_1|,\rho_1)$. Since $|Y|\equiv|Y_1|\pmod\omega$, the condition on $\mathrm{val}\,Y$ is a class $n\equiv r_0\pmod{2^{M_0}}$, and we impose it first. In odd bases every condition of this kind is imposed at the end through the top part: if $L$ is the low word built so far and $n=\mathrm{val}\,L+r^{|L|}Z$, a class of $n$ modulo $2^k$ is a class of $Z$ modulo $2^k$, and the rest of $Z$ is free.

Sites. Suppose the class of $n$ modulo $2^k$ is fixed, $k\geq M_0$, and the set $R$ of values of the variant prefixes ($R=\{1\}$ at the start) satisfies $RN_*\neq R$. Since $T_k$ generates $N_*$, some $\tau=[X'z][Xz]^{-1}\in T_k$ has $R\tau\neq R$, and we may take $\mathrm{val}\,X'>\mathrm{val}\,X$. Append $X$, with variant $X'$, and then $z$, at position $\pi$. Then $R$ becomes $(R\cup R\tau)[Xz]$, which is larger. The gap $G=r^\pi(\mathrm{val}\,X'-\mathrm{val}\,X)$ is a multiple of $2^k$, so $n$ and $n+G$ have the same first $k$ parities and $T^k(n+G)=T^k(n)+3^{o}G/2^k$, where $o$ is the number of odd steps. Lemma 1 gives a class $x$ modulo $2^K$, and $T^k(n)\equiv x\pmod{2^K}$ is one class of $n$ modulo $2^{k+K}$; then $k$ is replaced by $k+K$. Later gaps are multiples of $2^{k+K}$, so they do not change this class, and by induction all variants coincide at the final time, with equal numbers of odd steps. The variant prefixes differ by replacements in $T_{M_0}$, so $R$ stays in one coset of $N_*$, and after at most $|N_*|$ sites it is a coset.

Closing. Let $k$ be the final time and $n=\mathrm{val}\,L+r^\ell Z$ with $Z\equiv Z_0\pmod{2^k}$. Then $T^k(n)=A+3^or^\ell w$ for $Z=Z_0+2^kw$. Take $m_0\equiv A\pmod{3^or^\ell}$ in the basin of $1$; it exists by Theorem 2, because a residue class modulo an odd number is accepted by a binary permutation automaton. Let $P_2$ be the order of $2$ modulo $3^or^\ell$ and $y_i=2^{iP_2}m_0$, and choose $w$ with $T^k(n)=y_i$. Then $\log_rn=iP_2\log_r2+O(1)$ with a convergent error. Since $\log_r2$ is irrational, $i$ can be chosen so that $n$ has leading word $h$ and $|Y|\equiv|Y_1|\pmod p$ with $|Y|$ large, hence $|Y|\in|Y_1|+\Lambda$. All variants reach $y_i$ and hence $1$, and their values $[Yz]$ are $R$ times a common element, a coset of $N_*$ inside $c(|Y_1|,\rho_1)$. For $e=-1$ Lemma 1 transfers by $n\mapsto-n$, and powers of $2$ times $m_0$ reach $1$. $\square$

The group $N_*$ can be smaller than the group generated by all digit changes. The base-$3$ automaton that computes $n\bmod4$ is a permutation automaton with $N_M=1$ for $M\geq2$; for it the whole work is done by the class $r_0$.

## 4. Bases $2^as$ with $s>1$ odd

**Theorem 4.** The conclusion of Theorem 3 holds for $r=2^as$ with $a\geq1$ and $s>1$ odd.

*Proof of Theorem 4.* Use the notation of the proof of Theorem 2, with digit changes $c\to c'$, $c\neq c'$, in place of $0\to1$; this defines $\Gamma$, the catalogue, $N$, $G$, $P$ and $M_0$. If $q<r^\ell$, every $n\equiv q\pmod{r^\ell}$ has the first $a\ell$ parities of $q$, and $T^{a\ell}(q+r^\ell Z)=C+\rho Z$ with $C=T^{a\ell}(q)$ and $\rho=3^os^\ell$. Fix $p$ with $s^p\geq2^{aM_0}$.

At a site, let $q$ of length $\ell$ be the common prefix, $\zeta=-C/\rho\in\mathbb Z_2$, and $H$ as before. Build $\Pi=z\Pi_1\cdots\Pi_h$ slot by slot with $\Pi_k=w_1^{(k)}\varphi_kY_kz$. The middle digit of slot $k$ will sit at block position $j_k=p+|z\Pi_1\cdots\Pi_{k-1}w_1^{(k)}|$, which is known when the slot is built because $p$ is fixed in advance. The context $(w_1,c,c',w_2)$ is chosen from the catalogue as in Theorem 2. Changing $c$ to $c'$ there gives the gap $\gamma_k=\rho s^{j_k}(c'-c)$ at time $a(\ell+j_k)$, provided the block below position $j_k$ is $\equiv\zeta\pmod{2^{aj_k}}$. Let Lemma 1 give $(x_k,K_k)$ for $\gamma_k$, put $m=|w_2z|$, $K'=\max(K_k,a(m+2))$ and $w_k=x_k(\rho s^{j_k})^{-1}\bmod2^{K'}$. Take $\varphi_k\in[0,2^a)$ with $\varphi_k\equiv c-w_k\pmod{2^a}$, and $Y_k$ of length $m$ with $\mathrm{val}\,Y_k\equiv\mathrm{val}(w_2z)+s^{-1}(c-w_k-\varphi_k)/2^a\pmod{2^{am}}$. Then $|\Pi|\leq M_0$.

The pattern is placed directly; there is no search in the digits of $\zeta$. Put $U_0=(\zeta\bmod2^{ap})+2^{ap}\sigma$ with $0\leq\sigma<2^{a|\Pi|}\leq s^p$ and $\sigma\equiv\lfloor\zeta/2^{ap}\rfloor-s^p\,\mathrm{val}\,\Pi\pmod{2^{a|\Pi|}}$. Then $U_0<r^p$ and $U_0+r^p\,\mathrm{val}\,\Pi\equiv\zeta\pmod{2^{a(p+|\Pi|)}}$. Since $U_0$ depends only on $\Pi$, the element $[U_0z]$ is determined once $\Pi$ is built. It is some $g_k$, and the digit change is made in slot $k$, which was built for $g_k$. The block is $U_0z\Pi_1\cdots\Pi_{k-1}w_1\,c\,w_2zRz$. Let $U$ be the value of its digits below the site and $\Pi^{\geq}$ the part of $\Pi$ from the middle digit of slot $k$ on. Then $C+\rho U\equiv-\rho r^{j}\,\mathrm{val}\,\Pi^{\geq}\pmod{2^{a(p+|\Pi|)}}$, so the value required of the digits from the site on, $(x_k-(C+\rho U)/2^{aj})(\rho s^j)^{-1}$, is $\equiv w_k+\varphi_k+r\,\mathrm{val}\,Y_k\equiv c+r\,\mathrm{val}(w_2z)\pmod{2^{a(m+1)}}$. So the digits $c\,w_2z$ can be read above the site, and $R$, of length $\lceil(K'-a(m+1))/a\rceil$, fixes the class modulo $2^{K'}$. The two variants merge before the end of the block with equal numbers of odd steps, and $\tau=(g_k\pi_k)\,d\,(g_k\pi_k)^{-1}\notin H$, so $R$ grows as in Theorem 2.

The closing needs no $m_0$ search and no length filler. Let $q'$ of length $\ell'$ be the common prefix after the last site, with $C'$ and $\rho'=3^{o'}s^{\ell'}$. Take $m_0\equiv C'\pmod{\rho'}$ in the basin of $1$ (Theorem 2), let $P_2$ be the order of $2$ modulo $\rho'$, $y_i=2^{iP_2}m_0$ and $Z_i=(y_i-C')/\rho'$. Then $T^{a\ell'}(q'+r^{\ell'}Z_i)=y_i$ for all variants. Since $\log_r2$ is irrational, the fractional parts of $(\log_rZ_i-\Delta)/P$ are equidistributed, with $\Delta=|X|-\ell'+|Wz|$. So some $Z_i$ has top word $zt$ above $\lambda\equiv\Delta\pmod P$ digits, and the values $[X'z]$ of the variants fill $G(|X|)$. For $e=-1$ the changes are those of Theorem 2. $\square$

The steps of the proofs of Theorems 3 and 4 were checked by computer. `b3_e2e.py` and `bodd_e2e.py` (bases $3$, $5$ and $7$) fix $n$ modulo $2^{M_0}$, place sites with nested merges, close with the top word at a length in the right class, and check that the variants coincide at the final time, reach $1$, and hit the target state. `bgen_e2e.py` (bases $6$, $10$ and $12$) places $\Pi$ as above and asserts each congruence, the merge, the formula for $\tau$ and the growth of $R$ at every site. With the catalogue restricted to involutions it needs several slots. Multiplied by a counter of the length modulo $2$, it has $N$ of index $2$, and the target is hit for one parity of $\lambda$ only. These tests close directly with the top word instead of through $y_i$, whose exponent is too large to build. In all, $65$ runs in base $3$, $14$ in bases $5$ and $7$ and $47$ in bases $6$, $10$ and $12$ passed, and none failed; $24$ runs stopped at a time limit of $300$ or $400$ seconds ($16$ in bases $5$ and $7$, $5$ in base $10$, $3$ in base $6$).

## 5. Proof of Theorem A

**Remark 5 (one occurrence suffices).** In Theorems 2, 3 and 4 it is enough that $D$ accepts an integer whose expansion contains $z$ once. Write that expansion as $Wzt$. The permutations $[uz]$ form the finite group $\Gamma$, so some word $X$ has $[Xz]=1$. The state reached on $Wz$ lies in $I_0$, so $WzXzt$ ends in the same state and is accepted, and its leading digit is unchanged. (For empty $z$ this is the reduction at the start of the proof of Theorem 2.) So every automaton $D$ either accepts an element of the basin of $1$ or accepts no expansion that contains a word of minimal rank. In the second case all accepted expansions avoid a fixed word.

*Proof of Theorem A.* Theorems 2, 3 and 4 with Remark 5 give the first sentence: if $D$ accepts an expansion containing a word $z$ of minimal rank, it accepts an element of the basin of $1$. (a) A set of positive upper density in base $r$ cannot avoid a fixed word, because at most $(r^\ell-1)^{\lfloor m/\ell\rfloor}r^{\ell}$ expansions of length $m$ avoid a word of length $\ell$, so at most $n(r^\ell-1)^{\lfloor n/\ell\rfloor}r^{\ell}=o(r^n)$ integers below $r^n$ do. So it contains an expansion with $z$ and meets the basin of $1$. For $r=2^a$ this is [1, Corollary 21], since a set is $2^a$-automatic exactly when it is $2$-automatic [4, Ch. 6]. (b) Let $r=2^a3^b$ and let $S$ be nonempty, $r$-automatic and forward invariant, with no periodic point. Then $S$ has positive lower density by [1, Proposition 14] and its addendum, so by (a) it meets the basin of $1$, and forward invariance puts $1$ into $S$. But $1$ is periodic. (c) If $S$ is nonempty, forward invariant and without periodic points, it does not meet the basin of $1$, since otherwise forward invariance would put $1$ into it. By Remark 5 its elements avoid a fixed nonempty word $z$, of length $\ell$ say, and splitting expansions into blocks of length $\ell$ gives at most $C N^{\alpha_0}\log N$ elements below $N$, with $\alpha_0=\log_r(r^\ell-1)/\ell<1$, hence at most $CN^\alpha$ for any $\alpha\in(\alpha_0,1)$. The statement for $\mathrm{Anc}(y)$ is proved at the end of this section. $\square$

In bases other than $2^a3^b$, (b) would need positive density of such a set, and we do not know whether it holds. By Remark 5 a weaker property would also do: if, in base $r$, the expansions of every orbit that is not eventually periodic contain every word as a factor, then every nonempty $r$-automatic forward-invariant set contains a periodic point. [1, Proposition 14] and its addendum give this property in bases $2^a3^b$, through the leading words: the leading words that contain a given word cover all of $[0,1)$ except a null set, in the coordinate $\log_r$, so a limit set of positive measure meets the interior of one of their intervals. In base $r$ an even step changes $\log_rx$ by $-\log_r2$ and an odd step by about $\log_r(3/2)$. When $r$ is not of the form $2^a3^b$, the numbers $1$, $\log_r2$ and $\log_r3$ are linearly independent over $\mathbb Q$, so the leading digits of an orbit follow a walk with two independent steps. Such a walk can keep its fractional parts in a set of small measure, so [1, Proposition 14] has no analogue. By Proposition C below it cannot keep them in a countable closed set, so a forward-invariant automatic set without periodic points also has at least $N^c$ elements below $N$ for some $c>0$ and infinitely many $N$. Lagarias and Soundararajan [6] showed that for most starting values the first $N$ iterates approximately satisfy Benford's law in every base, and Kontorovich and Miller [9, Theorem 5.3] had proved, for the map between consecutive odd iterates and for bases $B$ with $\log_B2$ irrational of finite type, that the leading digits of $x_m/((3/4)^mx_0)$ become Benford as $m\to\infty$, after a limit over the starting value $x_0$; neither result says anything about a single orbit. The question asks whether such a walk can stay among the leading words that avoid a fixed word. It resembles Furstenberg's problems on sets invariant under multiplication by $p$ and by $q$.

The basin of $1$ enters the proofs of Theorems 2, 3 and 4 only through the closing, where an integer of the basin with a prescribed leading word, or in a prescribed residue class, is reached through powers of $2$. Replacing $1$ by any $y$ prime to $3$ changes nothing. Inverse odd steps from $y$ exist because $3\nmid y$, and with large powers of $2$ they give elements of $\mathrm{Anc}(y)$ prime to $3$ with every leading binary word, as in the closing of Theorem 2. So, for $e=\pm1$, every $y$ prime to $3$ and every base $r\geq2$, every $r$-automatic set of positive upper density contains an integer whose orbit passes through $y$. For an arithmetic progression and $e=+1$ this is known: Monks, Monks, Monks and Monks [8, Theorem 4.1] prove that every positive $y$ prime to $3$ back traces into every arithmetic progression $a+d\mathbb N$, within a number of steps bounded in terms of $d$ alone, and they attribute the qualitative statement to Monks [5]. The extension to every automatic set of positive upper density, and to $e=-1$, is new. For $3\mid y$ the ancestors of $y$ are the $y2^k$, a set of density $0$.

## 6. The maps $5x\pm1$

Let $T_5(x)=x/2$ for even $x$ and $T_5(x)=(5x+e)/2$ for odd $x$, with $e=\pm1$.

**Theorem B.** Lemma 1 holds for $T_5$. Consequently, for $e=\pm1$, every nonempty $2$-automatic set $S$ with $T_5(S)\subseteq S$ contains a periodic point, and every $2$-automatic set of positive upper density meets the basin of $1$ under $T_5$.

*Certificate for Lemma 1.* Call a triple $(k,\rho,\delta)$, with $\rho,\delta$ residues modulo $2^k$, a move if $\rho$ and $\rho+\delta$ have the same number $o$ of odd terms among their first $k$ iterates and $5^o<2^k$. The parities of the first $k$ steps of an integer depend only on its class modulo $2^k$, and on a fixed parity vector $T_5^k$ is the affine map $u\mapsto(5^ou+c)/2^k$. So for every $u\equiv\rho$ and every $d\equiv\delta\pmod{2^k}$, the integers $u$ and $u+d$ have the same odd count $o$ and
$$T_5^k(u+d)-T_5^k(u)=d',\qquad d'=\frac{5^od+\Delta}{2^k},$$
where $\Delta$ depends only on $(k,\rho,\delta)$. Hence $|d'|<|d|$ whenever $|d|>|\Delta|/(2^k-5^o)$. A search (`moves2.py`) finds, for each residue of $d$ modulo $2^8$ and each parity of $\rho$, a move with $k\leq8$, and the largest threshold $|\Delta|/(2^k-5^o)$ over the table is exactly $206/131\approx1.573$ for both signs of $e$ (attained at $k=8$, $o=3$, $\Delta=206$). So every gap with $|d|\geq2$ shrinks. Explicit classes for the gaps $\pm1$ exist modulo $2^{11}$ or $2^{13}$, for both initial parities (`gap1.py`). Composing moves gives Lemma 1: after a move, $T_5^k(u+2^kt)=T_5^k(u)+5^ot$ with $5^o$ odd, so a class of $u$ modulo $2^{k+k'}$ puts $T_5^k(u)$ in any prescribed class modulo $2^{k'}$, and the odd counts add. The parity of the first class is prescribed and the later ones are free. The tables are in the supplement, and `lemma_m5.py` checks the composite classes on members of each class. $\square$

*The rest of the proof.* Theorem 2 uses the multiplier $3$ in two more places: the forced digits and the closing need $2$ to be a primitive root modulo every power of $3$, and $\log_23$ to be irrational. Both hold for $5$, since $2$ is a primitive root modulo $25$, hence modulo every power of $5$, and $\log_25$ is irrational. With $3$ replaced by $5$ throughout, and the filler $2\cdot3^b$ of the closing replaced by $4\cdot5^b$ (the order of $2$ modulo $5^b$ is $4\cdot5^{b-1}$), the proofs of Theorem 2 and [1, Corollary 21] go through. Under $T_5$ the integer reached by the closing lies in the basin of the cycle through $1$, which is $\{1,3,8,4,2\}$ for $e=+1$; this is what "basin of $1$" means in Theorem B. $\square$

An end-to-end test of the construction for $T_5$ (`dfa_e2e5.py`) passed on $60$ of $60$ random automata. In this test the final check is that the variants merge with equal odd counts and reach the target state. It does not check that they reach $1$, because orbits of $5x+1$ need not.

*What Theorem B shows.* Most orbits of $5x+1$ are expected to diverge: the average logarithmic drift per step is $\frac12\log\frac12+\frac12\log\frac52=\frac12\log\frac54>0$. Theorem B shows that no $2$-automatic forward-invariant set certifies this. So the obstruction of [1] and of Theorem A is a property of affine maps of $\mathbb Z_2$ read by finite automata. It cannot tell a map whose orbits are expected to converge from one whose orbits are expected to diverge, and it gives no evidence for the Collatz conjecture. The same holds for Proposition C, whose proof uses only $x_j\to\infty$ and the equation $3^A=2^{A+B}r^N$ (Section 7).

## 7. Leading digits of single orbits

The gap between Theorem A(b) and A(c) is a question about the leading digits of one orbit. This section proves two results about them. Proposition C holds in every base that is not a power of $2$ and gives the lower bound $N^c$ in Theorem A(c). Proposition D is a finite-depth exclusion for single digits, certified by computer. Neither closes the gap, and both hold for $5x\pm1$ as well.

**Proposition C.** Let $e=\pm1$, let $r\geq2$ not be a power of $2$, and let $x_0,x_1,\dots$ be an orbit of $T$ that is not eventually periodic. Then the set $\Omega$ of accumulation points of $\log_rx_j\bmod1$ in $\mathbb R/\mathbb Z$ contains a nonempty perfect set; in particular it is uncountable. Consequently no such orbit lies eventually in a sparse $r$-automatic set, that is, one with $(\log N)^{O(1)}$ elements below $N$, and every nonempty $r$-automatic set $S$ with $T(S)\subseteq S$ and no periodic point has, for some $c>0$, at least $N^c$ elements below $N$ for infinitely many $N$.

*Proof.* The orbit visits each integer at most once, so $x_j\to\infty$. Put $\beta=\log_r2$, $\gamma=\log_r(3/2)$ and $\theta_j=\log_rx_j\bmod1$. An even step gives $\theta_{j+1}=\theta_j-\beta$, and an odd step gives $\theta_{j+1}=\theta_j+\gamma+\log_r(1+e/(3x_j))$, where the last term tends to $0$. Call a set $F\subseteq\mathbb R/\mathbb Z$ closed under successors if every $\theta\in F$ has $\theta-\beta\in F$ or $\theta+\gamma\in F$. The set $\Omega$ is closed, nonempty and closed under successors: if $\theta_{j_i}\to\theta$, pass to a subsequence on which the steps at the times $j_i$ have one type; then $\theta_{j_i+1}$ tends to $\theta-\beta$ or to $\theta+\gamma$. If $F$ is closed and closed under successors, so is its derived set $F'$: if $\theta_i\to\theta$ in $F$ with $\theta_i\neq\theta$, choose for each $i$ a successor of $\theta_i$ in $F$; along a subsequence these have one type, say $\theta_i-\beta$, so they are distinct and tend to $\theta-\beta$, which then lies in $F$ (as $F$ is closed) and in $F'$. The intersection of a decreasing chain of nonempty closed sets that are closed under successors is nonempty by compactness, and it is closed under successors: if $\theta-\beta$ is missing from some member of the chain, then $\theta+\gamma$ lies in every smaller member, hence in every member. By Zorn's lemma $\Omega$ contains a minimal nonempty closed set $F$ that is closed under successors, and then $F'=F$ or $F'=\emptyset$. In the first case $F$ is perfect. In the second $F$ is finite, so a path of successors in $F$ returns to its starting point after $A$ steps $+\gamma$ and $B$ steps $-\beta$ with $A+B>0$. Then $A\gamma-B\beta$ is an integer $N$, that is, $3^A=2^{A+B}r^N$. Let $p$ be an odd prime factor of $r$. Comparing powers of $p$ gives $A\,v_p(3)=N\,v_p(r)$, so $N\geq0$, and comparing powers of $2$ gives $0=A+B+N\,v_2(r)\geq A+B$, a contradiction. So $F$ is perfect.

A regular language with polynomially many words of each length is a finite union of languages $w_0v_1^*w_1\cdots v_m^*w_m$, and a regular language without this property has at least $h^n$ words of length $n$ for some $h>1$ and infinitely many $n$ [7]. Read expansions most significant digit first. For one such language, the numbers $x/r^{|x|}$, where $|x|$ is the number of digits, are the base-$r$ fractions $0.w_0v_1^{n_1}w_1\cdots v_m^{n_m}w_m$. In a convergent sequence of them, pass to a subsequence on which each $n_i$ is constant or tends to infinity. If $i$ is the first index that tends to infinity, the limit is $0.w_0v_1^{n_1}\cdots w_{i-1}v_iv_iv_i\cdots$; if there is none, the sequence is eventually constant. So these numbers have a countable closure in $[1/r,1]$, and so do the numbers $\log_rx\bmod1=\log_r(x/r^{|x|})\bmod1$. If the orbit lies eventually in the set, $\Omega$ lies in this closure, which is impossible. If $S$ is forward-invariant without periodic points, the orbit of any element of $S$ stays in $S$ and is not eventually periodic, so $S$ is not sparse. $\square$

In bases $2^a3^b$, Theorem A(b) gives more. In bases $2^a$ the proof fails, because $a$ even steps return $\theta$ to itself. The proof uses only that $x_j\to\infty$ and the equation $3^A=2^{A+B}r^N$, and the same argument works for $(mx\pm1)/2$ with any odd $m$, so like [1, Corollary 21] it does not distinguish $3x+1$ from $5x+1$.

**Proposition D.** Let $e=\pm1$ and let $x_0,x_1,\dots$ be an orbit of $T$ that is not eventually periodic. For every decimal digit $z$, infinitely many $x_j$ have $z$ among their first $L(z)$ decimal digits, where $L(0),\dots,L(9)$ are $6,2,3,4,3,4,5,5,4,6$. The same holds in base $5$ with $L(0),\dots,L(4)$ equal to $4,1,2,3,4$, and in base $7$ with $L(0),\dots,L(6)$ equal to $4,2,3,3,3,4,5$.

*Proof.* Let $C$ be the closure of the set of $\theta\in\mathbb R/\mathbb Z$ such that the first $L$ base-$r$ digits of $r^{\theta}$ avoid $z$. It is a finite union of closed arcs. Put $F_0=C$ and $F_{n+1}=F_n\cap\big((F_n+\beta)\cup(F_n-\gamma)\big)$. Every closed subset of $C$ that is closed under successors lies in every $F_n$. For each pair $(z,L)$ in the statement, a computation finds an $n$ with $F_n=\emptyset$. By the proof of Proposition C the set $\Omega$ is closed under successors, so $\Omega\not\subseteq C$. The complement of $C$ is open, and the first $L$ digits of $r^\theta$ contain $z$ for every $\theta$ in it, so infinitely many $\theta_j$ lie in it. $\square$

The computation (`sieve.py`) widens $C$ and every shifted copy of $F_n$ by $10^{-12}$, so each computed set contains the exact one. An independent computation in exact rational arithmetic in the coordinate $r^\theta$ (`sieve_exact.py`) gives the same index for every entry of the table, for example $n=67$ for $z=1$, $L=2$ in base $10$, and it confirms the empty iterations for $\gamma=\log_r(5/2)$ described next. The widening also gives a finite version. An odd step at $x>10^{12}$ changes $\theta$ by $\gamma$ up to less than $1/(3x\ln10)<1.5\cdot10^{-13}$, and an even step changes it by exactly $-\log_{10}2$. In `sieve.py` every interval is widened by $10^{-12}$ on each side after every shift, and the double-precision rounding of the shift and of the endpoints is below $10^{-15}$ per step. So at each step the widening covers both the deviation of the orbit and the rounding, each computed set contains the corresponding set for the orbit, and among any $68$ consecutive terms above $10^{12}$ of any orbit, one has a digit $1$ among its first two decimal digits. Proposition D itself rests on the exact computation. On $3000$ orbits starting near $10^{40}$, the longest run without one has $25$ terms. For $L=1$ the iteration stops at a nonempty set for every digit. For example, $[\log_{10}2,1)$ is closed under successors, so as far as this argument can see, the first digit alone can avoid $1$.

With $\gamma=\log_r(5/2)$, the step of $5x\pm1$, the iteration also becomes empty for every digit in these bases. In base $5$ this is trivial: there $\gamma\equiv-\beta\pmod1$, so both steps of the sieve shift by $-\beta$, and it is the sieve of an irrational rotation, which empties for every $C\neq\mathbb R/\mathbb Z$. So Proposition D, like Theorem B, holds for a map whose orbits are expected to diverge. It is a statement about walks with steps $x/2$ and $(mx\pm1)/2$, and it says nothing about convergence.

In base $5$ the iteration also becomes empty for the word $10$ at $L=8$ ($n=1368$, in floating point with the widening and in exact arithmetic), so infinitely many terms of every orbit that is not eventually periodic have $10$ among their first $8$ base-$5$ digits. For the other words of length $2$ in base $5$, every run with $L\le9$ stops at a nonempty set, except five runs at $L=9$ that reach the time limit of $600$ seconds while still nonempty; the data are in Appendix A. If some depth made the iteration empty for every word $z$, then by Remark 5 every nonempty $r$-automatic set $S$ with $T(S)\subseteq S$ would contain a periodic point. The same iteration on the last digits does not become empty for a word that the last digits of some cycle avoid. In base $5$ the last $k$ digits are $x\bmod5^k$, both steps act on these residues as bijections, and the residues of the cycle $\{1,2\}$ of $3x-1$ avoid many words. The leading digits are not affected by cycles, because the argument uses $x_j\to\infty$.

## 8. Limitations

All results concern sets recognised by finite automata, or leading digits of single orbits. None of them proves that any orbit reaches $1$.

1. Theorem B shows that the method of Theorems 2, 3 and 4 applies unchanged to $5x\pm1$, whose orbits are expected to diverge. Propositions C and D hold for $5x\pm1$ as well. So none of these results uses the property that distinguishes $3x+1$, the negative drift $\log(3/4)<0$.
2. Theorem A(b) is proved only in bases $2^a3^b$. In other bases, Theorem A(c) and Proposition C bound a counterexample set between $N^c$ and $N^\alpha$ elements below $N$, with $c>0$ and $\alpha<1$, and we do not know whether it can exist.
3. The computer tests of Theorems 3, 4 and B close directly with the top word instead of through the large powers of $2$ that the proofs use. The tests of Theorem B check that variants merge with equal odd counts, not that they reach $1$.
4. Lemma 1 for $5x\pm1$ and Proposition D are computer-assisted. The certificates are finite tables and exact rational computations, included in the supplement.
5. Theorems 3 and 4 (called Theorems B and M on rStation post 53 [2]), Theorem B (Remark 26 there) and Propositions C and D (Propositions 27 and 28 there) were posted for audit. At submission no second reader had checked them. The audit in [2] covers Lemma 1 and Theorem 2 of [1].

## Appendix A. Leading-word data

The supplement contains the full output of the sieve for words of length $2$ in base $5$ (`ld_batch2.txt`, `ld_batch_exact5.txt`) and the removal shares computed from it (`cmeas.py`). After $k$ steps with $a$ odd steps, $\theta_k=\theta_0+a\log_r3-k\beta$, so the iteration asks for an infinite oriented path on the lattice of pairs $(k,a)$, with steps $(1,0)$ and $(1,1)$, through the sites with $\theta_0+a\log_r3-k\beta\in C$. This is oriented site percolation in a quasi-periodic environment. In the computations the iteration becomes empty once $|C|$ falls to between $0.47$ and $0.73$. For independent sites the critical density is about $0.7055$. For words of length $2$ the avoiding sets shrink like $(1-r^{-2})^L$, and enumerating $r^L$ cells rarely reaches a depth at which the iteration becomes empty. Apart from $z=10$ (Section 7), every run with $L\le9$ stops at a nonempty set, except the five runs at $L=9$ for $z=12,13,21,22,23$, which are still nonempty at the time limit; for $z=01$, for instance, the iteration stops at a set of measure about $0.54$. For each word of length $2$ in base $5$, the share of $C$ that the iteration removes, $1-|F_\infty|/|C|$, grows by a factor between about $1.5$ and $3.0$ with each extra digit once it exceeds $0.01$, and between $1.5$ and $2.3$ once it exceeds $0.05$, up to the last depth computed. For $z=10$ it was $0.47$ at $L=7$, one digit before the iteration became empty, and for $z=20$ it is $0.23$ at $L=8$ and $0.52$ at $L=9$. This suggests that every word gives an empty iteration at some depth, but we have no proof.
