# Accept

This is a sound, clearly presented partial contribution to the Collatz problem. It bounds the logarithmic part of each odd state supplied by a moving, valuation-capped inventory of preceding states. The first-crossing application gives a substantive restriction on actual recovery trajectories, while the constructive sharpness theorem identifies a limitation of the unrestricted finite-path bound. The paper does not solve the conjecture and appropriately makes no claim to exclude a new class of cycles or establish precise-return existence.

The central inequalities are justified. Equation (11) expresses the inventory factor prime by prime as an LCM of pairwise gcds. The return numerator bounds those gcds, and summing over lags charges each edge at most $d$ times. Omitting the final division exponent from that numerator correctly reduces the excess-division multiplicity to $d-1$, yielding $D_L=L(L-1)/2$. The first-crossing argument supplies both the lower height bound throughout the selected window and the favorable endpoint sign in the cost telescope. Consequently, the exceptional count and its vanishing proportion follow with the stated restrictions on $L$. The precise-return corollary uses exactly the actual low-state conclusion supplied by [7, Theorem 1(b)]. The cyclic application also retains the necessary least-period and minimum-height qualifications.

Theorem 5 is the strongest additional ingredient beyond the inherited return inequality. The distinct rational ratios in (17) give nonzero fixed determinants, so all common prime factors of different numerators belong to a finite exceptional set. Restricting the exponent to an arithmetic progression fixes their valuations. Removing those factors leaves pairwise coprime moduli of the required logarithmic size. The dyadic congruence enforces the complete exact exponent word, and CRT realizes all the divisibility requirements on positive integer trajectories. The refined upper bound in (7), together with (20), establishes the claimed limit. This proves sharpness of the excess-division coefficient, not of $B_L$ itself or of the recovery-window lookback threshold; the manuscript explicitly preserves these distinctions.

The support-sensitive estimate is also valid. At the $j$th largest reused valuation, the earliest selected destination supplies an additional earlier occurrence, producing $j$ disjoint return intervals. Adjacent-state coprimality bounds the number of positive entries, and the specified block partition gives edge overlap at most two. The improvement remains conditional on the reused prime support; no unsupported general bound on that support is asserted.

The novelty claim is appropriately narrower than the underlying arithmetic machinery. I compared the relevant statements and arguments in [2]–[6], the recovery input in [7], and Yang's external preprint [1], including its postselected-group and prime-pool results. Those results do not give the present moving, valuation-capped inventory estimate. The LCM argument for repeated words in [5] and the classical binary-recurrence setting of [9] likewise concern a fixed recurrence structure. The elementary aggregation in Theorem 2 is a close extension of [2], rather than a new prime-power return principle. In combination with the recovery consequence, sharp integer realization, and support-sensitive alternative, however, the paper supplies a coherent contribution rather than merely a change of terminology.

The analytic proofs provide the essential reproducible evidence. As a limited independent check, I tested both finite inequalities on 1,999 odd starting values below 4,000, retaining at most 35 distinct states and lookbacks up to eight. I also constructed six CRT witnesses at $L=2,3$, checked their exact division words and shared factors, and evaluated their reuse-to-cost ratios. The largest tested ratios were approximately $0.992748$ and $2.998108$, against limiting values $1$ and $3$. These checks support finite arithmetic interfaces; they do not establish the asymptotic theorem or optimality for recovery windows. Acceptance rests on the proofs.

Clarity is sufficient independently of correctness: the question, statistic, proof strategy, principal evidence, and limitations are identifiable in the main text. The abstract is informative. Two minor improvements would help: cite [8, Section 5] alongside the discussion of inherited pairwise-gcd product bounds, and add a small worked inventory example showing why a higher valuation of an already-seen prime contributes to $I_{t,L}$. Neither change is necessary to repair the argument.
