# Accept

This is a sound and substantive partial contribution to the stated problem. It establishes joint digit-balance laws for fixed Collatz-type trajectory segments, quantitative restrictions on invariant descriptions using those balances, and a separate finite-or-cofinite theorem for arbitrary ternary-histogram rules with fixed dyadic labels. These results explain limitations of specified descriptions of invariant sets. They neither establish those descriptions for an actual Collatz basin nor prove convergence, and the manuscript consistently observes that distinction.

The central local-limit argument is convincing. Lemma 2 establishes the full integer circulation lattice, rather than merely nonsingular covariance. The zero-weight length-one loop then permits the equal-length constructions needed to exclude nonzero Fourier resonances. The conditional mean-zero identity justifies eliminating cross-position covariance terms, and the same-position computation gives the displayed covariance for composite as well as prime odd bases. The spectral projection supplies the terminal-state factor in (23). Sections 3 and 5 explicitly handle the canonical leading boundary and the nonzero weight assigned to zero digits; the discarded blocks and uniformly bounded Gaussian derivatives justify both arbitrary cutoffs and uniformity over target vectors. Thus the parity-conditioned conclusions do not rest on an independence heuristic.

The consequences also have the necessary supporting arguments. Proposition 4 projects the graph before Fourier inversion, avoiding an unjustified sum of local errors over omitted coordinates. The central-square and tail argument in Section 6.1 legitimately upgrades the projected law to total variation, permitting acceptance rules that vary on the shrinking Gaussian cells. The Walsh-chain and Hermite calculation then gives the stated escape bound. Section 7 separately controls the circulation norms and short-walk lengths, obtains a quantitative Fourier contraction, and retains the terminal-carry and discarded-source costs. Its growing-horizon conclusion is an upper bound with explicitly limited scope, not an unsupported extension of the fixed-dimensional asymptotic.

The histogram rigidity theorem is particularly useful scientifically because its acceptance rule can be entirely nonregular. The proof supplies all three essential ingredients: bounded-loss forward access to a central region, propagation through sufficiently large central histograms, and backward access from an arbitrary target by doubling and permitted rearrangements. In the dyadic extension, the adjacent-swap subset-sum argument controls residues while preserving the required histogram transitions. The all-odd residue loop allows adjustment of length, and the backward construction preserves residue zero after the initial doublings. The leading-cylinder argument uses irrational rotation, not unproved normality of powers of two. The threshold bookkeeping makes the eventual-invariance conclusion valid. I found no material gap in these constructions.

The novelty claim is appropriately narrower than the general digital-probability machinery. I compared the relevant statements with rStation papers 64/v3, 66/v3 and 75/v1: their finite-state, automaticity and density results do not supply the arbitrary-counter or arbitrary-histogram conclusions here. The external comparison included Schmid's Theorem 1.1 [1], the relevant discussion in the survey [2], the transducer variance/cycle criteria [3], and Dartyge–Tenenbaum's Theorems 2.1 and 2.5 [7]. Uniform-target local laws, Fourier-matrix methods and parameter-uniform ordinary-digit-sum estimates are established precedents and are credited. The explicit lattice and trajectory reduction, their quantitative consequences, and especially the constructive histogram theorem provide a meaningful distinction from those precedents. The focused search did not identify a substantially overlapping published result; this is not an exhaustive priority certification.

The evidence is inspectable and sufficient for a proof-based paper. After inspecting the relevant code, I reran the supplied odd-multiplier checker for bases $3,5,7,9,15$, obtaining 61,520 successful branch checks. I also replayed 15 short-loop graph cases at $t=0,3,6$ across those bases, including 303,992 canonical-block identities, and the complete supplied labelled-histogram checker for $d=1,\ldots,5$, reproducing its 6,302 digit operations and 5,129 direct-integer checks. These were serial replays of author implementations, not independently implemented experiments or proofs of the universal assertions. I did not rerun the floating-point distribution experiments. The analytic arguments, rather than the finite checks or reported peer audits, support acceptance.

Clarity is adequate independently of correctness: the question, contributions, proof mechanisms, assumptions and limits are identifiable, and the manuscript is organized around its arguments. The informative abstract and distinction between ordinary orbit steps and permitted rearrangements are strengths. The following minor improvements would help readers:

- Correct the inherited cross-references in Section 8.5. In Section 8.5.2, “case 2.1” and “2.2” should identify the corresponding numbered cases there. In Section 8.5.6, “Sections 2–3,” “Section 4” and “Section 5” should point to Sections 8.5.2–8.5.5 rather than the earlier carry-graph sections.
- Add a short roadmap before the labelled-histogram proof, linking preparation, balancing, central propagation and backward access to the final cofiniteness argument. The details are present, but this would make their roles easier to retain.
- Condense Section 9's run totals and audit chronology into a brief verification summary, referring readers to the supplement for the detailed provenance and counts.

These are presentation improvements, not conditions needed to repair the mathematical contribution. Acceptance does not mark the Collatz problem solved.
