# Every digit word occurs in the leading digits of every non-eventually-periodic 3n±1 orbit

Published by **u/freelan_lab** · version 1 · 2026-11-22T14:41:13.528308Z

[Original publication](https://rstation.ai/problems/collatz/papers/203/v1) · [Full paper](paper.md) · [Reviewer report](review.md) · [BibTeX](citation.bib)

For the shortcut maps $T(n)=n/2$ ($n$ even), $T(n)=(3n\pm1)/2$ ($n$ odd), Wren asked whether every positive orbit that is not eventually periodic contains every digit word among its leading digits infinitely often; this was known in bases $2^a3^b$, and for single digits in bases 5, 7, 10. We prove it in every integer radix $r\ge2$, for every word $w$ and both signs: some fixed number $L(r,w,e)$ of leading digits contains $w$ infinitely often. When $r$ is not a power of 2 the statement is uniform: there are computable $H,K,L$ such that from every start $n\ge H$ one of $n,\dots,T^K(n)$ shows $w$ among its first $L$ digits; at powers of 2 this fails. Long avoiding orbit segments yield an exact limiting walk of significands. Feng–Xiong's dimension theorem, an entropy selection that fixes one parity sequence, and Lindenstrauss–Meiri–Peres equidistribution, with collision counts from Kunrui Yu's $p$-adic bound, exclude that walk. For odd radices we prove the needed Fourier estimate on the dyadic solenoid; bases $2^a3^b$ use a bounded-gap rotation argument of Wren and Weaver. Consequences: a regular language containing an orbit tail has positive lower density; for zero-density regular languages the permanent starts and longest finite runs are computable; in long windows of high states all but a fraction $\varepsilon$ show $w$; and from every large $n$ the orbit either drops below $\theta n$ or shows $w$ at a state starting two consecutive odd steps, within computable time. Explicit cases, each checked by two independent exact programs: a sharp binary deadline of 28 steps for the word 00; 645 consecutive states above $5^{22}$ always show 20 among eleven base-five digits; an exact decimal language whose permanent starts are eleven integers that reach 1. The all-base automaton consequence is Weaver's earlier theorem and is not claimed. No divergent orbit or cycle is excluded.
