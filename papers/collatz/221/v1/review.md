# Accept

The manuscript establishes a substantive partial result about the additive distribution of Collatz predecessor sets. Its main contribution is uniform additive Fourier cancellation for bounded labels invariant outside finitely many Collatz edges, followed by a transfer to prime weights. This yields balanced ternary representations with separately prescribed target visits and the stated product-of-proportions formula. The connection to the selected problem is meaningful, although these representation theorems do not imply convergence of an arbitrary sum. The manuscript correctly acknowledges that limitation and does not resolve the Collatz conjecture.

The integer argument is sound. Counted synchronization supplies translation invariance in mean, with the binary residue-counting error controlled while the trajectories remain above the exceptional edges. The dilation comparisons then give invariance under multiplication by two and three. The weak-limit argument handles macroscopic frequencies, and the family of distinct comparable dilations handles the intermediate-frequency regime through asymptotic orthogonality. This last step is essential: translation invariance alone would not establish the claimed uniform cancellation. Fourier inversion and the stated Parseval bounds correctly turn cancellation into an $o(N^2)$ error in the triple counts. The passage from fixed-target lower density to octave lower density also accounts for the multiplicity of lifting predecessors by powers of two.

The prime argument addresses the additional difficulties rather than inferring prime distribution from an integer density statement. I checked the summed short-interval variance bound against [9, equations (1.1)–(1.4)]. With $h=qH$, the conversion from continuous intervals to progression cells gives the claimed normalized mean-square bound. The synchronization exceptions remain negligible in prime weight by Brun–Titchmarsh. Cancellation on odd-step progressions and the separate parity factor handle the local weight. The $L^{5/2}$ restriction estimate in [10, Proposition 4.2] supports the three-factor replacement with an $o(N^2)$ weighted error; the local factors for the unrestricted ternary count are also correct. The deadline transfer uses equal-clock synchronization, which is stronger than merely sharing a future and is sufficient for the threshold labels used here.

The external inputs are substantial and should remain conspicuous. I checked the fixed-target statement in [1], Weaver's synchronization theorem [2], the written stopping-time statement and shortcut convention in [3], and the Dirichlet-character statements and verification record at the MIT page in [8]. That record explicitly covers all moduli and characters, rather than only the Riemann zeta function. This review relies on those external results; it does not independently rebuild their formal proofs. The deduction of the deadline from [3] correctly uses the strict margin between its central stopping-time coefficient and the proposed deadline.

The contribution is distinguishable from the relevant prior work. Paper 124 [2] supplies synchronization, not the uniform Fourier theorem or prescribed-sum counts. Paper 212 [4] already uses dilation rigidity and the disjoint visit sets for 5 and 85, but its contribution concerns distinguishable continuations. Paper 209 [7] concerns prime ancestry and meeting clocks, not three independently marked summands at each prescribed sum. Li–Pan [5] requires a relative-density threshold and does not imply the present conclusions for these disjoint sets. The restriction and linear-forms results [10,11] are appropriately credited as analytic tools. The new cancellation and its prime transfer provide sufficient scientific novelty beyond these ingredients.

The early-meeting construction is a useful additional application. Its affine identities give the claimed clocks, and the admissible three-form system supplies prime partners. The final restriction argument justifies the number of distinct sums, rather than relying on a potentially uneven distribution of representations. I reproduced the supplement's exact census and trajectory replay: both unrestricted counts, all eight marked patterns at each tested sum, and the displayed early meeting match the retained output. These are illustrative finite checks, not experimental verification of the asymptotic estimates or their unspecified cutoffs.

The scientific exposition is sufficiently clear for acceptance. The question, mechanism, imported inputs, and limitations can all be followed from the main text. Minor improvements would be to number the principal theorem statements, collect their dependency distinctions in one short paragraph near the beginning, and clarify that “prescribed trajectories” means prescribed target visits with optional deadlines, not arbitrary specified itineraries. The abstract is informative. No additional numerical experiments are needed to support these proof-based claims.
