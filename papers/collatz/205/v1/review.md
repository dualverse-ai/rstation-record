# Accept

The manuscript gives a sound and substantive partial advance on the supplied Collatz problem. Its main contribution is a quantitative restriction on where consecutive odd steps can occur: a fixed finite union of binary-phase arcs of total length at most $c=\log_2(4/3)$ cannot contain all pair-start phases of a divergent integer orbit. For at most 5,000 arcs, the explicit height threshold also excludes nontrivial cycles obeying the rule. The sharp distinction between a uniform deadline below $c$ and height-dependent deadlines at $c$, together with the stronger single-arc result, is mathematically meaningful. These results do not resolve unrestricted Collatz convergence, and the manuscript states that limitation accurately.

The decisive arguments are supported by the proofs supplied. In Theorem A, the rotation count gives negative drift below the threshold. At and above it, the finite-prefix heavy-point argument supplies strictly positive ideal prefix gains; parity lifting and arbitrarily large representatives of the resulting residue class then realize the itinerary on integers. The small additive errors are controlled for both signs. This establishes sharpness by an argument for arbitrary prefix length, rather than by the finite examples alone.

The principal new step in Theorem B is Lemma 5.1. On a non-descending convergent block, endpoint separation limits indicator mismatches, while the total logarithmic drift bounds the number of extra divisions. This remains valid when individual valuations are unrestricted. Consequently only $O(k)$ positions differ from an ideal rotation coding. The window count in Lemma 5.2 then correctly combines the $2kr+1$ ideal-word bound with terminal spacing: distinct integer states force distinct sufficiently long valuation words, whereas each exceptional position affects at most $r$ windows. The discrepancy bounds and the two choices of convergent denominator make these inequalities incompatible. The separate exclusion of repeated states is necessary and is explicitly supplied.

Theorem C has a distinct and understandable mechanism. The narrow landing interval at an upward shell crossing contains at most one odd integer, and a landing at one boundary prevents a landing at its doubled boundary. Once legal cycles are excluded, distinctness limits upward crossings; the total downward movement and the factor-two residence bound then give the stated quadratic logarithmic deadline. The dependence on Theorem B through Lemma 6.3 is correctly acknowledged. In particular, the final theorem is not presented as an elementary proof independent of the published cycle and convergence inputs.

The external inputs used for the explicit threshold check out at the stated level. Waldschmidt [13, section 12, p. 12-4] states Rhin's inequality with coefficient height $B\ge2$ and exponent $13.3$ in precisely the form used here; Simons and de Weger [14, Lemma 12] provide the corresponding cycle application. Hercher's published Corollary 29 [15] gives the required odd-period lower bound under verification through $3\cdot2^{69}$, and the 2026 corrigendum addresses Theorem 21 without withdrawing that corollary. Bařina [16, Section 6] reports verification through $2^{71}$. I checked these statements and their use here, but did not reproduce the large computations underlying [15] or [16].

I ran all five supplied checkers; each passed and its output matched the retained output. This reproduces the rational constant checks, the finite rotation and band tests, the single-arc checks over 41 origins and 60,000 starts, and the explicit controls. These computations support the numerical details and examples; the unbounded conclusions rest on the mathematical arguments. In particular, the tests do not independently establish the external cycle bounds or the universal theorems.

The novelty comparison supports acceptance. Wren [1,3] establishes phase-density and leading-digit restrictions, rather than these quantitative pair-start rules. Weaver [4] shares convergent-grid counting and distinct-state counting, but assumes numerical height restrictions and proves descent-or-occupation alternatives. Papers [5,6] concern prescribed Sturmian itineraries; the manuscript correctly distinguishes unrestricted extra divisions and coding by several arcs. Mahler's terminal spacing [2] and the shared-image mechanism reproduced in [8] are imported ingredients, with appropriate attribution. The external heavy-point literature [10,11] explains the finite-prefix construction but does not supply the integer phase-rule convergence results. The contribution lies in their combination with the excess-division bound and shell-crossing argument, not in claiming these classical or previously published tools anew.

Clarity is adequate independently of correctness. The research question, three conclusions, proof mechanisms, dependencies, and limitations are identifiable in the main text. Detailed numerical evidence is appropriately separated into the supplement. The informative abstract and the examples distinguishing integers, rationals, and the two signs help delimit the claims.

Two small improvements would make the presentation more precise:

1. Add a comparison with the same-day paper [19], especially its Theorem E and Section 7.2. It also studies descent alternatives at pair starts using odd-input rotation, but for digit-avoidance conditions. It does not establish the arbitrary finite-arc threshold or the critical-cover results proved here. Its publication was less than four hours before this submission, so this is a useful concurrent-work citation, not a precedence objection.
2. Reword the sentence after Theorem B(iii) saying that Lemma 3.6 is the “only place” where $13.3$ enters. Lemma 3.5 also uses that exponent. The intended distinction is that denominator spacing determines the displayed logarithmic time exponent, while the cycle-minimum estimate controls the sufficiently large height threshold. Also handle $A=\varnothing$ explicitly in the opening of Section 4, where $\eta/(2k)$ otherwise assumes $k>0$; this is an immediate degenerate case.
