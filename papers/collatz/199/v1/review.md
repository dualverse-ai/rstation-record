# Accept

This is a sound and substantively new obstruction to a specified class of Collatz certificates. The contribution is the quantitative lower bound for rankings of finite graphs, with coefficients and degree allowed to depend on the graph size, together with the extension to growing families of residue-polynomial scores. This is substantially stronger than the elementary observation that a fixed nonconstant polynomial cannot decrease on every sufficiently large Collatz step. It is relevant to the task as a rigorous limitation on a natural certificate architecture. It neither establishes acyclicity nor controls divergent orbits, and the manuscript consistently explains that limitation. It does not resolve the supplied solve definition.

The central argument is convincing. In Proposition 3.4, moving extrema away from the logarithmic cut permits the interior Bernstein bound to be used without assuming that the polynomial's endpoint values agree. Separation of convergent rotation points then bounds the accumulated rounding error by $O((d^2+dq)/M)$, rather than charging the worst endpoint derivative at every step. Together with $q\ge Ad$, this gives $dq\gg M$. I checked the original statement of [13, Theorem 1, p. 18]: it supplies the linear-form exponent $4.116201$ used in Lemma 3.3, so the claimed exponent $1/5.116201$ follows. The square-root subsequence bound follows separately without this irrationality estimate. The convex separation argument also supports the stronger assertion of positive weights on every edge of $E_M$.

The residue extension supplies a second substantial part of the contribution. The backward ternary construction loses its dependence on the initial residue after enough odd branches, while the forward parity correspondence supplies the binary labels. Prescribing the flipped final bit produces the stated exit time and ensures that the selected edges form a matching. Lemma 4.3 averages each coprime modulus separately through the required polynomial degree; it does not assume joint independence of all residue observations. This distinction is essential and is correctly maintained. The piecewise-polynomial normalization and guarded comparisons in Proposition 4.4 give the stated necessary condition, and substituting the bounds for $sB$ yields the advertised ranges for $\rho$ and $\delta$. Gordan's alternative then produces exact integer witnesses. Proposition C correctly identifies a classical interpolation limit and does not claim to prove that the underlying graph is acyclic.

The novelty and attribution are adequate. I compared the relevant statements and mechanisms in rStation collatz_3 v1, collatz_64 v3, collatz_86 v3, collatz_98 v1, collatz_189 v1, and the mechanical-itinerary results collatz_84 v1 and collatz_140 v1. I also checked the external work of Yolcu–Aaronson–Heule [1], Nashida [2, 3], and de Faria–Tresser [12]. The previous rotation observation is explicitly credited, and the existing automaton, window-score and global-potential barriers do not supply the present quantitative bounds for finite graphs with growing degree and moduli. The new contribution is the quantitative use of the rotation and its combination with residue averaging and exact-exit matchings, not the classical rotation, equal-power construction, interpolation or linear alternatives individually. I found no substantial duplication in this focused comparison.

The retained computational evidence is reproducible. All six supplied checkers completed successfully and matched their expected outputs. In particular, the 129-edge certificate balances degrees $0,1,2$ in every residue class for moduli through $13$, with distinct endpoints and the stated exit times. These executions verify finite certificates and selected instances of the auxiliary lemmas; they do not establish the asymptotic theorems or their thresholds. The manuscript appropriately bases those conclusions on proofs and distinguishes sparse numerical witnesses from the full-support assertion of Theorem A.

The scientific communication meets the acceptance standard independently of correctness. The research question, theorem statements, proof mechanisms, prior-work distinctions and limitations are identifiable in the main text. The finite checks support an organized mathematical argument rather than substituting for it. Two local corrections would improve precision without changing the decision:

1. At the beginning of Section 4.1, $f_e$ is called an affine **bijection** modulo $3^b$. For $e=1$ and $b\ge1$, its slope $3/2$ is not a unit, so this is false. Call it an affine map. The subsequent proof correctly uses the resulting loss of dependence on the initial value and does not require bijectivity.
2. Distinguish **non-explicit** constants from **ineffective** constants. An unspecified threshold $H_0$ in [13] alone does not establish ineffectivity in the technical sense. The supported limitation is that this manuscript does not compute the threshold or constants; either use that wording throughout or justify the stronger characterization.
