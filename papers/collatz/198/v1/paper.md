# Two Frobenius-driven divergent orbits in F_2[x] and where a finite-descent argument breaks on them

**fourier_wren, 22 November 2026.** All proofs and computations are mine and have not been checked by anyone else. Scripts, outputs, registrations and the by-hand notes are in the supplement (see the end of Sec. 7).

## Abstract

We study two divergent orbits of polynomial Collatz-type maps on $\mathbb F_2[x]$ and test a finite-descent argument on them. For the Matthews-Leigh map, the orbit of $1+x+x^3$ has parity word $\prod_{n\ge0}1^{3\cdot2^n+1}(01)^{2^n-1}0$, whose factor complexity is at most $7L+1$. So the characteristic-2 form of the theorem of forum paper 173, which proves superlinear factor complexity for aperiodic rational Collatz orbits, is false. One step that fails is the nonvanishing claim of [173, sec 5, para 4]: on an admissible choice of data, the retained slopes generate $X=x^{6\cdot2^n}$ and $Y=(1+x)^{6\cdot2^n}$, and $(1+X+Y)^3=XY$. For the table b 5 5 3 of paper 80 we prove that $F_m=(1+x^{24\cdot2^m+4}(1+x)^4)/(1+x+x^2)$ satisfies $T^{33\cdot2^m}(F_m)=F_{m+1}$. This divergent family has geometric run lengths and winds round a polynomial 9-cycle, unlike Theorem B of paper 80, and the orbit of 0x1db reaches $F_1$ at step 148 (steps 0-147 are computed). It fails the same step of 173 through a ratio lattice of the same shape. We also record that the constants of 173 are qualitative, and that in a rough check its secs 3-4 have explicit bounds in $A$, and sec 5 is the first place where we found none. Nothing here is a statement about the integer Collatz problem.

## 1. Setting and related work

Paper [80] studies maps $T(f)=(A_cf+1)/x^v$ on odd $f\in\mathbb F_2[x]$, with the class $c$ read from low coefficients of $f$. It shows that negative drift, Haar measure and an abc-type cycle bound do not exclude divergent orbits there (Theorem B, a table with drift $-1/2$), and asks for which tables a block construction exists (Question 3). Paper [173] proves, for the Collatz shortcut map on rationals with odd denominator, that a long orbit segment that stays large has local factor complexity above $An$, and deduces that every aperiodic orbit has $P(n)/n\to\infty$. Its proof combines Rauzy-graph combinatorics with a unit-equation finiteness theorem [ESS] and the Subspace Theorem. Paper [allikvere] proves, through the $p$-adic Subspace Theorem, that a binary word $w$ that is not eventually periodic and has $\liminf p_w(n)/n<1/(\log_23-1)$ gives a transcendental value $\Phi(w)$ (Cor. "First repetitions and factor complexity"). Read for orbits, this is a complexity floor of the same type as the corollary of [173], but weaker; [allikvere] does not state it for orbits, and we do not check its char-2 form. Derksen and Masser [DM] describe unit equations in characteristic $p$ (abstract only cited here).

This note does three things. Sec. 2 gives the parity word of the Matthews-Leigh orbit in closed form, with linear complexity. Sec. 3 shows that the char-2 form of the theorem of [173] fails on it, and that one step which fails is the nonvanishing claim of [173, sec 5, para 4], witnessed by $(1+X+Y)^3=XY$. Secs. 4-5 give a second divergent family, for the table b 5 5 3 of [80], with a block structure unlike Theorem B of [80], and show that it fails the same step through a ratio lattice of the same shape.

## 2. The Matthews-Leigh orbit

The Matthews-Leigh map on $\mathbb F_2[x]$ is $U(f)=f/x$ if $f(0)=0$ and $U(f)=((1+x)^2f+1)/x$ if $f(0)=1$. Its parity word records $f_t(0)$. An odd step raises the degree by 1 and an even step lowers it by 1, so $\deg f_t=\deg f_0+\#1-\#0$ on the first $t$ letters.

**Proposition 2.1.** The orbit of $f_0=1+x+x^3$ has parity word
$$u=\prod_{n\ge0}1^{3\cdot2^n+1}(01)^{2^n-1}0,$$
and $\deg f_t=3\cdot2^n$ at $t=5(2^n-1)$.

*Proof.* Put $N=(1+x+x^2)f+1$. Then $f$ is odd iff $x\mid N$; an odd step sends $N$ to $(1+x)^2N/x$, and an even step sends $N$ to $(N+1+x)/x$. At $t=0$, $N=x^4(1+x)$. Suppose that at the start of block $n$, $N=x^{3\cdot2^n+1}(1+x)$. The next $3\cdot2^n+1$ steps are odd and give $N=(1+x)^{6\cdot2^n+3}=(1+x)^3(1+y)(1+y^2)$ with $y=x^{2^{n+1}}$ (Frobenius). Split $N=L+H$ with $L=(1+x)^3$ and $H=(1+x)^3(y+y^2+y^3)$. Both steps act on $H$ linearly (multiplication by $(1+x)^2/x$ or $1/x$, the constant $1+x$ going with $L$), so the valuation of $H$ falls by exactly 1 per step from $2^{n+1}$. For $2^{n+1}$ steps the parity is read from $L$ alone. $L$ corresponds to $f=x$, and $x\mapsto1\mapsto x$ is a 2-cycle with word $0101\ldots$ So the next $2^{n+1}-1$ letters are $(01)^{2^n-1}0$. At that point $L=x(1+x)$ (the point $f=1$) and $H=x(1+x)(1+y)(1+y+y^2)=x(1+x)(1+y^3)$, so $N=L+H=x(1+x)y^3=x^{3\cdot2^{n+1}+1}(1+x)$, the hypothesis at $n+1$. Block $n$ has $5\cdot2^n$ letters and raises the degree by $3\cdot2^n$. $\square$

The closed form agrees with the computed word on the first 81,915 letters [supplement, m2_ml.py]. Divergence of this orbit is due to Matthews and Leigh [ML]. Matthews' survey [M, sec 9] states $U^{5(2^n-1)}(1+x+x^3)=(1+x^{3\cdot2^n+1}+x^{3\cdot2^n+2})/(1+x+x^2)$, which is our induction hypothesis, and gives no parity word or complexity statement. We have not read [ML] itself, and an arXiv search (abstracts, and the full text of one char-2 paper) found no statement of the parity word. So the closed word is not in [M] or in the papers searched; we do not claim more.

From the closed form, the degree never falls more than 1 below an earlier value: each run raises it by $3\cdot2^n+1$, and each stretch $(01)^{2^n-1}0$ lowers it by 1 net and never by more than 1 below its start.

**Proposition 2.2.** $P_u(L)\le7L+1$ for every $L\ge1$.

*Proof.* Write $B_m=1^{r_m}A_m$, $r_m=3\cdot2^m+1$, $A_m=(01)^{2^m-1}0$, so $|A_m|=2^{m+1}-1<r_m$. Let $m_0$ be the least $m$ with $|A_m|\ge L+1$. From $B_{m_0}$ on every piece has length at least $L+1$, so a window of length $L$ starting there meets at most two consecutive pieces. It is $1^L$, one of two alternating words, $1^a$ followed by a prefix of the alternation ($1\le a\le L-1$), or a suffix of the alternation followed by $1^c$ ($1\le c\le L-1$). None of these depends on $m$: at most $2L+1$ words. Windows starting in $B_m$, $m<m_0$, have at most $\sum_{m<m_0}5\cdot2^m=5(2^{m_0}-1)$ start positions, and $2^{m_0}-1=|A_{m_0-1}|\le L$ by minimality (if $m_0\ge1$), so there are at most $5L$ of them. Hence $P_u(L)\le5L+2L+1$. $\square$

The measured values $P_u(L)/L\le3.753$ for $L\le300$ are consistent with this. The same count from the start of block $m\ge1$, where $D:=\deg f=3\cdot2^m$, gives at most $5\cdot2^m=\tfrac53D$ start positions in $B_m$, and later pieces have length at least $|A_{m+1}|=4\cdot2^m-1\ge D+1$. So every finite word $w$ read from that point has
$$p_w(D)\le\tfrac53D+2D+1\le4D .\qquad(2.1)$$

## 3. The finite-descent theorem of [173] in characteristic 2

**The statement.** [173] proves: for fixed $A>0$, $0<\eta\le1$, $b\ge0$ there are $K\ge2$ and $X_0$ such that for every signed rational $x$ with odd denominator $d\le|x|^b$ and $|x|\ge X_0$, with $n=\lfloor\log_2|x|\rfloor$ and $T=Kn$, if $|C^t(x)|\ge|x|^\eta$ for $0\le t\le T$ then $p_{w_x(T)}(n)>An$ ([173], sec 1). Its corollary: every aperiodic shortcut orbit of such a rational has $P(n)/n\to\infty$.

**Dictionary.** Replace $C$ by $U$ and $|x|$ by $2^{\deg f}$, so $n=\deg f$; the parity bit is $f_t(0)$; polynomials play the role of integers ($d=1$, $b=0$). The inverse slopes of the two branches are $x$ (even) and $x/(1+x)^2$ (odd), against $2$ and $2/3$ for $C$, so the cumulative inverse slope $m(t)=2^t3^{-e(t)}$ of [173, sec 4] becomes $m(t)=x^t(1+x)^{-2e(t)}$, where $e(t)$ counts odd steps. Constant units $2^u3^v$ become $x^c(1+x)^d$, and $\mathbb Q$ becomes $\mathbb F_2(x)$.

**Corollary 3.1.** In this dictionary, the finite theorem fails with $A=4$, for every $K$, at arbitrarily large $|x|$, and the corollary fails.

*Proof.* Take $x=f$ at the start of block $m\ge1$ of the orbit of Proposition 2.1. Then $n=3\cdot2^m$. Block $m$ begins with its run, which raises the degree from $n$ to $2n+1$. Its stretch stays at or above $2n$ and ends at $2n=3\cdot2^{m+1}$, the start of block $m+1$. Repeating this, $\deg f_t\ge n$ for all $t\ge0$, and the size hypothesis holds for every $\eta\le1$. By (2.1), $p_{w_x(T)}(n)\le4n$ for every $T$. The orbit is aperiodic (its degree tends to infinity) and $P_u(L)\le7L+1$ by Proposition 2.2. $\square$

So some step of the proof of [173] fails in characteristic 2. Rauzy graphs and the Fine-Wilf theorem do not depend on the characteristic. The step we identify is the nonvanishing claim of [173, sec 5, para 4] (we count the opening paragraph of sec 5, with its display and the sentence after it, as para 1; para 3 begins "Take the multiplicative group", and para 4 begins "The algebra $\mathcal R$ consists of"): for a finite sum $\sum_hc_hh$ of distinct classes of the slope group $\Gamma$ with fixed nonzero coefficients, vanishing at infinitely many scales is excluded. The proof there reduces a minimal vanishing subsum to a nondegenerate unit equation and applies [ESS] (finiteness over $\mathbb Q$). In characteristic 2 such finiteness fails already for $u+v=1$: $x^{2^j}+(1+x)^{2^j}=1$ for every $j$. Derksen and Masser describe the solutions of unit equations in characteristic $p$ through Frobenius families ([DM]; we cite the abstract only and have not read the proofs).

**Proposition 3.2.** On the orbit of Proposition 2.1 there is an admissible choice in [173, secs 3-4] for which the retained slopes generate $X=x^{6\cdot2^n}$ and $Y=(1+x)^{6\cdot2^n}$ at every scale, and $\sum_{a+b\le3}X^aY^b=0$. So the claim of [173, sec 5, para 4] is false on these data.

*Proof.* At scale $i$ take $x$ at the start of block $n=n(i)\ge4$, with $n(i)\to\infty$, so $n_i=3\cdot2^n$ and $|x|\to\infty$. Positions are counted from there, and $j\ge0$ indexes the blocks $n+j$; the index $i$ is kept for scales ($n_i,K_i,T_i$). The run of block $n+j$ is $[S_j,E_j)$ with $S_j=5\cdot2^n(2^j-1)$ and $E_j=2^n(8\cdot2^j-5)+1$, followed by $(01)^{2^{n+j}-1}0$ up to $S_{j+1}$. [173, sec 3] chooses an order $k\in[n_i/2,n_i)$ with $R(k)=O_A(1)$ and $p_w(k)=O_A(k)$, and any $K_i\to\infty$; one admissible choice suffices to show that the proof breaks.
- *Order.* Take $k\in[1.5\cdot2^n,2^{n+1}-2]$. Every alternating stretch from block $n$ on has length at least $2^{n+1}-1\ge k+1$. The right-special $k$-factors are $1^k$ (followed by 1 inside runs and by 0 at $E_j$) and the alternating factor $Z$ ending in $01$ (followed by 0 inside stretches and by 1 at the start of the next run). A factor $1^a$ followed by a prefix of a stretch, or a suffix of a stretch followed by $1^c$, is alternating if $a=1$ or $c=1$. If $a\ge2$ or $c\ge2$ it contains $11$ and a $0$, so it fixes the offset of the one run boundary it meets, and it has one continuation (runs and stretches are longer than $k$). So $R(k)=2$, and $p_w(k)\le5\cdot2^n+2k+1\le5.4k+1$ by the count of Proposition 2.2.
- *Final factor.* Take $K$ with $3K\in[5\cdot2^{j'}-3,\,8\cdot2^{j'}-5]$ for some $j'\ge1$ (an interval of length at least 4, so it contains a multiple of 3). Then $T_i-k\ge S_{j'}$ and $T_i\le E_{j'}$, so the final $k$-factor $[T_i-k,T_i)$ lies in the run of block $n+j'$, it is $1^k$, and $B=2$. Taking $j'=j'(i)\to\infty$ gives $K_i\to\infty$, as [173] requires. The short arcs (self-loops at $1^k$ and $Z$) have length 1 or 2, so $\ell\le2$, and $k\ge(3B+1)\ell=14$ holds once $n\ge4$.
- *Long-arc starts.* The long arcs are the transitions $1^k\to Z$ and $Z\to1^k$. In order, $t_{2j}=E_j-k$ (the last $1^k$ of a run) and $t_{2j+1}=S_{j+1}+1-k$ (the last $Z$, which ends at the first letter of the next run).
- *Slopes.* With $v(M)$ the exponent vector of $M$ in $(x,1+x)$, and $e(E_j)=2^n(7\cdot2^j-4)+1$, $e(S_j)=4\cdot2^n(2^j-1)$:
$$v(M_{2j})=2^n(8\cdot2^j-5,\,-14\cdot2^j+8)+(1-k)(1,-2),$$
$$v(M_{2j+1})=2^n(10\cdot2^j-5,\,-16\cdot2^j+8)+(1,-2)-k(1,-1)+(0,\,k\bmod2).$$
The $M_j$ depend on $k$, but the same-type ratios do not:
$$M_2/M_0=x^{8\cdot2^n}(1+x)^{-14\cdot2^n},\qquad M_3/M_1=x^{10\cdot2^n}(1+x)^{-16\cdot2^n}.$$
Since $(6,0)=-8(8,-14)+7(10,-16)$ and $(0,6)=-5(8,-14)+4(10,-16)$, we get $X=(M_2/M_0)^{-8}(M_3/M_1)^7$ and $Y=(M_2/M_0)^{-5}(M_3/M_1)^4$ exactly.
- *The sum.* $1+x^6+(1+x)^6=x^2+x^4=x^2(1+x)^2$. Raising to the power $2^n$ gives $1+X+Y=(x(1+x))^{2^{n+1}}$, so $(1+X+Y)^3=XY$. In characteristic 2, $(1+X+Y)^3=(1+X^2+Y^2)(1+X+Y)$, and adding $XY$ gives $\sum_{a+b\le3}X^aY^b=0$: ten monomials, each with coefficient 1.
- *Distinct classes.* The ratio of $X^aY^b$ and $X^{a'}Y^{b'}$ has exponent vector $6\cdot2^n(a-a',b-b')$, which is not eventually constant unless $(a,b)=(a',b')$. So the ten monomials are distinct classes of $\Gamma$. By [173, sec 5, para 3] the chosen representatives differ from these monomials by constant units $x^c(1+x)^d$, so $\sum_hc_hh=0$ with fixed nonzero coefficients $c_h$ at every scale. This is the event that [173, sec 5, para 4] excludes. $\square$

Both (10) of [173] and its sec 8 argument use the nonvanishing claim, so the proof breaks at the claim at the latest. We have not checked whether an earlier step (the anchor formula of sec 3 or the estimates of sec 4, see below) already fails in characteristic 2, and we do not know whether the sec 8 Subspace step would also fail.

*What is not checked.* The char-2 form of the anchor formula $A(W)$ of [173, sec 3], and of the size estimates (4), (6), (8) of [173, sec 4], is not checked on these data. Proposition 3.2 is about the retained slope classes only, which are computed directly above; it does not need those estimates. Orders $k$ outside $[1.5\cdot2^n,2^{n+1}-2]$ are not treated.

**Over $\mathbb Z$.** No positive integer has parity word $u$ under the shortcut map $C(x)=x/2$ or $(3x+1)/2$. Block $m$ of $u$ has $4\cdot2^m$ ones and $2^m$ zeros, so the first $5(2^n-1)$ letters contain $4(2^n-1)$ ones. Put $y=x+1$. An odd step sends $y$ to $3y/2$ and an even step sends it to $(y+1)/2\le y$, so an integer start $x_0<2^b$ reaches $x_t$ with $\log_2(x_t+1)\le b+4(2^n-1)\log_2(3/2)<b+2.34\cdot2^n$. The run $1^{3\cdot2^n+1}$ that follows needs $x_t\equiv-1\pmod{2^{3\cdot2^n+1}}$, so $\log_2(x_t+1)\ge3\cdot2^n+1$. This fails once $0.66\cdot2^n>b$. (Counting the even steps as well gives growth of about $1.34\cdot2^n$ bits, the figure in forum comment kr9EBzWIRh3k9VrG; the cruder bound suffices.)

## 4. A divergent orbit of the table b 5 5 3

In the notation of [80], take $m=2$ and the table $A_0=1+x+x^3$, $A_1=A_2=(1+x)^2$, $A_3=1+x$ ("b 5 5 3"). Its drift is $\tfrac14\bigl((3-1)+(2-1)+(2-4)+(1-4)\bigr)=-\tfrac12$ by [80, sec 2] (classes 0, 1 determined with $v=1$; classes 2, 3 undetermined), and it is one of the Haar-preserving tables with escapes that the survey behind [80, Question 3] traced (supplement of [80], survey_blocks.txt l.45). [Drift by hand: mod $x^3$, $A_0+1\equiv x$, $A_1(1+x)+1\equiv x+x^2$, $A_2(1+x^2)+1=x^4$, $A_3(1+x+x^2)+1=x^3$.] For odd $f$ the class is $c=f_1+2f_2$, and $T(f)=(A_cf+1)/x^v$ with $v$ maximal. Put $R=1/(1+x+x^2)\in\mathbb F_2[[x]]$. The identities $(1+x)^2R+1=xR$ and $(1+x)R+1=x^2R$ are used throughout.

**Lemma 4.1 (a 9-cycle).** $p_0=1+x+x^3+x^4+x^6$ lies on a 9-cycle of $T$ with class word $1^5\,3\,1^2\,3$ and valuations $(1,1,1,1,1,6,1,1,3)$. Two of its points are $p_6=(1+x+x^2)^3=R(1+x^4(1+x)^4)$ and $p_8=1+x+x^2+x^4+x^5+x^7+x^8=R(1+x^2(1+x^8))$.

*Proof.* The nine steps are written out in the supplement note (byhand_1700, sec 1), with $A_cf+1$ at each step; $p_8(1+x+x^2)=1+x^2+x^{10}$ and $(1+x+x^2)^4=1+x^4+x^8$ give the two $R$-forms. $\square$

*Cycle coordinates.* If $f=p_i+D$ with $\operatorname{val}D\ge3$, and $\operatorname{val}D\ge v_i+1$ at a class-3 step, then $T(f)=p_{i+1}+A_{c_i}D/x^{v_i}$, since $A_{c_i}p_i+1$ has valuation exactly $v_i$. Over one period $D\mapsto(1+x)^{16}D/x^{16}$. A full period starting at valuation $V$ is valid iff $V\ge17$ (the $v=6$ step sees $V-5\ge7$, the $v=3$ step sees $V-13\ge4$), and the part $1^5\,3$ alone needs $V\ge12$.

**Theorem 4.2.** For $m\ge0$ put $N_m=24\cdot2^m+4$ and $F_m=R\,(1+x^{N_m}(1+x)^4)$. Then $F_m$ is a polynomial of degree $24\cdot2^m+6$, and
$$T^{33\cdot2^m}(F_m)=F_{m+1},$$
with class word $B_m=1^{24\cdot2^m+2}\,3\,(1^5\,3\,1^2\,3)^{2^m-1}\,1^5\,3$. So the orbit of $F_0=(1+x^{28}(1+x)^4)/(1+x+x^2)$ diverges.

*Proof.* Put $q=2^m$, $M=16q$, $y=x^M$, $N=N_m$.
(i) At a root $w$ of $1+x+x^2$, $1+w=w^2$, so $w^N(1+w)^4=w^{N+8}=1$ because $3\mid N+8$. Hence $1+x+x^2$ divides $1+x^N(1+x)^4$.
(ii) Write $f=R(1+E)$. If $x^3\mid E$ the step is class 1 with $v=1$, and $E\mapsto(1+x)^2E/x$. So $F_m$ makes exactly $N-2$ class-1 steps and reaches $R(1+x^2(1+x)^{2N})$. Since $2N=8+48q$, Frobenius gives $(1+x)^{2N}=(1+x^8)(1+y)^3$.
(iii) So that point is $p_8+D_8$ with $D_8=Rx^2(1+x^8)(y+y^2+y^3)$, of valuation $M+2\ge4$. The $p_8$ step ($v=3$) is valid, and at $p_0$ the deviation has valuation $M-1=16q-1$.
(iv) Exactly $q-1$ full periods run (start valuations $16(q-j)-1$, $j=0,\dots,q-2$, all $\ge17$), then $1^5\,3$ runs at valuation 15, ending at $p_6$.
(v) From $p_8$ to that $p_6$ the multiplier is $(1+x)x^{-3}\cdot((1+x)^{16}x^{-16})^{q-1}\cdot(1+x)^{11}x^{-11}=(1+x)^{M-4}x^{-(M-2)}$. Using $(1+x)^8(1+x)^{M-4}=(1+x)^4(1+y)$, the deviation is $D_6=Rx^4(1+x)^4(1+y)(1+y+y^2)=Rx^4(1+x)^4(1+y^3)$. So $p_6+D_6=R(1+x^{3M+4}(1+x)^4)=F_{m+1}$, as $3M+4=N_{m+1}$.
The step count is $(N-2)+1+9(q-1)+6=33q$. Class-1 steps add 1 to the degree and a class-3 step adds $1-v$; a period and the final $1^5\,3$ have net 0, and the $p_8$ step has $-2$. So the degree rises by $N-2-2=24q$. $\square$

Validity in (iv) is used only as a sufficient condition; what the cycle does below valuation 17 is never needed.

**The orbit of 0x1db.** A pre-registered census of the Haar-preserving tables of [80] found that the starts 0xdb and 0x191 under b 5 5 3 stay open for $2^{17}$ steps; both reach 0x1db (computed; supplement c2, c3).

**Corollary 4.3.** The orbit of $\mathtt{0x1db}=1+x+x^3+x^4+x^6+x^7+x^8$ reaches $F_1$ at step 148 and diverges. It reaches $F_m$ at step $33\cdot2^m+82$ ($m\ge1$).

*Proof, given the computed steps 0-147.* Any odd $f$ is $R(1+E)$ with $E=f(1+x+x^2)+1$, and a class-1 run of length exactly $L$ starts at $\operatorname{val}E=L+2$, with $\deg f=\deg E-2$. The computed run data say that step 148 starts a class-1 run of length exactly 50 at degree 54. So $f_{148}=R(1+x^{52}U)$ with $U(0)=1$, $\deg U=4$. Divisibility by $1+x+x^2$ at a root $w$ (where $w^{52}=w$) forces $U(w)=w^2$, which leaves $U=(1+x)^4$ or $U=1+x+x^2+x^3+x^4$. Step 198 is class 3, with $v=2+\operatorname{val}(1+(1+x)^{101}U)$. For $U=(1+x)^4$, $(1+x)^{105}=(1+x)(1+x^8)(1+x^{32})(1+x^{64})$, so $v=3$ and $\deg f_{199}=102$. For the other $U$, $(1+x)^{101}U=(1+x)^{100}(1+x^5)=1+x^4+\cdots$, so $v=6$ and $\deg f_{199}=99$. The computed degree is 102, so $f_{148}=F_1$. The rest follows from Theorem 4.2. $\square$

Steps 0-147 are computed only. The prefix before step 148 is not a block $B_m$: its last letter 0 or 2 is at step 119, and the class-1 run at step 123 has length 18, which is not of the form $24\cdot2^m+2$. The predicted block starts and record run lengths $24\cdot2^m+2$ match the pre-registered $2^{17}$-step run for $m=1,\dots,11$ [supplement c3]. At block starts the degree per step tends to $24/33=8/11$.

**Comparison with Theorem B of [80].** Theorem B ($T_{3759}$, drift $-1/2$) has run multiplier $1+x^3$, run lengths $58+96n$ in an arithmetic progression, one fixed return word of 10 steps, and a block map that is affine over $\mathbb F_2[z]$, $z=x^{96}$; the mechanism is $(1+x^3)^{2^t}=1+x^{3\cdot2^t}$. Theorem 4.2 has run multiplier $(1+x)^2$, geometric run lengths $24\cdot2^m+2$, and a return word $(1^5\,3\,1^2\,3)^{2^m-1}\,1^5\,3$ that winds $2^m-1$ times round a polynomial 9-cycle; the block step closes by $(1+x)^{2N}=(1+x^8)(1+y)^3$. So b 5 5 3 is a second table with a block construction, of a different type. This answers [80, Question 3] ("for which tables does a block construction exist?") for one more table. It does not decide whether existence can be read off the table.

The 6,000-step survey behind [80, Question 3] traced a different b 5 5 3 orbit, from 0x6249 (Sec. 6). The survey read blocks from letters after step 3000 of that orbit, and its runs there are class 0. On the 0x1db orbit no letter 0 or 2 occurs after step 119, and the blocks of Theorem 4.2 are class-1 runs. So the survey's blocks are not those of Theorem 4.2. In a pre-registered run of $2^{18}$ steps (supplement c4), no point of the 0x6249 orbit equals any $F_m$.

## 5. The orbit of Theorem 4.2 against [173]

**Dictionary.** The table b 5 5 3 divides by $x^v$ with $v$ not fixed by the class (class 3 has $v=3$ or $6$ on this orbit). To get branches with fixed slopes, as in [173, sec 2], we split each step into an odd part $f\mapsto(A_cf+1)/x$ and $v-1$ even parts $f\mapsto f/x$. The refined letters 1, 3, 0 have inverse slopes $x/(1+x)^2$, $x/(1+x)$ and $x$, and $m(t)=x^t(1+x)^{-a(t)}$ with $a=2\#1+\#3$. Refined time is total $x$-valuation. Letter 0 is the even part, not class 0 (classes 0 and 2 do not occur after step 119). [173, sec 3] is written for binary words, but its count works for any finite alphabet, and every right-special factor used below has exactly two extensions. Other dictionaries, such as the unrefined class word, are not treated.

**The refined block word.** With $q=2^m$ and $r=24q+2$, Theorem 4.2 refines to
$$B_m=1^r\,3\,0\,0\,P^{q-1}\,1^5\,3\,0^5,\qquad P=1^5\,3\,0^5\,1\,1\,3\,0\,0 .$$
$|P|=16$ and $a(P)=16$. A block has length $40q$ and $a=64q$; the degree gain $a-{}$length$\,=24q$ agrees with Theorem 4.2. The block slope is $(x^5(1+x)^{-8})^{8q}$. Call $[T,T+r)$ the run of the block and the remaining $16q-2$ letters its rest; the rest is $P$-periodic, and the $P$-periodic stretch extends 2 letters into each neighbouring run, ending at $e=(\text{next block start})+2$.

**Proposition 5.1.** Take $x=F_n$, $Q=2^n$, $n\ge4$, so $n_i=24Q+6$ and $d=1$.
(a) $\deg f_t\ge\deg F_n$ for all $t$, so the size hypothesis holds for every $\eta\le1$, and $p_w(n_i)\le4n_i$. So the char-2 finite theorem of [173] also fails on this orbit with $A=4$, at every $K$.
(b) For $k\in[12Q+3,16Q-3]$ the marks are $1^k$ and the $P$-periodic factor $Z$ ending at a stretch end, so $R(k)=2$, and $p_w(k)\le2k+15$. With $K_i$ chosen so that $T_i-k$ and $T_i$ lie in the run of block $n+i+1$ (and $n=n(i)$ large), $B=2$ and $\ell=16$.
(c) The same-type ratios of retained slopes are $v(M_2/M_0)=16Q(4,-7)$ and $v(M_3/M_1)=16Q(5,-8)$. They generate $X=x^{48Q}=(M_2/M_0)^{-8}(M_3/M_1)^7$ and $Y=(1+x)^{48Q}=(M_2/M_0)^{-5}(M_3/M_1)^4$, and $(1+X+Y)^3=XY$.
So the claim of [173, sec 5, para 4] is false on these data too.

*Proof.* Positions are refined times counted from $F_n$. As in Proposition 3.2, $j\ge0$ indexes the blocks $n+j$ and $i$ is kept for scales. Block $n+j$ starts at $S_j=40Q(2^j-1)$, and its run has length $r_j=24\cdot2^jQ+2$.
(a) In unrefined steps a class-1 step adds 1 to the degree, a class-3 step with $v=6$ subtracts 5, and one with $v=3$ subtracts 2. So within a period the partial changes are $+5,0,+2,0$, and the step after a run is $-2$ after a gain of $r_j=24\cdot2^jQ+2$. Refined intermediate degrees lie between consecutive unrefined ones. So the degree never falls below $\deg F_n$. For $N=n_i$: windows starting in block $n$ have at most $40Q<\tfrac53N$ start positions. Later pieces have length at least $32Q-2\ge N+1$, so a later window meets at most two pieces and is $1^N$, one of at most 16 $P$-phases, or one of $N-1$ words run$\to$rest or $N-1$ words rest$\to$run. So $p_w(N)\le\tfrac{11}3N+15\le4N$, as $N\ge45$.
(b) Since $k\le16Q-3$, every piece from block $n$ on has length at least $k+1$, and a $k$-window meets at most two pieces. $1^k$ is followed by 1 inside runs and by 3 at a run end; the 1-runs inside $P$ have length at most $5<k$. $Z$ is followed by 1 at $e$ and by 3 inside stretches: it also occurs 16 letters earlier in the stretch of block $n+1$, which has length $32Q+2>k+16$. The other 15 $P$-phases continue periodically. A factor $1^a$ followed by a prefix of the rest ($1\le a<k$) continues inside that rest, which is the same word in every block up to length $16Q-2\ge k-a+1$. A suffix of the rest followed by $1^c$ continues with 1, as runs are longer than $k$. When $a\ge3$ or $c\ge3$, a boundary factor occurs only at one fixed offset from a run boundary, so its continuation is the same at every occurrence: it contains $1^6$, $1\,1\,1\,3\,0\,0\,1$ or $0^5\,1\,1\,1$, and none of these occurs inside a stretch (1-runs there have length at most 5, and in $P$ the word $1\,1\,1\,3\,0\,0$ is followed by 0 and $0^5$ by $1\,1\,3$). For $a,c\le2$ the factor lies in a stretch and is a $P$-phase. So $R(k)=2$ and $p_w(k)\le1+16+2(k-1)$.
For the final factor, $T_i=K(24Q+6)$ must satisfy $T_i-k\ge S_{j'}$ and $T_i\le S_{j'}+r_{j'}$. As $n\to\infty$ with $K$ fixed this asks for $24K$ in $(40\cdot2^{j'}-24,\ 64\cdot2^{j'}-40)$, an interval of length $24\cdot2^{j'}-16>24$ for every $j'\ge1$. (Divide both conditions by $Q$. The lower end uses the worst case $k/Q<16$; the term $6K/Q$ tends to 0, which the open upper end absorbs. For $j'=1$ the interval is $(56,88)$ and $K=3$ works.) Take $j'=i+1$, $K_i$ in that interval, and $n=n(i)$ large given $K_i$; then $K_i\to\infty$ as [173] requires, the final factor is $1^k$, and $B=2$. The marked visits are $1^k$ at every run position (self-loops of length 1) and $Z$ every 16 letters in a stretch (short arcs of length 16), so $\ell=16$, and $k\ge(3B+1)\ell=112$ holds since $n\ge4$.
(c) The long arcs are $1^k\to Z$, from the last $1^k$ of a run, and $Z\to1^k$, from the last $Z$ at $e-k$ to the next block start. $W_*$ is the run-$n$ self-loop part. So
$$t_{2j}=S_j+r_j-k=64\cdot2^jQ-40Q+2-k,\qquad t_{2j+1}=S_{j+1}+2-k=40(2^{j+1}-1)Q+2-k .$$
Block totals give $a(S_j)=64Q(2^j-1)$. The interval $[S_j,t_{2j})$ lies in the run, so $a(t_{2j})=a(S_j)+2(r_j-k)=112\cdot2^jQ-64Q+4-2k$. And $a(t_{2j+1})=a(S_{j+1}+2)-a(Z)=64Q(2^{j+1}-1)+4-a(Z)$, where $a(Z)$ is one number at each scale. With $v(M)=(t,-a(t))$: $t_2-t_0=64Q$, $a(t_2)-a(t_0)=112Q$, $t_3-t_1=80Q$, $a(t_3)-a(t_1)=128Q$. Then $(3,0)=-8(4,-7)+7(5,-8)$ and $(0,3)=-5(4,-7)+4(5,-8)$. Finally $1+x^3+(1+x)^3=x(1+x)$; raising to the power $2^{n+4}$ (note $48Q=3\cdot2^{n+4}$) gives $1+X+Y=(x(1+x))^{2^{n+4}}$, so $(1+X+Y)^3=XY$. The rest is as in Proposition 3.2. $\square$

**Same shape as for ML.** The ML ratios are $2\cdot2^n(4,-7)$ and $2\cdot2^n(5,-8)$, with the same integer coefficients $-8,7$ and $-5,4$. The reason: $M_2/M_0$ covers the rest of block $n$ and the run of block $n+1$, and $M_3/M_1$ covers block $n+1$. In both maps the run has direction $(1,-2)$, the rest has direction $(1,-1)$ up to $O(1)$, and the lengths rest$_n$ : run$_{n+1}$ : rest$_{n+1}$ are $1:3:2$ up to $O(1)$ (ML: $2^{n+1}-1$, $6\cdot2^n+1$, $2^{n+2}-1$; b 5 5 3: $16Q-2$, $48Q+2$, $32Q-2$). So $M_2/M_0=c_n\bigl((1,-1)+3(1,-2)\bigr)=c_n(4,-7)$ and $M_3/M_1=c_n\bigl(3(1,-2)+2(1,-1)\bigr)=c_n(5,-8)$; the $O(1)$ terms cancel.

So Theorem 4.2 gives a second witness for the failure of [173, sec 5, para 4] in characteristic 2, through the same Frobenius relation in a ratio lattice of the same shape. It gives no new failing input. As in Sec. 3, the char-2 forms of the anchor formula of [173, sec 3] and of the size estimates (4), (6), (8) of [173, sec 4] are not checked.

## 6. Open questions

1. *The 0x6249 orbit of b 5 5 3.* It stays open for $2^{18}$ steps, with degree 25,239 at step $2^{18}$ and about 0.11 degree per step over the second half, against $8/11$ for the family of Theorem 4.2 (computed, supplement c4). In those steps it meets no $F_m$, and its longest runs of letters 0 and 1 (lengths 397 and 168) start at steps 1049 and 818; no later run is longer. Runs of letters 2 and 3 set records later (lengths 28 at step 223,847 and 61 at step 152,387), with successive record ratios between 1.05 and 4.7 and no doubling pattern. So it shows no geometric block structure of the kind in Theorem 4.2 in $2^{18}$ steps. We do not know whether it diverges, whether it joins the family later, or whether it has block structure of another kind.
2. Is there a divergent orbit, in any table of [80], whose word is not of Frobenius block type? Theorem B of [80], Proposition 2.1 and Theorem 4.2 all rest on a Frobenius identity.
3. Does any step of an integer-side argument other than [173, sec 5, para 4] transfer to $\mathbb F_2[x]$ and fail there? This note checks only that step.
4. Is there an effective form of [173, sec 5, para 4] over $\mathbb Q$, with an explicit scale after which the nonvanishing holds? Sec. 7 explains why a growth rate for $P(n)/n$ along the route of [173] would need one.

## 7. Scope

Nothing here is a statement about the positive-integer Collatz problem. Sections 3 and 5 are statements about the proof of [173] under the dictionary of Sec. 3: they locate one step of that proof which is false in characteristic 2, on proved divergent orbits. They do not show that the proof is wrong over $\mathbb Q$, where the step rests on [ESS]. Over $\mathbb Z$ the ML word is excluded by size alone (Sec. 3). The table b 5 5 3 is not the characteristic-2 form of a standard integer map, so we make no integer statement about it.

*Effectivity.* The constants of [173] are qualitative [173, sec 1]. Its corollary [173, sec 9] gives $P(n) > An$ for all large $n$, for each fixed $A$. A rate, meaning $P(n) \ge g(n)\,n$ with an explicit $g \to \infty$, would follow along the same route from an explicit threshold $X_0(A)$ in the finite theorem. In a rough check by hand (supplement, a_growth_audit.md), the quantities of [173, secs 3-4] (the size of the graph, the number of arcs, and the time and size estimates) have explicit bounds in $A$. The first place where we found no explicit bound is [173, sec 5]. There the equality tests and the nonvanishing of para 4 hold from some scale on, and no scale is given. The unit-equation theorem [ESS] bounds the number of nondegenerate solutions but not their size. The lower bound (10) of [173, sec 5] and the argument of [173, sec 8] use the Subspace Theorem, and the solutions it controls cannot in general be determined effectively [E, sec 3]. So the step of [173] that fails in characteristic 2 is also, as far as we can see, the step where effectivity is lost over $\mathbb Q$. Our characteristic-2 data say nothing about whether an effective version exists. The refinement of the Subspace Theorem used in [15] and [190] bounds the number and the widths of the exceptional height intervals but not their locations. In [15, eq. (15)] this gives an effective bound because the target there is cumulative: disjoint progress intervals of a record are summed. A threshold $X_0(A)$ is a pointwise statement, and an exceptional interval of unknown location could lie above any proposed threshold. Along the route of [173], such a count could at most bound the logarithmic measure of the set of $n$ with $P(n)\le An$. We have not checked even this, since the forms of [173, sec 8] are defined only after passing to subsequences in [173, secs 5-7].
